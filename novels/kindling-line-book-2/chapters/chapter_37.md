Sol did not answer him on the bridge, and she did not answer him at supper, and by the second morning Kael had stopped watching her face for it every time she opened her mouth.

That was the change he noticed first. Not in her. In himself. He had asked a question that would cost him the house, and instead of chasing the answer the way he chased everything, he had let it sit where he'd put it and gone back to the ledgers. It felt like leaving a door unlatched on purpose. Every hour it stayed shut was a small, deliberate thing he had done right, and none of them added up to anything he could point to, which was, he was beginning to understand, the whole of what doing it right looked like from the inside.

He was cross-referencing Thorne's contractor chain against the withdrawal-form stack when Sol came up the spire stair with her coat still on and stood in the doorway rather than crossing it.

"I have an answer," she said. "I don't think it's the one you're braced for."

"I don't have a guess I'm holding onto." He set the pen down. "Say it."

"No." She came in and shut the door behind her, which she did not usually bother with. "Not yet. I want the co-lead. I want it recorded, I want it before the Council, I want my name on the same line as yours where Malrik's steward or the Chancellor's clerk or any petitioner with a grievance has to go through both of us to get anything signed. That part isn't in question, and I want you to hear it plainly before I say the rest, because the rest is going to sound like a refusal and it isn't one."

"Say the rest."

"Not this week." Sol pulled the second chair round to face his desk rather than sit beside him at it. "If you petition now, three days after Thorne named you in open chamber, it reads as retreat. Ashworth's Auditor, already weakened, hands half his authority to the woman he's courting, and every house still weighing its own vote will read it as a man propping himself up with a marriage he hasn't even made yet. It will cost you the standing you're trying to protect by doing it, and it will cost me a different, harder thing. It will mean I got the title because you needed me to have it, not because the work earned it."

"The work has earned it three times over."

"I know that. You know that. Malrik's steward does not know that, and neither does the elder who's going to have to vote on it." She folded her hands in her lap, and Kael recognized the stillness in them, the one she used when she had already decided and was only waiting to see if he'd argue. "I want it to come after a proof. After we've stood in front of the Council on the commission's report and been right about what we told them. After the reading on the twenty-eighth, win or lose. A thing the whole Reach watched us do together, so that when the petition goes in, nobody has to take it on your word that I've earned it. They'll have already seen it."

Kael sat with that longer than he wanted to. His instinct, sharp and immediate, was to say the timing didn't matter, that a thing correctly done owed nothing to how it looked, and he heard the instinct for what it was before it reached his mouth: the same wanting to move first that had put a boy up a ropewalk stair without her, dressed now in the clothes of principle instead of protection.

"You're not saying no," he said slowly. "You're saying I don't get to decide when it's safe for you to be seen taking a title you've earned."

"Yes."

"That's harder to hear than a no."

"It's supposed to be." The line at her mouth eased, not into a smile, but close to one. "You asked me to sit with it and tell you what I actually thought, not what I believed I owed you for asking. This is what I actually think. I want it. I want it timed by me."

"Then it's timed by you." He said it without the beat of hesitation he'd have needed a month ago to get there, and felt the saying of it land where it was meant to. "I'll draft nothing until you tell me to draft it."

"You'll want to. Before the twenty-eighth. I know you."

"I'll want to," he agreed. "That's not the same as doing it."

She studied him a moment longer, as if checking the sentence for a seam she expected to find and didn't. "You did it," she said. "The letting-alone. You actually did it, since the bridge."

"I noticed myself wanting to ask you every morning. I counted the mornings instead of asking."

"How many?"

"Nine."

"That's not nothing." She stood, and for a moment neither of them moved toward the door, the desk between them still covered in Thorne's contractor chain and the withdrawal stack, the ordinary work of the morning waiting under the larger thing they'd just finished saying. "I'm going to want you to ask me things too, from here. Not defer to me out of penance. There's a difference between not deciding for me and refusing to have an opinion at all."

"Noted." He almost smiled. "What's your opinion on the withdrawal stack?"

"That it's two hundred short of meaning anything, and that you already knew that, and asked anyway to see if I'd let you off the hook for asking." She picked up her coat from the chair-back where she'd slung it. "I won't, every time. Just most of them."

Ansa's boy found them at the second bell with a slate that made Sol read it twice.

"Two hundred and eighty-eight," she said, "and then a line under it. *Withdrawal paper signed: one.*" She looked up. "One of the held men broke it."

"Who?"

"The boy doesn't know. Ansa's hand, not the clerk's, so she got it secondhand at the awning and copied it fast." Sol was already reaching for her coat. "Two hundred and eighty-eight held for eleven days, and now one of them has broken it in the open, where every other held man can see it done and survived."

They went down together, which had stopped being a thing either of them announced and started being simply the shape of how they left a room, and found the awning crowded thin with morning trade, the brazier cold, one clerk at the table with a face like a man guarding an empty till.

"Who withdrew," Kael asked him.

"The office doesn't say names before the book's closed for the day, Auditor."

"The office will say this one, or I'll ask the Chancellor's clerk who's been recording your hours since the ninth to ask it for you."

The clerk's jaw worked. "Tam," he said finally. "Ansa's boy's uncle. Came at first light, said his piece, signed, and walked out before I could send for the house. Said to tell you." The clerk stopped himself, then finished it anyway, as if the words had already left his hands once and there was no taking them back a second time. "Said to tell the Auditor: the bread's managed. Tell him thank you for the no, and tell him this one's mine to give, not his to keep."

Sol let out a breath that had a laugh caught in the middle of it, thin and worn through with two weeks of not laughing. "He's telling you he doesn't need saving from this either."

"I heard it." Kael looked at the cold brazier, the one clerk, the empty stretch of morning where two hundred and eighty-seven men had not yet followed and might, now, begin to. "Two hundred and eighty-eight, minus one, and the first crack anyone's put in it on purpose."

"It won't hold at one."

"No," Kael said. "But it didn't hold at zero either, and yesterday I'd have called that the whole of the difference."

Ansa herself caught up with them before they'd cleared the awning, her sleeves still rolled from the splicing bench, tar under her nails. "You'll want to know he came to me first," she said to Sol. "Before the table. Sat where you're standing and told me what he meant to do, and I told him it was his to give, not mine to keep, and then he went and did it anyway with my own words in his mouth."

"Are you angry with him?"

"I'm angry that it took eleven days and a warden hurt for one man to trust the ground under his own feet again." Ansa looked between them, sharper than her voice had let on. "You two gave him the ground. Don't let anyone tell you different, including yourselves." She turned back toward the tar-shop, then stopped. "The boy's proud of his uncle. Let him say so at you, if he tries. Don't wave it off the way you wave off everything else that isn't work."

She went. Sol watched her go a moment, then looked at Kael. "She's not wrong."

"About which part?"

"Any of it."

Bram found them on the Reach path before either of them had gone much further, a strip of Council wax in his fist and his coat still buttoned wrong from a hurried morning. "The senior clerk sends this," he said, out of breath. "Commission of surveyors moved its sitting up. Tomorrow, not the twelfth. Dessa's asking for both of you, and for the second peg, in the room, not sent ahead."

"Why moved?" Sol asked.

"She didn't say why to me, my lady. Only when. She did say to bring the withdrawal stack, if the Auditor still has it counted."

"I have it counted," Kael said. "Tell her two hundred and eighty-seven held, one freed, by his own hand and no one else's." Bram held the strip out, and Kael took it, and read it once before he passed it to her, as he always did now, and neither of them commented on the fact that he still did.

They walked back up the Reach without deciding to, the way they did most mornings now, and neither of them said anything else about the petition, or the twenty-eighth, or what it would cost either of them to wait for a thing they had both already agreed was owed. The water under the boiling-house cellar, unseen from the street, kept falling on its own schedule, a hand's width lower than it had been the week before, toward whatever it had been holding back.
