The clerks had set a table on the floor of the chamber, a plain deal table with a green baize cloth and a single inkwell, and by midmorning the whole Reach knew what it was for.

Sol had taken the end of the public rail again, the place where the iron turned a corner and the tier narrowed to a shelf. From here she could see the horseshoe of benches, the Chancellor's dais, and, three tiers down and to the left, the bench that had been her house's. Nobody sat on it. A porter had swept it that morning, and the broom marks showed in the dust of the seat, neat as a reproach. She looked at it for as long as she could bear and then looked at the table.

Kael sat at the auditor's chair at the foot of the dais, alone, in his father's coat cut down to his shoulders. He had told her the night before what he would do if Thorne asked him for a word in open session. She had written it on the back of her hand in ink so she would not have to trust her memory. *Say nothing has been found. Say it plainly. Say I said it.* He had not asked her to copy it. He had merely said it aloud to her, before, at the kettle, and let her decide what to do with it.

The senior clerk read the order of the day in a voice young for his office and steady all the same. Two matters of grain tariff. A dispute over a bridge toll. Then he lowered the sheet and looked toward the door.

"The Council will now receive a petition under the third part of the Schedule of Dissolution."

The door opened, and House Thorne came in as it had come in four days ago, a white-haired man on a young man's arm. This time the old man carried a leather case under the other elbow, flat and dark and no bigger than a large book.

He did not speak until he had laid it on the green cloth and opened it. The chamber leaned in. Sol watched the way the front benches shifted their weight, a hundred small movements, until the room seemed to lean as one animal.

"House Thorne files a proposal to administer the reform of the ward trade under the third part of the Schedule," Malrik said. His voice carried without effort. It was flat and clean, the voice of a man reading a bill of sale, and it was worse than the speech had been. "The proposal is in four heads. The first is the continuity of protection for those districts whose wards the trade has left unsupported. The second is a standard of contract to replace the old form. The third is a record of the houses' service, as the trade kept it. The fourth is the authority by which the house makes its offer."

"The authority," said the Chancellor. Her thin voice fell across the chamber like a ruler. "Read it, Lord Thorne."

"By precedent, Chancellor." Malrik lifted a single sheet. "Three succession instruments from the years of the ward trade, each passing the maintenance of a ward line from a failing house to the house that bore its cost longest. The house cites them by date and seal. The Council's own registers will confirm them. And by a fourth instrument, held by the house under seal, which the house will produce at the hearing."

Sol felt Kael go still at the auditor's chair, the small fixed stillness of a man who has heard a word he has been waiting for.

"A fourth instrument," the Chancellor said. "Named?"

"The Ward Deed of Succession."

It fell into the room with none of the weight of the other words. That was what made Sol's neck prickle. There was no drama in it, no pause for effect. Malrik Thorne named it the way a man names a cellar he has always owned.

"The Council has no record of such an instrument," said the Chancellor.

"The Council has no record of many things the trade kept for itself." Malrik closed the leather case. "The house does not ask the Council to take its word. It asks for a hearing at which the instrument will be produced, opened before the clerks, and read. Eight days is sufficient. The house's allies will sign in the interval."

"The hearing is set for the twenty-first," said the Chancellor, after a silence that Sol counted at four breaths. "The petition will be posted at the Council door on the morning of the fifteenth. Any house may sign or decline. The auditor will examine the records the petition cites and report to the Council before the hearing whether they bear out its claim."

She turned her head. "Auditor Ashworth."

Sol read the words on her hand. Kael did not look up at the rail. He looked at the Chancellor, which was correct, and at the table, which was also correct, and his voice when it came was quiet and dry.

"The auditor will examine what the petition cites. He has found nothing yet. He will say so when he has a finding, and not before."

"That is a very short report," said Malrik, gently.

"It is the truthful one."

"I did not say it was not." The old man bowed a finger's width. "The house awaits it, Auditor. With interest."

Whatever else he might have added was lost in the noise that rose from the benches. It was a low noise, like water finding its level, and under it Sol heard the first clerk's pen scratch. She watched the young man, Aldous, take the pen from its stand and hold it out to his grandfather. The old man signed. He wrote his name, and the house's name, and beneath them, with the pen's very tip, a small mark that she could not see from the rail but felt in her spine as a knot, three strands crossing.

The pen went to the Estler bench. A stout woman rose and signed without reading. The pen went to Farrow's bench, where a tall man in a brown coat took it, weighed the sheet for the length of a slow breath, and signed. Two. Three, with Thorne. The clerk wrote the count in the margin in red.

Then the pen went across the floor toward the Ashworth benches, held on the flat of the clerk's palm like a small offering.

Sol had never studied the Ashworth benches. She had spent her life across the room from them. They sat in the second tier on the right hand, six elders in grey with a steward standing at their shoulder, a tall woman with iron hair and a ring of keys at her belt. Ferra. Sol had met her once. She had not liked being looked at by her, and had never known why she disliked it, until the clerk reached the bench and Ferra looked at the pen and then at the auditor's chair and then, very slowly, at the ceiling.

Ferra shook her head. Once.

The clerk waited. She did not move again. The pen hung in the air above the palm, a thin black line, and for the space of three breaths the entire chamber watched a woman decline to sign a sheet of paper, and understood that it was a statement and could not agree on what it said.

The clerk withdrew. He carried the pen across the floor to the Vane bench, the empty one, and stood before it with his palm out for the length of a held breath, because the law required him to.

Sol did not know she was gripping the rail until her hand hurt. Nobody in the chamber was looking at her. Everybody was looking at the bench, and the clerk, and the pen, and the dust, and the broom marks that showed in it. The clerk lowered his hand.

"House Vane declines by absence," he said, in the flat voice of the record. The scratch of his pen sounded very loud.

The Chancellor adjourned the Council a few minutes later. Sol did not hear it. She stood at the rail while the crowd emptied around her, and it took her a moment to notice that a woman had come up the tier stair and was standing at her elbow. It was not Kael. It was Ferra.

"Lady Vane." The steward's voice was low and dry, a woman used to giving a great deal of information in very few words. "The house asks me to say that it will not be signing. Not on the fifteenth, and not at the hearing."

"Not on the fifteenth and not at the hearing," Sol repeated. "Which means?"

"That the elders have talked among themselves for two nights and could agree on one thing, and it was to say so." Ferra looked at the empty Vane bench below them and did not soften her face. "I was told to tell you and no one else. You are the auditor's second reader, and the house would prefer that the auditor not hear it from the house."

"Why not?"

"Because he would decide what it meant." Ferra almost smiled. "The elders know their boy. I am told the phrase was theirs."

Sol looked down at the back of her hand, where the ink had smudged into a blue cloud. Below her Kael was rising from the auditor's chair, alone at the foot of a dais with the Chancellor's clerks gathering their papers around him. He did not look up. She had asked him to say it plainly, and he had, and it was the truest thing he had said in that room since the reading.

"I'll tell him," she said. "Tonight. Before I decide what I think it means."

Ferra inclined her head. "That is all the house asked."

She went down the stair with her keys chiming. Sol followed a minute later, and found Kael waiting at the foot of it, out of the stream of people, with his back against the stone and his coat still buttoned wrong at the collar.

She told him. She told him all of it, in the order it had happened, the pen and Ferra's face and the message, and she watched him take it in without moving. When she reached the elders' phrase, *he would decide what it meant*, his mouth twitched.

"They said that."

"Ferra said they did."

"They're right." He looked at the floor. "I would have. I'd have stood in the corridor and worked out what a refusal from Ashworth meant to Thorne, to the Council, to you, and by the time I reached the end of it I'd have a plan and no memory that it had started as a message." He drew a breath. "What do you think it means?"

"That they decided a thing about themselves, for once, and didn't decide it about you." She said it carefully. "It's an odd sort of gift. I don't know if it's a kind one."

"Neither do I." But he was almost smiling, and it was not the auditor's smile. "I'd rather have been asked."

"Ask them. They'll say they did."

That won the laugh. It was brief and tired and real, and she kept it.

They stood a moment longer looking back up the stair at the chamber door. The clerk was still there at the green table, alone now, standing guard over the leather case Malrik Thorne had left closed on the baize.

*A fourth instrument. Held under seal. Produced at the hearing.*

"Eight days," Sol said. "A house that can produce a document in eight days could have produced it in one. They chose the eight."

"I know." Kael's voice had gone very quiet. "So a hand is going to move before the twenty-first. I'd like to know whose, and where, while there is still time to watch it."

She thought of the closed case, and the eight days, and a man who names a thing before he shows it and has already decided who will be standing there to see it. She thought of the dotted line running east off the edge of a sheet of ward-paper.

"So would I," she said.

"Together?"

"Ask me again."

He asked again, and this time she answered.
