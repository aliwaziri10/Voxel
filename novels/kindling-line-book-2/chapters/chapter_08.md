The courier came up the auditor's stair at second bell with mud to the knee, and Kael, who had been reading the same line of a dissolution schedule for a quarter of an hour, was glad of her until he saw the violet piping on her coat.

She was young, nineteen at the outside, with the flat pale look of a rider who had not slept in a proper bed since the petition went up. She did not take the chair he offered. She drew a sleeve of waxed vellum from her satchel, laid it on the blotter, and stepped back to the distance a person keeps from a dog that might not be friendly.

"Auditor Ashworth. From Lord Thorne's steward, to be placed in the Auditor's own hand." She said it like a lesson learned on the road. "The house has the honor to send a site pass, in his capacity as lead of the audit. Anchor Seven, in the Lower Reach. The site is decommissioned."

"Decommissioned by whom?"

The girl blinked. "That is the word on the paper, Auditor."

"I only want to know who chose it."

"The steward, I think." She wet her lips. "I was told to say the word. I wasn't told what it meant."

It was the most honest thing anyone in violet had said to him in a week. He thanked her and meant it, and she went down faster than she had come up.

The desk had been his father's for thirty years and was Kael's for eight months, and he had still not moved the blotter. He studied the sleeve for a while before he touched it. The wax at the mouth was near-black and pressed deep, and even from his chair he could make out the knot in it, three strands over and under, drawn tight as a fist. He slid the sheet out.

It was short: four lines in a ruling pen's steady black. *Anchor Seven, Lower Reach, beneath the old ropewalk. Decommissioned. Admits the bearer, once, in company of a warden of the house, upon a day named to the house steward on the evening before. Sealed this sixteenth of Sunspire.*

He read the third line twice. *The bearer.* One bearer. He thought of the visitors' book at the Lower Spine stair, and an entry in it ruled by a pen the Works Office did not own: *one bearer, name not given, pass of the house.*

His mind did what it had been trained to do with any document and reached for the record. The decommissioning register would say whether the word was Thorne's or the Accord's. The record desk was twenty minutes away, and Bram had been quick all week. He could go down, read the card, and carry the whole matter up to Sol as a finding instead of a question. Certainty first, then Sol. It was how he had balanced every ledger he had ever touched, and on paper the order had never failed him once.

He had his hand on the back of the chair before the rest of the thought caught up. He had been writing that order into his habits for years, and only this last fortnight had he seen what it looked like from the other side of a table. It looked like a man who wanted to arrive with the ground already swept.

He put the sheet back in its sleeve. He did not lock it in the drawer with the gouge, or turn it face down, or slip it into his coat. He carried it down three flights in his hand, where anyone on the stair could see what he was carrying.

---

Sol had taken the small room at the end of the research wing, the one with a north window and a table too wide for it, and she had the Accord's ward-line manuals open around her in a ring, like a woman holding a fort. She glanced up when the door opened. Her eyes went to his face first, and then to his hand.

"It came a quarter hour ago," Kael said, before he had reached the table. "A Thorne courier. A pass to Anchor Seven. I have not been to the register. I have decided nothing about it. I would like to read it beside you before I do."

He laid it down. She did not touch it. He understood that she was waiting for him to finish, so he finished.

"I had the whole walk to the record desk built by the time I stood up. Down, read the card, come back with an answer. I stopped at the chair. I thought you should see the paper before you were given my reading of it."

"That is two sentences more than the last time," Sol said. Her mouth moved at one corner, and she reached for the sheet.

She read it as she read a ward-line, lips shaping the words, following where it leaned. Then she took the charcoal drawing from her coat, the one on the vellum she kept for readings, and unfolded it beside the wax.

The lean was there. The third strand, the flame, pulled a hair tighter than the other two, and the whole knot tilted toward it. She held her fingertip an inch above the seal. "Same die. Same hand on the press, or a hand taught by it. That is the house's own mark and not a copy." She let out a breath. "I had hoped I would not be glad to be right."

"It admits the bearer," Kael said. "Once. With a warden at his elbow, on a day the steward is told the evening before."

"Only the bearer." She carried the sheet to the window as if daylight might change what it said. Below, the square lay open, and at the petition frame a boy in Council grey was chalking a sixth stroke on his slate. Kael counted it without meaning to. Corvane, then. Two more names would carry the petition, and the hearing was five days off. "It does not say the Auditor may bring the Lady Vane. It does not say anyone else may come at all."

"No."

"Yesterday a narrow man in violet asked me to hear his lord once, with no Council and no Auditor. Today a girl in violet brings you a door that opens for one and has a warden standing behind it." She laid the sheet flat. "They have cut a door for each of us, Kael, and made each one to fit a single person. They want us to walk through apart, and they want us to believe we chose to."

He did not answer at once. The plain answer was that he had been about to do the same to her unasked, and that Thorne had only noticed the habit and made a doorway of it. He said so.

"It would have worked on me. You know it would. I have gone through doors like that alone for years, and told you after."

"You have told me before since the reading," Sol said. "Mostly."

"Mostly."

She turned back to the paper and tapped the second line without touching it. "And this. Renn read us a closure on the gallery floor. The year, the crew that sealed it, the crew master's name, a lead disc on the hatch the size of my palm. An Accord closure has a name on it. Decommissioned is a word the house brought with it."

"Anchor Seven is the Accord's number," Kael said slowly. "On Renn's district sheet it has another. The ropewalk sites are numbered one to six." He watched her arrive a breath ahead of him. "Site six."

"The one he redrew. The one he would not describe until he had a second line to set beside the first." She sat back. "He said he did not know what he was looking at."

"Nor do we."

"No. But we know the house has a word for it, and has sent the word on a paper with a warden attached."

He ought to have stopped there. He heard himself go on. "I lean toward returning it unused. I would not walk into that site behind a Thorne warden if the Council itself ordered me."

"You lean." Sol held his gaze. "You built that on the way down."

"Yes."

"Then say it is a lean, and ask me. You have done the first half well twice this week. I would like the second."

He took it and found that it did not sting. "I lean toward returning it unused. What would you do with it?"

"I would not return it." She was already opening the notebook. "Returning it tells Thorne we read it and refused. Keeping it in a pocket tells no one anything and gives us nothing. I would put it in the Council's receiving ledger, with the hour, so that the house's own word for the site sits in the Council's own hand. And I would like to see what the register says before either of us decides what any of it means."

"At the record desk."

"Together."

"Together," Kael said. He had not meant to say it so fast, and he did not take it back.

She rose and gathered the manuals into a stack that would not fall. He stopped her at the door, because the alternative was to carry the question down with him and ask it later, and he had lately been counting how often later meant never.

"Thorne's invitation. Have you decided?"

"It stays open." She did not pretend the answer was casual. "If I go, you will have every word by supper. I told his man so, and I meant it."

"If you go, I would like to be within reach. The passage, the street, the foot of the stair. Not in the room. I am asking. I am not arranging."

She considered him, and the room was quiet enough that he heard a page settle in one of the manuals. "Ask me again the day I decide to go."

"I will."

---

The Council's record desk stood at the foot of the north stair in a pool of lantern light, and Bram was on his feet behind it before they reached the bottom step, as though he had heard them coming through the floor. He was the clerk who had carried the registers to Sol unasked and stood at the end of her table until she thanked him. He nodded to Kael, then, with small dignified effort, to Sol, and bowed to both.

"The decommissioning index card for Anchor Seven," Kael said. "Lower Reach, the ropewalk district."

"The long tray, Auditor. Filed by district." Bram drew it toward him across the desk and stopped with his fingers on the lid. "That is strange."

"What is?"

"The dust." He lifted the lid. A clean stripe ran across the film on the inner edge, the width of a hand, and the cards stood at uneven angles in their slots like a row of teeth. "Before the fourteenth, no one had drawn from this tray in eleven years. On the fourteenth I set the ropewalk cards back at the rear myself." He touched the front of the row. "This one is at the front. It does not belong at the front."

He drew it out, cream-edged, in a clerk's hand three generations old, and laid it under the lantern.

*Anchor Seven. Vault-linked. Ropewalk district, Lower Reach. Closure entry: none. Maintenance flag: open, entered in the last year of the trade, not lifted.*

Kael read it once and felt the morning tilt. Sol read it beside him with her lips moving. Nobody spoke for the length of a breath.

"None," Sol said. "The clerks' summary you were given for the district said closed."

"It did." He kept his voice level and slow, the voice he used when he refused to draw a conclusion in front of a witness. "Either a tired clerk compressed six sites into one word, or a clerk was told which word to use. This card cannot say which. I would not like either of us to say it for the card."

"Then we don't."

"The full register entry is in the decommissioning room, Auditor," Bram said. "The senior clerk keeps the key, and she sits at the Chancellor's table through the eighteenth. The room opens after. I can enter a written request for you now, in both names, if it pleases you."

"In both names," Kael said. "And enter another line, if you would. One pass of House Thorne, for Anchor Seven, received by the Auditor this morning at second bell from a Thorne courier. Held unused. Add that the Lady Vane read it in my presence before any step was taken."

Bram's pen moved fast and did not blot. When he had finished he turned the ledger so they could both read the line, then reached for the sleeve, and Kael watched the black wax go into a Council box with a Council slip tied around it. He felt lighter and did not trust the feeling. A thing lodged in the open was not a thing solved. It was only a thing where two people, and now a clerk, could see it.

Sol had taken out the notebook. She wrote a line on a fresh page, standing, with the book flat on her palm, and shielded it with her wrist in the way a person shields a private tally. Kael was an auditor, and an auditor learns to read upside down. He did not let his face say so.

*16 Sun. Told before. Asked. Pass entered, unused.*

The two words in the middle were the ones his eye stayed on. Told before. Asked. He read them as he might have read a receipt for a debt he had not known he was paying down. Then he looked away, because the note was not meant for him, and because the alternative was to ask her what number the tally stood at, and he did not yet want to hear.

Bram slid the card back toward the tray. "By your leave, Auditor."

"One moment." Kael took it up again, because a card that had been moved deserved a second look at its back. The reverse was blank cream. But in the margin beside the site number, faint against the ruling and fresh enough to shine at an angle, was a small pencil tick, the mark of a reader who means to return.

"Did you make this?"

Bram bent to it, and the color left his face by degrees. "I do not keep a pencil at this desk, Auditor. No one does. The rule is ink, so that nothing can be rubbed out."

Sol's hand had stopped over the page. Neither of them said aloud the only question the tick raised. A stranger had stood at this desk in the hours it was dark, had drawn the same card from the same tray, and had left a mark to find it by again. Whether they had been searching for the site, or for the two people who would come asking after it, the pencil did not say.
