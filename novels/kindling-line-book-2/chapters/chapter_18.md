The house at the top of the Reach had no gate. It had a stair, wide and shallow, with a lamp under a violet glass shade at every fourth step and two retainers at the head of it who bowed to Sol before they bowed to him. Kael noted the order. He noted everything at Thorne now, the way he would have noted a column in a ledger that a careful hand had made to look like nothing.

Sol climbed beside him with her hands held close to her ribs. The healer had changed the linen that morning to a finer weave, and she could curl her fingers as far as the second knuckle, though not press them against anything. She had told him so over breakfast in the level voice she kept for figures. He had answered in kind, "Thank you for telling me," and she had looked at him a moment as if checking a sum, and let it stand.

Two pages lay folded in her coat pocket under the archive's seal, delivered by noon yesterday, as promised. She had shown him the envelope and not the pages. That was hers. He was learning its outline.

A small upright woman with a copper clasp met them in the hall and led them through without a word. The panelling was dark, the lamp brackets copper, the walls bare. Kael had expected portraits and found none, and understood that a house which kept its dead in ledgers had no need to hang them.

The dining room held a round table laid for four, and one wall that was all window: the long drop to the Reach floor, the lamps coming on tier by tier below like an account being totted up. Lord Malrik Thorne stood by his chair without help. At home he wore the same deep grey, the same plain silver at his throat, the same straight spine, and he was smaller than Kael had let himself remember.

"Auditor. Lady Vane." He inclined his head to each, and again the depth was the same. "You came together. I asked it, and did not expect to be obeyed."

"Neither of us was going to come alone," Sol said.

"No. I see that." The old face approved of the answer and did not trouble to hide it. "My grandson, Aldous."

The young man beside him was perhaps twenty-five, with his grandfather's height and none of his ease. He drew out Sol's chair, looked stricken at having touched it, and stepped back. She said, "Thank you," and sat, and he seemed to breathe again.

There was no cutlery at her place. There was a cup of broth with two wide handles, bread already torn, cheese cut in squares, a pear halved and cored. Kael saw her see it.

"The house is attentive," she said.

"The house has had practice." Malrik lowered himself into his chair. "In the last year of the trade our infirmary wing fed forty people who could not hold a spoon. It became a habit of the kitchen. Habits outlive their reasons."

Sol did not smile. She lifted the cup between both palms, slowly, her wrists doing the work, and drank, and set it down without losing a drop. Kael kept his hands flat on the cloth. "The cheese," he said, low. "On the bread, or as it is?"

"On the bread." She waited a breath. "Only that."

He laid four squares on the torn bread and took his hand away. It was a very small thing, and he felt the whole table watching him do it correctly.

"Vrell tells me you read every page," Malrik said.

"I read every page."

"And found nothing to admire."

"I found a great deal I believe. It isn't the same." She held his eyes. "There were red-brown dots beside the dead. Always in the same place on the page."

Malrik looked at his own right hand, lying on the cloth, the forefinger stained faintly at the tip. "Oak gall and rust. I make the ink myself. Every year at the turn of the season I go down to the archive and touch each name, so that the clerk's column does not become the whole record." He said it the way a man reports the weather. "It takes four days now."

Aldous looked at his plate. Kael understood that the boy had never once been allowed to skip the descent.

"The two pages," Sol said. "My mother's contract. I want to say one thing about them."

"They were not put on account," said Malrik. "If that is what you wish to know."

"It is." She drew a breath. "Then I will say thank you here, once, and it won't be filed."

"It will not be filed."

The steward stood behind the old man's chair and did not move. Kael thought she was counting.

They ate for a quarter of an hour. It was not a bad meal, and Kael tasted very little of it. The talk went where such talk goes: the drought on the eastern terraces, the price of hemp, whether Renn would be made a full commissioner. Malrik was courteous and well informed and, once, unexpectedly funny about the Chancellor's habit of ruling in the gap between two other people's sentences. Then he set down his cup.

"I will not insult you with a long approach, Auditor."

"I would rather you didn't."

"Your father's name stood behind the Accord's instruments, and the Reach believed them because it was his. Thorne asks the same of the sitting auditor. Not a false word. A true one, spoken by the man the Council trusts, before the Council opens the box on the twenty-eighth."

The steward stepped forward and laid a folded slip beside Kael's plate. He did not open it.

"What true word?"

"That for three years House Thorne kept the Lower Reach lit at its own cost. That the tap was a stewardship."

"The ledger calls it a tap on a trunk line that belonged to the Vault. It calls the man who kept the meter a prisoner."

"The clerk was kept," said Malrik. "I am told he was warm. I am not proud of the locked door, and I would lock it again." He said this evenly too, with no heat in it at all. "You have found the tap. You have not yet asked what happens when it is closed."

Kael had. He had asked it in the gallery with the water at his knees, and had not liked the arithmetic. "The trunk line surges back up the tap."

"Unless it is shut in order. There is an order. My smiths took three years to learn it and set it down in one hand, and one person holds the page." Malrik folded his hands. "The Council's chain is a brave thing, Auditor. It is twelve pounds of survey iron doing the work of a Thorne ingot, and it will slip, and when it slips forty households go dark with the water coming up under them. When the Council opens the box it will argue for weeks. The lamps must be kept lit through the arguing. I would prefer that it be a house that knows how to shut the tap. I would prefer that you were the one to say so."

The trap, if it was one, was built entirely of true things. Kael felt the pull of it low and strong, like the charge in the gallery on the swell: certify the sentence, take the order, retire the tap in the dark before it could drown anyone, and carry the cost of the certifying alone, as he had carried the archive and the coat and the ledger. It would be clean, and it might even be right. It was the very shape of the thing Sol had named in the lamplight two nights ago, made large enough to walk into.

"And if I say the opposite?" he asked.

"Then I say it myself, in the same chamber, and the Council chooses between a house it distrusts and forty households in the dark." Malrik's voice did not change. "I have counted the votes. I would rather not win that way. I will if I must."

Kael looked at Sol. Not to ask permission. To see her. She had set both palms flat against the cloth and was watching the folded slip, not the old man, and her face was doing the sum he knew it was doing.

"I'm not going to answer that," he told Malrik. "Not at this table, and not alone. Whatever I say to the Council, I will say after I have spoken to her."

"That is your right."

"Read us the sentence," said Sol.

Kael unfolded the slip and read it aloud, because her hands could not have held it. "House Thorne kept the Lower Reach lit for three years, at its own cost, in good faith and in the public interest, and stands ready to make the trunk line safe." He lowered it. "In good faith is a finding. I can't certify a finding I haven't made."

"Then strike the words," Malrik said at once. "Keep the lamps. Keep the last clause." He turned to Sol. "Lady Vane, the offer is to the auditor, but it is to you as well. I would sooner be opposed by both of you than tolerated by either."

"You say things twice, my lord, in different words, until they sound like law." Her voice was steady. "I heard it at the archive. I'll hear it again on the twenty-eighth."

Aldous coughed into his hand, and might have been hiding a smile.

"The house files its statement with the Chancellor's clerk at the seventh bell tomorrow," Malrik said. "It will say one of two things. It will say that Thorne offers the shutting order to the Council in good faith, with the sitting auditor's word behind it. Or it will say that Thorne offers it against the auditor's silence, and the Council may draw its own conclusions from the difference. I would like to know which before I set my seal to it. Give me your answer by the sixth bell, before the day's first session."

"You'll have it," Kael said. He did not say which.

Aldous saw them down. The retainers at the head of the stair bowed to Sol first again, and she inclined her head to each, and Kael found that he minded the courtesy less than he had on the way up. At the third lamp from the bottom she stopped, and he stopped with her.

"I wanted to say yes," he said. "At the table. I'd have said it alone and told you after, and it would have been a good sentence in a clean ledger. I want you to have that from me now, not later."

She looked at him for a long moment. "Thank you for saying it before I asked."

"It doesn't change what he's holding."

"No. He's right about the chain." She turned her wrapped hands over in the violet light and studied them as if they were a document. "I set it on that plate. It won't stand forever, and I knew that when I did it."

"I know."

They went on down. Behind and above them the house put out its lamps one violet shade at a time, until only the dining room window still burned, where an old man sat on alone beside a cold cup, waiting for a bell.
