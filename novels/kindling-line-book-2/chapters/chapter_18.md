<!-- chapter_date: 26 Sunspire, Year 3 of the Reckoning Accord -->

The notice arrived with the morning post, carried by a courier whose uniform bore the Accord's crossed keys rather than Thorne's climbing ivy. Sol stood in the kitchen of the narrow flat she had taken above the archive annex, the paper warm from the courier's hand, and read the summons twice before the words stopped swimming.

*Formal Hearing: Ward Deed of Succession — Contested Claims and Absorption Clauses. Council Chamber, Spire Crown. 3 Brightward, Year 3. Nine bells. Attendance compelled for all named parties and accredited auditors.*

Seven days. Seven days to turn a sealed ledger page into something the Council could not ignore.

She set the notice on the scarred table and pressed her palms flat against the wood grain, breathing. The flat smelled of dust and ink and the faint mineral tang that clung to everything in the Reach's lower tiers. Morning light cut pale across the floorboards through the single window. Somewhere below, a water vendor called out prices in the street.

Kael had not come home last night. He had told her he would be at the auditor's office in the Spire Crown, reviewing the Thorne petition clause by clause. He had told her this before he left, the words careful and deliberate, the pattern named between them now but not yet broken. *I'm going to the office. I'll be late. I don't want you waiting up.*

He had not lied. He had simply decided, once again, that his presence there mattered more than his presence here, and he had informed her of the decision after making it.

The confrontation in the hallway two nights past still lived in her shoulders. The Thorne-sealed entry pass. The date stamp. The site name she had never heard him mention. *I'm not asking you to tell me everything. I'm asking you to stop deciding what I don't need to know.*

He had admitted it without denial. Without excuse. *You're right. I did decide. I'm sorry.*

The apology had settled in the air like sediment, heavy and insufficient. She had not forgiven him. She had not known how to begin.

Now the Council had handed her a deadline, and the luxury of silence was gone.

She picked up the notice again, turning it over. The Accord seal was pressed into the wax, the crossed keys clean and deep. No ivy. No Thorne crest. The legal arena, not the political one. That distinction mattered. Thorne had petitioned Kael as sitting auditor, had framed their bid as reform, had wrapped seizure in the language of stewardship. But the Council had chosen to hear the matter under Accord law, not Thorne's preferred narrative.

*A house that has already paid the cost has earned the right to control who pays it next.*

The words had come from Thorne's representative at the public forum on 14 Sunspire, spoken into the murmuring crowd with the quiet conviction of someone stating weather. Sol had stood at the back of the square, Kael's hand warm on her elbow, and watched the crowd lean toward the logic. Three generations of ward service. Three generations of blood given to the old trade. The grievance was real. The wound was old. And Thorne was not wrong that the system had failed them — it had failed her mother, failed countless others — but their cure was a knife disguised as medicine.

The absorption clause in the Ward Deed of Succession was the blade. *Ultimate authority over absorbed contracts shall vest in the house administering reform, for the duration of the transition and such further period as the Council may determine.* Duration undefined. Transition undefined. Council oversight theoretical.

She moved to the narrow desk beneath the window, pulled out the notebook she had begun on 19 Sunspire, the night she recognized the Thorne seal on the entry pass. The seal she had seen pressed into wax at the forum, on the reform proposal documents Kael had brought home for review. She had memorized it then, the climbing ivy wrapped around a hammer, because Kael had pointed it out. *Thorne's house mark. You'll see it on everything they submit. Get used to it.*

She had gotten used to it. She had recognized it on the pass in his coat pocket. She had recognized it on the ledger page they had pulled from the disused ward site on 21 Sunspire, the Ward Deed of Succession itself, sealed in Thorne's own wax.

The notebook's pages were dense with her handwriting. Timeline. Cross-references. Accord statutes. Thorne's petition language. The absorption clause dissected word by word. She had spent the last five days in the archive annex, pulling every ward contract Thorne had ever held, every succession record, every Council ruling on absorption precedents. There were three. Three precedents in two hundred years. Each had transferred authority temporarily. Each had reverted to the Accord within eighteen months.

Thorne's clause contained no reversion date.

She opened to a fresh page and wrote the hearing date at the top. *3 Brightward. Nine bells. Council Chamber.*

Below it, she began to map her questions. Not for Kael. For the Council. For the record.

*Question one: Under what statutory authority does the absorption clause eliminate the Accord's reversionary interest in ward contracts?*

*Question two: The deed cites "duration of transition" as the measure of transferred authority. Which Council resolution defines transition in the context of ward-trade dismantlement?*

*Question three: Thorne's petition argues that three generations of ward service constitute earned authority. Does the Accord recognize hereditary service as a basis for administrative control over dismantlement?*

*Question four: The deed is sealed in Thorne's house wax, not the Accord's. Does the Accord accept privately sealed instruments as binding on public ward infrastructure?*

She wrote until her fingers cramped, until the light shifted across the floorboards, until the water vendor's call faded into the general hum of the Reach waking. She wrote questions about the ledger page's provenance, about the disused ward site where they had found it, about the entry pass in Kael's coat and the site it accessed and the date he had been there instead of at paperwork.

She wrote until the door opened behind her.

"You're up early."

Kael's voice. Tired. The exhaustion in it was not performed. She did not turn.

"The notice came."

She heard him cross the room, the creak of floorboards, the rustle of the courier's envelope being set beside her notebook. The Accord seal caught the light as he turned it over.

"Three Brightward," he said. "Seven days."

"Nine bells."

"The full Council panel. Not a subcommittee."

"Good."

He moved to the window, stood with his back to her. His coat was rumpled. Dark circles scored the skin beneath his eyes. He had not slept, or had slept badly. The auditor's office in the Spire Crown would have been cold at this hour, the stone holding the night's chill.

"Did you read the attachment?" he asked.

"There's an attachment?"

He turned. Held out a second sheet, folded once. She took it, unfolded it, scanned the dense script.

*Supplemental Notice: Pursuant to Accord Article VII, Section 4, the following parties are designated as required witnesses: Isolde Vane, accredited ward-taker; Kael Ashworth, lead auditor; Thorne representative(s) to be named. Written testimony due 1 Brightward. Oral examination to follow.*

Her name. Printed in the Accord's hand. Required witness.

"They're calling me," she said.

"They're calling both of us. You as the ward-taker who discovered the deed. Me as the auditor who certified the site excavation."

"I didn't certify the excavation. You did. Alone."

The words hung between them. Not an accusation. A statement.

Kael's jaw tightened. "I certified it because you were not there. Because I told you to stay at the archive."

"Because you decided I didn't need to be there."

"Because the site was unstable. Because the ward residue was unpredictable. Because I have seen what unstable residue does to ward-takers who don't have the luxury of a buffer."

The word landed between them. *Buffer.* The mechanic from the old trade. The role her mother had filled for her father, the role the Accord had tried to formalize, the role she and Kael had never chosen and could not choose now. The involuntary cost-transfer between them was not a buffer. It was a collision. She had felt it in the disused site, the sudden sharp spike of Kael's pain when the ward residue had lashed out, the echo in her own bones that had nothing to do with sympathy and everything to do with the thing binding them that neither had asked for.

"I know what it does," she said quietly. "I've felt it. So have you. That's not the point."

"Then what is the point?"

She stood. The notebook pressed against her thigh. The notice in her hand. The attachment with her name on it.

"The point is that you went to a Thorne-accessed site on 23 Sunspire. You used a Thorne-sealed pass. You told me you were doing paperwork. You didn't tell me about the site. You didn't tell me about the pass. You decided I didn't need to know until I found it in your coat."

"I told you after."

"After I found it. After you had already gone. After the decision was made."

"I told you the truth when you asked."

"And if I hadn't asked?"

He was silent. The silence stretched, filled with the street sounds rising through the window.

"If you hadn't asked," he said finally, "I would have told you before the hearing. When the testimony was due. I would not have let you walk in blind."

"But you would have let me walk in uninformed."

"I would have protected you from the choice until the choice was necessary."

She laughed, a short sharp sound. "That's not protection, Kael. That's the same logic Thorne uses. *We've paid the cost. We know better. We'll decide what you need to survive.*"

His face went still. The comparison struck deeper than she had intended, but she did not take it back.

"I am not Thorne," he said.

"No. You're not. But the pattern is the same. You decide what I can carry. You decide what I need to know. You decide the risk and you take it alone and you tell me after, and you call it honesty because you don't lie when I ask. But deciding for me isn't honesty. It's the same cage with a different lock."

She had not planned to say it. The words had formed in the notebook, in the margins of her questions, in the sleepless hours between 24 and 26 Sunspire. Now they existed in the air between them, and she could not unmake them.

Kael stared at her. Something shifted behind his eyes, something that might have been recognition or might have been defense.

"I go alone because the cost lands on me," he said. "Not on you. Not on anyone else. Me. The auditor. The lead. The one the Accord named. When the ward residue spikes, when the site collapses, when Thorne's traps trigger — I am the one who pays. So yes. I decide. Because I am the one who bleeds for it."

"The cost doesn't land only on you."

"It lands on me first. It lands on me hardest. And if I can keep it from landing on you at all —"

"You can't." The words came flat, certain. "We've been over this. The mechanic doesn't work that way. It never has. My mother proved that. Your father proved that. Every ward-taker and auditor pair in the history of the Reach has proved that. You go alone, you get hurt, I feel it anyway. The only difference is I don't know why until you tell me. Or until I find the pass in your coat."

His hand moved, an aborted gesture toward her. Stopped mid-air. Fell to his side.

"Then what do you want from me, Sol?"

She had asked herself that question every night since the hallway. She had written answers in the notebook, crossed them out, rewritten them. She did not have a clean one. She had only the truth, messy and incomplete.

"I want you to stop deciding the boundary of my involvement. I want you to bring me the pass before you use it. I want you to tell me about the site before you go. I want you to ask me to come with you, and I want you to accept my answer — whatever it is — instead of making it for me. I want to be your partner, not your ward."

The word hung. *Ward.* The old term. The protected one. The one who receives the cost.

Kael flinched. Barely visible. A tightening around the eyes.

"I never —"

"I know you never meant it that way. Intent doesn't change the shape of the thing."

He was quiet for a long moment. The light moved across his face, highlighting the exhaustion, the lines that had not been there two months ago. The auditor's burden. The Thorne petition. The Council hearing in seven days. The Ward Deed of Succession and its absorption clause and the ledger page sealed in Thorne wax.

"All right," he said.

"All right what?"

"All right. I'll bring you the pass before I use it. I'll tell you about the site before I go. I'll ask you to come. I'll accept your answer."

She searched his face for the tell she had learned to read in the archive's dim light, in the Spire Crown's council chambers, in the disused ward site's flickering residue. The tell that meant *I am saying this to end the argument.* The tell that meant *I will forget by tomorrow.*

She did not see it.

She saw something else. Something that looked like the pattern cracking.

"Tomorrow," she said. "There's a site on the docket. The old Thorne ward station at Greyledge. You have a pass for it."

His expression did not change. "I do."

"Bring it to me tonight. Before you go."

"I will."

"And the testimony. Due 1 Brightward. Three days. We write it together."

"We do."

She nodded once. The notebook pressed against her thigh. The notice in her hand. The attachment with her name printed beside his.

"Good," she said. "Then we should start."

She moved to the desk, pulled out the second chair. Kael watched her for a moment, then crossed the room and sat. The chair scraped the floorboards. He reached for the notice, turned it in his hands, studied the Accord seal as if seeing it for the first time.

"The absorption clause," he said. "The Council will focus on the transfer of authority. Thorne will argue precedent. We need to show them the precedent doesn't hold."

"Three precedents. Eighteen months maximum. Thorne's clause has no ceiling."

"Exactly. And the deed itself — the provenance is thin. Found in a disused site. Sealed in Thorne wax. No Accord witness. No independent verification."

"Which is why my testimony matters. I was there. I saw the seal. I can speak to the condition of the document, the site, the residue."

"You can. But Thorne will challenge your credibility. You're Vane. Fallen house. They'll say you're biased."

"Let them. Bias doesn't change the wax. Bias doesn't change the residue readings I took. Bias doesn't change the entry pass in your coat."

Kael looked up. His eyes held hers, steady and tired and something else. Something that might have been trust.

"The pass," he said. "The site at Greyledge. It's a ward station Thorne decommissioned in Year 1. The pass grants access to the sub-level archives. Contract records. Succession logs. If the absorption clause has teeth, the evidence is there."

"Then we go together."

"If you're sure."

"I'm sure."

He held her gaze. The morning light caught the gold in his irises, the flecks of amber that had caught her attention the first time she had really looked at him, across a training courtyard two years past. The world had been simpler then. Simpler and harder. She had been hiding. He had been hunting. Neither had known the other's truth.

Now the truth was all they had. The Accord seal on the notice. The Thorne ivy on the pass. The absorption clause in the ledger. The pattern named between them, not yet broken but cracked.

"Three days to the testimony," Kael said. "Seven to the hearing. We have time."

"Do we?"

He considered. "We have what we make. That's always been true."

She thought of her mother. Of the ward-trade contracts she had signed, the cost she had absorbed, the silence she had kept. Of the Accord that had promised reform and delivered a new set of chains. Of the Deep Vault and its unnamed Keeper, the hidden ledger burning somewhere beneath the Reach's foundations.

"We'll see," she said.

She opened the notebook to a fresh page. Wrote *Testimony — 1 Brightward* at the top. Beneath it, *Sol and Kael. Together.*

Kael picked up the pen she had left on the desk. His handwriting joined hers on the page, different slant, heavier pressure. *Question five: If the absorption clause is upheld, what prevents the administering house from extending the transition indefinitely?*

*Question six: The deed references "ward contracts Thorne proposes to absorb." By what authority does Thorne propose? The Accord? The Vault? Their own house seal?*

*Question seven: The Keeper of the Burning Ledger is not named in the deed. Does the Accord recognize instruments that reference an unnamed institutional authority?*

The pen scratched. The light moved. The street sounds rose and fell. Seven days to the hearing. Three days to the testimony. A pattern cracked but not broken. A partnership reforming in the space between questions.

Outside the window, the Reach climbed toward the sun, tier upon tier of stone and history and secrets. Somewhere in the Deep Vault, a ledger burned. Somewhere in Thorne's house, a seal pressed into wax. Somewhere in the Council Chamber, seven days hence, the Accord's crossed keys would weigh a deed and decide the shape of what came next.

Sol wrote the next question. Kael wrote the one after. The notebook filled. The morning passed. The pattern held, for now, reshaped by the simple act of asking instead of deciding.

But the pass in Kael's coat was still in Kael's coat. The Greyledge site was still waiting. The hearing notice still bore the Accord seal, not Thorne's — and she did not yet know whether that distinction would save them or condemn them.
