The Great Hall of the Accord sat carved into the living rock of Thornmere's central spire, its vaulted ceiling lost in shadow where the old wards still hummed. Morning light cut through the high western windows at a slant, painting gold bars across the stone floor where the assembled houses stood in their formal colors. House Thorne in black and burnt orange. House Ashworth in grey and pale blue. The lesser houses in their muted livery, packed into the galleries like moths drawn to a flame they could not name.

Sol stood at the forward edge of the Ashworth delegation, her hands folded at her waist, her spine straight. The reformed ward-taker trial she had chosen waited on the other side of this vote, a room below the hall where the new methodology would be tested on her body first. She had not asked Kael's permission. She had told him her decision the night before, and he had nodded once, the muscle in his jaw jumping, and said only that he would be in the gallery.

The Grand Auditor's gavel struck the sounding stone. The resonance rolled through the chamber, a low tone felt in the ribs.

"The Accord convenes," the Grand Auditor said, her voice unamplified, carrying on the hall's designed acoustics. "We sit in judgment of the Reform Proposal submitted by House Thorne, titled 'On the Centralized Administration of Ward Infrastructure.' The Ward Deed of Succession, recovered from the disused Vault-linked site at Graymire and authenticated under Accord seal, stands entered as Exhibit Prime."

A murmur rose and fell. Thorne's delegation did not shift. Their lead representative, a woman named Veyra Thorne with hair the color of cold iron and eyes that had not blinked since the session opened, stood with her hands clasped behind her back. Beside her, two younger Thorne scions flanked her like honor guards, their faces blank.

"The Deed," the Grand Auditor continued, "bears the wax seal of House Thorne. It lists forty-seven ward contracts currently administered by the Vault's distributed network. It contains a clause transferring ultimate authority over those contracts to 'the house administering reform' upon ratification of this proposal. The question before this body: does this clause constitute a lawful restructuring under the Reckoning Accord, or a seizure of infrastructure disguised as reform?"

She turned her gaze across the chamber. "House Ashworth, as lead auditor, presents the finding."

Kael stepped forward. The grey of his formal coat caught the light. He moved with the economy of someone who had learned to make stillness into authority. The censure that had dogged him since the Graymire injury, the whispered questions about his judgment, the formal reprimand from his own father's seat — all of it seemed to have settled into his shoulders as something heavier than shame, lighter than pride.

"Grand Auditor, members of the Accord," Kael said. His voice pitched to the hall's acoustics, clear without strain. "The audit began sixty-one days ago. I began it alone. That was an error of judgment, not of mandate. I corrected it when Sol Vane, my co-lead, confronted me with the pattern. The Ward Deed of Succession was discovered at Graymire on the seventeenth of Sunspire. It was sealed in Thorne wax. The clause in question — Article Nine, Section Three — reads: 'Upon ratification, all enumerated contracts shall transfer to the administering house for the duration of the reform period, renewable at the administering house's discretion.'"

He let the words hang.

"The reform period is undefined. The administering house is unnamed in the clause but identified in the preamble as the proposer — House Thorne. The discretion to renew rests solely with that house. There is no Accord oversight mechanism. No sunset provision. No appeal path. In the language of the Accord's founding charter, this is not reform. This is accession."

Veyra Thorne moved then, a fluid shift of weight. "Auditor Ashworth characterizes the clause in isolation. The preamble speaks of stability. Of ending the fragmentation that cost lives in the Ward Wars. My house has served as ward-keepers for three generations. We paid the cost when the old system failed. We buried our dead in the same ground as House Vane's. The Deed offers continuity under a house that knows the weight."

"You buried your dead," Sol said from the floor. Every head turned. She had not risen to the speaking dais. She stood where she was, and her voice carried anyway. "My mother died because the ward trade sold her contract to a house that did not honor it. House Thorne was the broker of that sale. You did not bury her. You invoiced her."

The chamber went cold. Veyra Thorne's expression did not change, but something tightened at the corners of her mouth.

"The Deed," Sol continued, "lists my mother's contract among the forty-seven. It lists the contracts of six other ward-takers who died under Thorne-brokered transfers. You propose to absorb the consequences of your own brokering and call it reform. The Accord was written to prevent exactly this — a single house converting stewardship into ownership."

"Auditor Vane speaks from grief," Veyra said, the title deliberate, the omission of 'co-lead' audible. "The Accord votes on law, not loss."

"The Accord votes on precedent," Kael said. "And the precedent of the Ward Deed is clear. A house that has already paid the cost claims the right to control who pays it next. Those were your words, spoken in the Graymire site, recorded by the Accord scribe. You said a house that has already paid the cost has earned the right to control who pays it next. That is not the language of reform. That is the language of inheritance."

He had not looked at her when he spoke the quote. He had not needed to. The scribe's transcript had been entered into evidence three days prior.

The Grand Auditor let the silence stretch. Then: "The floor recognizes House Thorne's right of response."

Veyra Thorne smiled. It did not reach her eyes. "The floor recognizes that House Ashworth's lead auditor and his co-lead share a bed. The floor might also recognize that the Ward Deed was 'discovered' at a site where House Ashworth conducted an unauthorized solo audit, then suppressed for nine days before being shared with this body. The floor might ask whether the Deed is evidence of Thorne's ambition or Ashworth's manufacture."

A ripple moved through the galleries. Kael's father, Lord Valerius Ashworth, sat in the senior gallery with his face carved from stone. He did not look at his son.

"The Deed was not suppressed," Kael said. "It was verified. The wax seal was authenticated by the Accord's own sigil-wardens. The document's provenance traces to Thorne's own archive at Graymire, logged in Thorne's hand. The solo audit was my error. I admitted it. I corrected it. The Deed stands on its own merit — which is to say, it condemns itself."

"And the injury at Graymire?" Veyra pressed. "The ward-contracted ally who bled while Auditor Ashworth worked alone? Was that also corrected?"

The hall held its breath. Sol felt the old mechanism stir in her chest, the involuntary pull that had bound her to Kael since the night the old ward trade fell. She did not reach for him. She did not need to. The cost-transfer was not voluntary, not redirectable, not altered by any reform. It simply was. She breathed through the familiar pressure behind her ribs.

"Corin Vell was injured because I did not wait for backup," Kael said. "He lives. He will walk again. The fault is mine. The Deed's authenticity is not."

"Is it not?" Veyra turned to the Grand Auditor. "Grand Auditor, we move to strike Exhibit Prime on grounds of compromised chain of custody. The solo audit. The nine-day delay. The personal entanglement of the auditors. If the Accord values integrity, it must value process."

"The Accord values truth," a new voice said.

It came from the gallery. A man in the plain robes of the Accord's procedural office stood at the rail. Sol recognized him — Master Scribe Olwen, the same who had overseen the Graymire transcript. He held a folded document.

"The Ward Deed of Succession was authenticated on the twentieth of Sunspire by three independent sigil-wardens. The chain of custody was reconstructed from Thorne's own archive logs, which were seized under Accord warrant on the twenty-second. The logs confirm the Deed was drafted in Thorne's hand, sealed in Thorne's wax, and stored in Thorne's strongroom. The nine-day delay allowed for that seizure and verification. The solo audit triggered the warrant. Without it, the Deed would never have been found."

Veyra Thorne's mask slipped. Just for a heartbeat. A flash of calculation, of reassessment.

"The warrant," she said, "was issued on whose authority?"

"Mine," the Grand Auditor said. "On the recommendation of Auditor Ashworth, who reported his own procedural error and requested independent verification. That report is also in the record."

Kael bowed his head. A fraction. An acknowledgment.

"The motion to strike is denied," the Grand Auditor said. "The Deed stands. The vote proceeds."

The voting stones were distributed. White for rejection. Black for ratification. Grey for abstention. The clerks moved down the rows, and the chamber filled with the soft click of stone on stone.

Sol watched Kael. He stood at the dais, motionless, his face giving nothing away. But she knew the tension in his hands, clasped behind his back. She knew the cost he had carried since Graymire — Corin Vell's blood on his coat, his father's silence at the evening meal, the formal censure that had stripped him of solo authority and named her co-equal. The repair had cost him standing in his own house. It had cost him the clean narrative of the competent heir. He had traded both for the truth.

And now the truth was voting.

The tally took twenty minutes. The Grand Auditor read each house's stone aloud. Thorne voted black. Three lesser houses voted black. Two voted grey. The rest, twenty-eight houses, voted white.

"The Reform Proposal is rejected," the Grand Auditor declared. "The Ward Deed of Succession is ruled evidence of bad-faith seizure. House Thorne's claim to centralized administration is denied. The Vault's distributed network remains under Accord oversight. The contracts enumerated in the Deed revert to their current administrators pending individual review."

The gavel fell. Final.

In the galleries, a breath released — not a cheer, not a celebration, but the sound of tension departing a room that had held it for weeks. Thorne's delegation did not move. Veyra Thorne inclined her head, a fraction, and turned. Her scions followed. The black and burnt orange livery withdrew in a column, silent, disciplined, leaving behind the smell of oil and old ash.

Kael did not sag. He did not smile. He simply lowered his hands from behind his back and turned to face the chamber. His father sat in the gallery, grey and pale blue, and for the first time in sixty-one days, Lord Valerius Ashworth met his son's eyes across the distance. He did not nod. But the set of his shoulders shifted. The censure that had been a wall between them cracked, just slightly, replaced by something that might have been respect — fragile, unspoken, earned at a price neither had wanted to pay.

Sol let out a breath she had not known she was holding. The pressure in her chest eased, not because the cost-transfer had changed — it had not, would not, could not — but because the immediate threat had passed. Thorne's bid was broken. The Ward Deed would be sealed in the Deep Vault under Accord guard, its dangerous clause neutralized by public record. The reformed ward-taker trial waited below, and she would walk into it by her own choice, bearing her own risk, with Kael watching from the gallery.

"House Ashworth," the Grand Auditor said. "The reformed trial proceeds as scheduled. Auditor Vane, you are expected in the lower chamber."

Sol looked at Kael. He was already looking at her. The distance between them — the dais and the floor, the auditor and the co-lead, the man who had decided for her and the woman who had forced him to stop — remained. But the pattern had been named. The pattern had been broken. The repair had been public and formal and costly.

She walked toward the stair that led down. Behind her, she heard Kael say to the Grand Auditor, "I will observe from the gallery. With the Accord's leave."

"Granted."

The heavy doors at the hall's rear closed behind Thorne's departing column. The sound was final. The vote was done. The Deed was sealed. The trial waited.

Sol descended into the cool dark, her hand brushing the carved rail, and wondered what the Keeper of the Burning Ledger would make of this day — the seat in the founding government, the office that had watched the ward trade rise and fall and rise again, the institutional silence that had never once required a face.

The stair turned. The light from above receded. Ahead, the lower chamber's door stood open, and beyond it, the new methodology waited in its glass and steel and living ward-stone, ready for the first volunteer.
