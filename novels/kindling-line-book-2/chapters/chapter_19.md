They did not sleep. They argued instead, properly, at the table by the window with the lamp turned low and the folded slip lying between them where neither of them would have to touch it.

"The case for yes," Sol said. "Say it plainly."

"The chain is failing." Kael did not soften it. "Not tonight. But it's iron in wet air, standing in for a Thorne ingot, and the tap will have to be shut in the right order before it fails for good. If Malrik does it, forty households never learn how close it came. What I'd pay for that is a sentence in a hearing."

"And the case for no."

"He wants the auditor to tell the Reach that a house which locked a man in a cellar was its steward. Said in that chamber, it stays said." He turned the slip a quarter turn with one finger. "And he wants it said before the box is opened. Before anyone has read what's inside. I'd be vouching for a house whose main paper I haven't seen."

"That's the one."

"That's the one," Kael agreed. He looked at the window, at the lamps of the lower tiers, and she watched a sentence arrive on his face and be put down unspoken. "I'll go and look at the chain, and then I'll..." He stopped. "No. Sol, will you come with me? I want to see what it's doing before I answer him."

She had learned to count small things. "Yes," she said, and took her coat down from its hook by the wrists, and he held it open, and she let him.

The Lower Reach in the small hours smelled of tar and wet hemp. Ansa's window over the tar shop was lit, and a rope creaked on a bridge. The ropewalk gallery ran away from them into the dark like a loom abandoned mid-thread, and at its far end two of Ashworth grey stood by the hatch beside two of the Council's guards, and none of the four looked as though they had slept either. Mara nodded to Sol. Joren, the scar white across the back of his hand, said, "My lord. My lady," and then, "Renn's been down the stair twice since dusk. He won't say why."

"He counts," Sol said. "It's what he has."

Renn sat at a trestle inside the hatch office with a lamp, a ledger, and a board of ten brass pegs, and he came awake so fast the pegs jumped in their holes. "Auditor. Lady Vane." He said it as though he had been caught out. "I have the count."

"Which count?" Kael asked.

"Both." He turned the ledger toward them. Two columns in his cramped hand: the cycle in breaths, the chain in links. "I take the links through a glass from the near edge of the gallery, on the drop, at every change of guard. Ten pins, one hundred links, the plumb. I laid it straight on the plate so the count would be honest. The cycle I take from the landing." He looked at Sol and away. "I can't feel it as you do. I count the wall lamp."

"Let me," Sol said.

They went down the spiral stair, the dry part, to the second landing where the walls began to sweat. Kael went one step below her, where she could see him, and did not offer his elbow. The stone was cold enough to bite through the linen. She stopped on the landing and shut her eyes.

The line came up through the wall the way it always did, less a sound than a change in the weight of the air. A rise. A held breath. The discharge up the walls, and the drop. She counted it against her own pulse, which ran too fast, and again against the slow tick of water below, which did not.

"Ten," she said. "Steady. Ten, ten, ten." She opened her eyes. "It hasn't moved."

"It has held since I laid the chain," Renn said behind them, low. "I counted last at the tenth bell."

"Then he said it would slip." Kael's voice was level. "How fast?"

Renn had done the sum. She could tell by how he answered without touching the ledger. "Iron in a wet gallery loses weight at the surface. A chain that size, an ounce a week, perhaps two. The plate forgives a few ounces before the cycle notices. It falls to nine by the middle of Cinderveil and to eight by the end of it, if nothing is done." He swallowed. "And eight is where it surges."

"Weeks," Sol said. "Not days."

"Weeks. It's a real problem, my lady. It isn't an emergency yet."

She stood a moment longer with her back against the wet stone. Malrik had told them the truth and dressed it in the size of an emergency. There would be weeks. The Council would argue through them, and the weeks would run out on a day Thorne had not troubled to mention.

"He's right about the chain," she said. "He's wrong about the hour."

"Both," said Kael.

They climbed back up into the lamplight. On the bench Renn's tally board stood open, its ten pegs ranked in their holes, and Sol, who had not tried to close a hand around anything since the ropewalk, put out her right hand and set thumb and forefinger against the nearest peg. Her fingers screamed inside the linen. She held on for a count of four, moved the peg one hole to the left, and let it go. It rocked once and was still. Nobody said a word. Mara, sharpening her blade at the far end of the bench, studied the whetstone with great attention.

"Four," Renn said quietly, and wrote it in the margin. He did not write what it was a count of.

Kael drafted the reply on the back leaf of the ledger, in his own good hand, and read it to her. It was a fair draft. It said that the auditor would certify to the Council that the Lower Reach had been lit for three years by a tap on the Vault's trunk line, and that House Thorne stood ready to make the line safe.

"Stands ready," Sol said. "That's Thorne's phrase. You're handing it back to him."

"It's true that they say it."

"Then say that they said it. Not that it's so." She was thinking aloud, and the thought had a shape she liked. "He asked you to vouch. Don't vouch. Report. Tell the Council what the house offered, in whose words, on what date, in front of a clerk. In that chamber, on the record, where he can't turn it into a favor he may withdraw." She heard her voice speeding and slowed it. "A man who offers can take it back. A man who has been heard to offer has only two choices left, keep it or break it, and both are public."

Kael was already writing. He stopped, read it under his breath, and looked at her over the lamp. "That's better than mine."

"It's yours with a lock on it."

"I know," he said. "That's what I mean."

They put it in plain words. *To Lord Malrik Thorne. The auditor will state to the Council what he has verified: that for three years the Lower Reach was lit by a tap on the Vault's trunk line, and that House Thorne kept the meter. He will also state that the house has offered the order for shutting the tap safely. He will not certify good faith or stewardship, which are findings he has not made. Isolde Vane joins this reply. We ask that the order be lodged under seal with the Council surveyor before the box is opened, and that it not be held against anyone's answer.*

"Witness," Kael said, and Mara set down her whetstone and came to the bench. Sol dictated her assent aloud, and Mara wrote beneath Kael's line in a square soldier's hand: *Isolde Vane assents by her word, hands unfit to sign. Mara, warden, twenty years' service, witnessing.* Sol had not asked for the last clause. She thought it was meant kindly, and found she did not mind.

The reply went out in a runner's pouch, sealed in Ashworth wax, an hour before the sixth bell. Kael watched the boy go up the gallery and did not say what he was thinking, and then did, because he had promised himself he would. "I'm afraid we've just told him no in a way he can't argue with."

"That was the idea."

"I know. I'm afraid of it anyway."

"Good," said Sol. "So am I."

Renn took the lamp and the glass to the near edge of the gallery at the change of guard, as he had said he would, and Sol went with him on the drop, with Joren on the rope behind. She could not have said why she wanted to see for herself. The gallery lay black and flat, the blue seam sleeping along its far wall. At its end the plate held the Council's chain in a long straight line, ten pins, the plumb at the tail. Renn set the glass to his eye and began to count under his breath, in tens, as he had every shift.

He reached the tenth pin. He stopped. He went back to the ninth and counted again, and she saw his lips move over one number twice.

"A hundred," he said. "A hundred, and..." He lowered the glass. His voice had gone very small. "A hundred and one."

She took the glass from him in both wrapped hands and held it badly and looked. Between the ninth pin and the tenth, where the links were dull iron and rust, lay a single link that was not. It was bright copper, untouched by the wet, and in its curve, small as a grain of rice, was a knot of three strands.

"I counted a hundred at the last change of guard," said Renn. "That was two hours ago. We were sitting forty feet above it, writing a letter."

"An ounce and a half," Joren said, who had come to look and had judged it by eye, as a man does who has carried iron. "No more. It won't move the cycle."

"It moves nothing," Renn said. "It only says that it could."

"The hatch seals are whole." Joren's voice was flat. He had checked them himself, twice, since dusk. "Council wax and ours. Nobody has come down that stair since Renn."

"Then nobody came down the stair." Sol handed back the glass. "Which means there is another way into this place."

Renn did not answer at once. He was watching the link. "And it isn't on any survey I have," he said. "And Thorne knows where it is."

Across the whole black length of the gallery, over a buried line that counted ten, the copper lay on the Council's iron, bright and unhurried.
