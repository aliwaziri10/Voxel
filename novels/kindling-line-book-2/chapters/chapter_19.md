The lower cliffs smelled of wet iron and old rot. Kael had been climbing since before dawn, his rope anchored to a rusted ring bolted into limestone that had not felt a ward's weight in forty years. The Thorne seal on the access hatch — three thorns bound by a chain, pressed into wax the color of dried blood — had been intact when he pried it open. That meant something. It meant no one had been here since the Accord froze the ward trade, and it meant Thorne had remembered to lock the door behind them when they walked away.

He descended into the shaft with a glow-stone cupped in his palm. The air grew colder with every meter. Ward-shafts were not built for comfort. They were built to channel raw current from the Deep Vault up through the Reach's bones, and the lower cliffs had taken the heaviest flow. The stone walls sweated condensation that tasted of copper. His boots slipped on algae-slick rungs. At thirty meters down, the shaft opened into a junction chamber where three feeder lines converged on a single distribution node. The node should have been humming. It was silent.

Kael set his pack down on a ledge of fused slag. He withdrew his auditor's kit: a current-meter calibrated to the Accord's new standard, a set of ceramic probes, a notebook bound in oilcloth. The meter's needle did not move when he touched the probes to the node's primary contact. Zero flow. Zero potential. The ward was dead, and it had been dead long enough for the mineral buildup to crust over the contacts like white frost.

He recorded the readings. He photographed the corrosion with a flash-lamp and a plate camera. He measured the gap where the ward-stone had once seated — a hollow cylinder of blackened bronze, its inner surface pitted by decades of ungrounded discharge. The failure was structural. The node's mounting brackets had sheared away from the living rock, probably during the last major tremor two winters past. No one had checked. No one had been assigned to check. The ward trade's dismantlement had left a vacuum of responsibility, and Thorne had held the contract on this line when the freeze came down.

Kael's fingers brushed the Thorne seal on the hatch above. Three thorns. A chain. He had seen that seal before, pressed into the margin of a ward deed in the Ashworth archives. Sol had shown it to him three weeks ago, spread across her desk in the attic room where she worked the Accord's reclamation ledgers. "This is what we're looking for," she had said. "Every Thorne-sealed document from the old trade. If they're petitioning for reform, their paper trail is the lever."

He had not told her he was coming here. He had told her he would be in the auditor's office all day, cross-referencing the compensation claims from the upper districts. The lie by omission sat in his chest like a stone. He had promised himself after their confrontation in the garden — after she had said *stop deciding what I don't need to know* — that he would stop. He had not promised he would succeed.

The current-meter's needle twitched when he tested the secondary feeder. A ghost current, faint and erratic, bleeding from a hairline fracture in the line's insulation. Not enough to sustain a ward. Enough to kill a man who touched the wrong conductor at the wrong moment. Kael noted the reading. He sealed the probe contacts with wax. He climbed back up the shaft with his shoulders tight and his breath rasping in the cold.

The hatch emerged onto a ledge overlooking the mining district. Below him, the Thorne-held wards glimmered in the late morning light — not the living wards of the upper houses, but the old industrial arrays that had powered the deep drifts and the pump stations. Smoke rose from the ventilator stacks. A bell clanged somewhere in the warren of tunnels, the shift-change signal.

He was rolling his rope when the foreman found him. A broad-shouldered woman with a gray streak in her black hair and a Thorne badge pinned to her leather apron. She did not ask what he was doing. She read his auditor's sigil on his coat and her jaw tightened.

"Ward-line seven," she said. "You've been in the node chamber."

"Structural failure," Kael said. "Mounting brackets sheared. Zero flow. Ghost current on the secondary feeder. I'm filing a hazard notice with the Accord compensation board."

The foreman's eyes narrowed. "That line's been dead since the freeze. My crew doesn't work it. We work the pump station on line nine."

"Line nine draws from the same junction," Kael said. "If the ghost current arcs — "

"We know the risks," she cut in. "We've been managing them for three years. Thorne manages them. The Accord sends inspectors who write reports and leave."

"This isn't an inspection," Kael said. "This is an audit. The difference matters."

"Does it," she said. "Because from where I stand, it looks like another Ashworth deciding what Thorne gets to keep."

She turned and walked back toward the tunnel mouth. Kael watched her go. The wind off the Reach carried the smell of coal dust and sweat. He pulled his notebook from his pack and wrote the hazard notice by hand, his script precise despite the cold numbing his fingers. *Ward-line seven, Thorne-held, lower cliffs junction chamber. Structural failure of distribution node. Zero operational capacity. Residual ghost current presents lethal hazard to personnel. Recommend immediate decommission and compensation review for affected ward-contractors.*

He signed it with his auditor's seal. He dated it 27 Sunspire. He would file it at the Accord hall before the afternoon session. He would not tell Sol until after.

***

Sol found the report on the auditor's docket when she went to the Accord hall to pull the reclamation ledgers for the eastern district. The clerk — a young man with ink-stained fingertips and a nervous habit of tapping his pen against his teeth — slid the folio across the counter without meeting her eyes.

"Filed twenty minutes ago," he said. "Lead auditor's signature. Hazard notice, ward-line seven, Thorne holdings."

She took the folio. Her fingers knew the weight of Kael's handwriting before her eyes confirmed it. The precise script. The measured language. The date: today. The site: lower cliffs junction chamber.

She had asked him at breakfast where he would be. *The auditor's office,* he had said. *Cross-referencing compensation claims. Long day at the desk.* She had nodded and poured his tea and kissed his cheek and watched him walk out the door with his briefcase and his coat collar turned up against the wind.

The clerk coughed. "Ma'am? The ledgers for the eastern district — "

"Thank you," Sol said. She closed the folio. She did not ask for the ledgers. She walked out of the Accord hall into the bright noise of the market square, where merchants shouted over the clatter of cart wheels and the smell of roasting chestnuts mingled with the ever-present dust of the Reach.

She stood beneath the clock tower and read the report again. *Structural failure. Zero operational capacity. Residual ghost current presents lethal hazard.* The language was clean. Professional. It told her nothing about how he had gotten there, or why he had gone alone, or what he had seen when he pried open that Thorne-sealed hatch.

A boy ran past her chasing a paper kite. A woman laughed at something her companion said. The Reach went on living while the wards beneath it rotted.

Sol folded the report and put it in her coat pocket. Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs until the trembling stopped.

***

The compensation board met in a chamber beneath the Accord hall, its walls lined with ledgers bound in red leather. Kael sat at the long table with the other auditors — three men and a woman from House Vellin, their faces drawn from weeks of claims review. The board's chair, an elderly magistrate named Corven who had overseen the ward trade's dissolution, rapped his gavel on the sounding block.

"Hazard notice, ward-line seven," Corven said. "Lead auditor Ashworth presenting."

Kael stood. He placed his folio on the reading stand. "Structural failure at the lower cliffs junction chamber. The distribution node's mounting brackets sheared from the living rock, likely during the tremor of Year One. The ward has been non-operational since the freeze. Ghost current detected on the secondary feeder at point-three milliamperes — sufficient to cause cardiac arrest in an ungrounded adult. I recommend immediate decommission and full compensation for any ward-contractors exposed."

"Any exposed?" Corven asked. "You have names?"

"The Thorne foreman on site indicated her crew works line nine, which shares the junction. Ghost current could arc across the common ground. I've requested Thorne's current roster for both lines."

A murmur ran through the board. The Vellin auditor, a sharp-featured man named Darrow, leaned forward. "Thorne's roster. You've asked Thorne for their ward-contractor records?"

"I've filed the request through proper channels."

"Through Thorne's channels."

"Through the Accord's channels. Thorne administers the contracts. The Accord holds the compensation fund."

Darrow's mouth thinned. "And if Thorne delays? If they claim the roster is proprietary? If they say the hazard is theoretical and their workers are safe?"

"Then the board subpoenas the records," Corven said. "Auditor Ashworth, your notice is accepted. The compensation fund will be notified. Auditor Darrow, you'll handle the subpoena if Thorne does not comply within three business days."

Darrow nodded once, sharp. Kael sat down. The board moved to the next item: a claim from a widow in the upper district whose husband had died maintaining a ward that the Accord had declared safe six months prior. The file was thick with medical reports and witness statements. Kael read the summary and felt the familiar hollow place in his chest where the cost-transfer lived — the mechanic that had bound him to Sol since the night the Vault opened, the thing that made every injury near her a wound in his own body, every injury near him a wound in hers. The Accord's lawyers called it *residual sympathetic resonance*. The ward-takers called it the price.

He did not think about Sol sitting in the market square with his report in her pocket. He did not think about the promise he had made in the garden. He listened to the widow's advocate argue for a doubled payout, and he voted with the majority to deny it, and he told himself he was doing the work.

***

Sol returned to the attic room as the afternoon light slanted gold across the floorboards. The reclamation ledgers waited on her desk, untouched. She sat in the chair Kael had built for her — a gift from their first winter together, the wood planed smooth and oiled until it smelled of pine and time — and she stared at the folded report in her hand.

She had seen the pattern. She had named it. *Stop deciding what I don't need to know.* He had admitted it without denial or excuse. He had looked at her with his face open and his hands loose at his sides and he had said, *You're right. I'm sorry. I'll do better.*

And today he had gone into a Thorne-held ward shaft alone and filed a hazard notice before telling her he had left the house.

The pattern was not the lying. The pattern was the deciding. He decided she didn't need to know because he decided the risk was his to carry. He decided the work was his to do. He decided, every time, that his protection was more important than her partnership.

She unfolded the report again. She read the clinical language. *Zero operational capacity. Residual ghost current presents lethal hazard.* She thought of the foreman with the gray streak in her hair and the Thorne badge on her apron. *We've been managing them for three years. Thorne manages them.*

Thorne managed them. Thorne had held the contract when the freeze came. Thorne had sealed the hatch and walked away and left the node to rot. And now Thorne wanted the Accord to hand them the keys to the Vault's infrastructure in the name of reform.

*A house that has already paid the cost has earned the right to control who pays it next.*

The words rose unbidden in her memory — not spoken by Thorne, not yet, but spoken by Kael three nights ago when he described Thorne's petition to the Accord council. He had quoted their representative, a woman named Mara Vex who carried herself like a blade in a sheath. *That's their argument,* Kael had said. *They paid in blood. Three generations of wardens. They say the Accord owes them the administration.*

Sol had asked, *And what do you say?*

He had said, *I say the Accord owes everyone the truth. I say we audit every claim. I say we don't let grief write the law.*

He had not said *I'll tell you when I go into the shafts.* He had not said *I'll take you with me.*

She refolded the report. She opened the top ledger. The first page showed a compensation claim from the lower cliffs mining district — a ward-contracted miner named Joren Vex, injured by ghost current from a decommissioned line three weeks past. The claim was pending. The medical report listed cardiac arrhythmia, nerve damage in the left arm, probable permanent disability. The estimated payout: twelve thousand crowns from the Accord fund.

Joren Vex. The same surname as Mara Vex. Sol's fingers tightened on the ledger's edge.

She pulled the next file. Another miner. Another ghost current injury. Another pending claim. The pattern extended backward through the ledger: seven claims in the last eighteen months, all from Thorne-held lines in the lower cliffs, all approved or pending, all paid from the Accord fund.

Thorne managed the lines. Thorne collected the compensation. Thorne petitioned for the Vault.

She stood and went to the window. The Reach spread below her — the vertical city carved into cliff faces, the rope bridges swaying in the wind, the ward-lights glowing in the gathering dusk. Somewhere in the lower cliffs, a miner named Joren Vex lay in a company infirmary with his heart beating wrong and his arm useless. The Accord would pay. Thorne would manage. And Kael would go into the shafts alone and write reports that told the truth without telling her.

The clock tower struck five. The sound vibrated through the stone floor.

Sol picked up her pen. She opened a fresh page in her working notebook — not the Accord ledger, her own. She wrote: *Ward-line seven hazard notice filed 27 Sunspire. Solo audit by K. Thorne-sealed hatch. Ghost current on secondary feeder. Compensation claims pattern: seven injuries in 18 months, all Thorne lines, all lower cliffs. Joren Vex — possible relation to Mara Vex?*

She underlined the last line twice. She capped her pen. She heard footsteps on the stairs below — heavy, familiar, pausing at the landing.

The door opened. Kael entered with his coat dusted in limestone powder and his eyes finding hers immediately. He did not smile. He did not pretend.

"You found the report," he said.

"I found the report," she said.

"I was going to tell you at dinner."

"When were you going to tell me about the seven compensation claims?"

He set his briefcase down. He removed his gloves. His fingers were raw at the tips. "I didn't know about seven. I knew about the one from three weeks ago. The foreman mentioned it. Joren Vex."

"And the others?"

"I'll pull the files tomorrow. Cross-reference the medical reports against the ward-line maintenance logs. If Thorne's been letting ghost current fester across multiple lines — "

"Kael." Her voice was quiet. "That's not what I asked."

He stopped. His hands stilled on his coat buttons. The lamplight caught the exhaustion carved into the lines around his eyes, the stubble dark along his jaw. He looked like a man who had climbed down into the dark and come back carrying something he couldn't set down.

"What did you ask?" he said.

"I asked when you were going to tell me. Not what you're going to do about it. Not how you're going to fix it. When you were going to tell me."

He was silent for a long moment. The wind rattled the window. Somewhere in the Reach, a ward-light flickered and steadied.

"I wasn't," he said. "I wasn't going to tell you about the claims. I was going to handle them. I was going to make sure the compensation fund paid, and I was going to make sure Thorne answered for the maintenance gaps, and I was going to put it in the audit summary for the council so you'd see it there with everything else. Clean. Complete. After."

"After what?"

"After it was done. After the danger was documented and the response was moving. After you didn't have to worry about it."

She nodded once. "That's the pattern. You decide what I don't need to worry about. You decide when I get to know. You decide that your silence is protection and my ignorance is safety."

"I don't think your ignorance is safety."

"Then what is it?"

He exhaled. The sound dragged rough from his throat. "It's the only way I know to keep you whole. The cost-transfer — every time you go into a shaft, every time you touch a live ward, every time you stand in the path of something that could break you — I feel it. Here." He pressed his hand flat against his sternum. "I feel it like a second heartbeat. And I know you feel mine. So I decide. I decide to go alone. I decide not to tell you until after. Because if you know before, you come with me. And if you come with me, the cost doubles."

"That's not your decision to make."

"It's the only decision I have."

"No." She stepped closer. The floorboards creaked beneath her weight. "The decision you have is whether you trust me to carry my half. Whether you trust us to carry it together. You keep deciding for both of us, and you call it love, and it's not. It's fear. And fear is not the same thing."

His face shifted. Something cracked in the set of his jaw. "I know."

"Do you? Because you said you'd do better. You said that in the garden. And today you went into a Thorne shaft alone and filed a report before you came home."

"I know."

"Then why?"

"Because the foreman was right. Thorne manages their lines. They've been managing them for three years. Seven injuries in eighteen months — that's not bad luck. That's negligence. Or it's deliberate. And if it's deliberate, then every minute I spend explaining is a minute they have to hide the evidence. So I went alone. I moved fast. I filed the notice. I'll pull the files tomorrow. I'll bring you everything. But I needed the notice on the record today."

"Needed. Decided. You."

"I'm not saying it's right. I'm saying it's what I did."

She studied him. The limestone dust on his coat. The raw fingertips. The exhaustion and the fear and the stubborn certainty that he could carry the weight if he just moved fast enough, worked hard enough, decided enough.

"You're going to break," she said. "Not the wards. Not the Accord. You. You're going to break yourself trying to carry everything so I don't have to."

"I already broke once. So did you. We put each other back together."

"Then stop breaking me by shutting me out."

The words hung between them. The clock tower struck six. The ward-lights below flared brighter as the dusk deepened.

Kael's hand rose. He stopped it inches from her face. His fingers trembled.

"I'll bring you the files tomorrow," he said. "All of them. The compensation claims. The maintenance logs. The Thorne roster request. Everything. We'll go through them together. You'll see what I see. You'll decide what we do next."

"And the next shaft?"

He lowered his hand. "I don't know. I'll try. That's all I can say. I'll try."

She wanted to believe him. She wanted to believe that naming the pattern had changed it, that the admission in the garden had been a turning point and not just a waypoint. She wanted to believe that the man who had held her through the night the Vault opened, who had carried her cost and let her carry his, who had said *I'm sorry* without qualification — that this man could learn to stop deciding.

But the report in her pocket said otherwise. The seven claims in the ledger said otherwise. The Thorne seal on the hatch, three thorns bound by a chain, said otherwise.

"Tomorrow," she said. "We'll start tomorrow."

He nodded. He turned to hang his coat. She watched the line of his shoulders, the way his spine straightened against the weight he carried. The attic room settled around them, familiar and fragile, full of ledgers and ledger-light and the sound of two people breathing in the same space while the Reach turned beneath them.

Outside, the wind shifted. It carried the smell of iron and coal and something older, rising from the Deep Vault through miles of stone — the scent of power waiting in the dark, patient and indifferent, while the houses above fought over who held the keys.
