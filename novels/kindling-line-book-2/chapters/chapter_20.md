Renn was in the chamber before them, standing where a surveyor stands when he wants nobody to ask him to move: beside the clerk's table, one hand flat on a brass case no longer than a forearm. Thorne's wax closed both ends. Across the seam ran a strip of his own, with his commission pressed into it.

"Eighth bell this morning," he said, low, before Kael could ask. "Lord Malrik brought it up the Reach on foot, with the grandson carrying nothing and looking as though he wished he were. I signed for it in front of the Chancellor's clerk. I have not opened it, and I am told I may not read it until the Council says." His eyes went to Sol's wrapped hands and then, carefully, to her face. "The link is still on the plate. Nobody wished to be the hand that changed the weight."

"The note?" Kael said.

"With the Chancellor's clerk since noon yesterday, under my seal. Joren and I walked the sublevel twice more after. No door, no drain, nothing that isn't on the survey." Renn's mouth thinned. "So the survey is wrong. I would like very much to know by whom."

Kael and Sol had settled that at the trestle, over the lamp. The link would go to the Chancellor sealed and stay out of the open chamber, because a thing said aloud in front of Thorne was answered before anyone had understood it. It still felt new, to decide a thing in that order.

The chamber was full to the top tier. Eight houses sat behind their signatures, and the four who had not signed sat with the look of people who had begun to count. Lord Malrik came in on his grandson's arm, deep grey, the plain silver clasp at his throat, and the small upright woman with the copper clasp took her place behind the Thorne bench as if the spot had been kept warm. Aldous Thorne was pale. He lowered his grandfather into the seat with more care than the old man wanted, and was looked at for it.

The Chancellor began with paper, not the box.

"Before the instrument is opened, the record will hear what House Thorne filed with this office on the evening of the twenty-seventh."

The senior clerk read it standing, a bald grey man with ink to the wrist. The house had received the auditor's reply. The house had offered the order for shutting the tap safely, and did not withdraw the offer. The order, in the hand of the house's master smith, had been lodged that morning with the Council surveyor under seal, to be read by him alone until the Council decided otherwise. The house did not ask to be called a steward. It asked to be read.

Nobody on the tiers stirred. Kael had prepared for a refusal, and for a counter-offer, and for a sly half-acceptance that would leave the lock open at one corner. He had not prepared for a man to hand over the one page that gave him leverage, unasked, in public.

"He's given up the page," Sol said, so low the words hardly disturbed the air. "Why would he give up the page?"

"Because he has found a better thing to hold."

She turned her head and looked at him. He had not softened it, and she seemed to approve.

The box came in on two clerks' arms from the strongroom where it had been kept under guard: lead-lined oak, bound in iron, one band sprung. They set it on the table under the tall window, where the light was best. The senior clerk bent over it with a glass and took his time, and the chamber let him.

"The Council surveyor's strip is whole," he said at last. "His commission, the hour, the date. Cut words, *Twenty-three Sunspire. First bell. Unbroken.* Beside it the wax of House Thorne, whole: three strands in a knot, a drop, a flame and a feather at each terminus, near-black, in violet and copper. Two later layers lie over the first. They were laid over it and not through it, in the manner of the old trade for an instrument kept in damp." He straightened. "The Council's clerks find both seals whole."

"Enter it," said the Chancellor. Her voice was thin and carried everywhere. "Open it."

The junior clerk cut Renn's strip with a bone knife, then the Thorne wax, and lifted the lid. Inside the lead, on dry felt, lay a vellum wrapper tied with cord of violet and copper, lettered on its face: *Ward Deed of Succession*. Under it lay eleven leaves of the instrument and six more of a Schedule. At the foot of the last leaf of the instrument, in near-black wax, the knot again.

Kael had leave to come to the table. He looked at Sol first. "Read it with me."

She was already moving.

The recital was long and aggrieved: three generations of wards, the names of the dead, the cost borne. Kael let it pass. It was true, and it was there to be read past. Then the senior clerk came to the operative clauses.

"Seven. All contracts of ward service named in the Schedule, and all that follow them upon any line, pass upon ratification into the custody of the house administering reform, to be kept, assigned or dissolved at its discretion."

Kael had read a hundred clauses like it in the old ledgers. It moved a person the way a ledger moves a sack.

"Twelve. In any question touching this instrument or the contracts it governs, the reading of the house administering reform is final, and no body shall sit in review of it."

The Accord's article on transfer let a contract change hands only with its holder's consent or the sitting auditor's certification. Clause twelve turned that article into a matter for the administering house's own reading. He held the thought and let the clerk go on.

"Nineteen. This instrument takes effect upon its opening by the clerks of the Council in sight of the Council entire, its own seal being whole. If within thirty days of that opening the Council has not set it aside by the voices of nine houses of twelve, it shall be held ratified, and the house whose seal closes it shall administer."

*Its own seal being whole.* The phrase went through him like a cold key. He knew it. It stood in the last article of the Accord's dissolution schedule, which kept alive any instrument of succession sealed under the old trade, on that one condition, so that no house should lose a lawful inheritance to the Accord's haste. The framers had written the safeguard with a decent purpose. Somebody had read it before the Accord ever sat, and had drafted this to its letter.

"Sol." It came out low and fast, because he had promised himself he would say it before he had finished thinking it. "It's a seizure. Seven takes the contracts, twelve takes the last word, and nineteen makes them live. The Accord preserves any old succession deed that's opened before the Council with its seal whole. We found it. We guarded it. We carried it into this room, and I called that being careful."

For five days he had thought of the box as evidence. Evidence could be counted, sealed, opened before witnesses so that no one could say it had been doctored. It was the most careful thing he had done all month. It was also precisely what clause nineteen asked for.

Sol did not answer him at once. She had her chin almost on his shoulder, reading the first leaf of the Schedule where it lay under the glass. "Nine of twelve to set it aside," she said. "Eight of twelve to pass the petition. It's built to be easier to keep than to stop." She moved a wrapped finger along the margin without touching it. "Read me the heads."

He read them. Eleven houses, by line. Under the Lower Spine two names stood side by side, Thorne and Vane, as they had in her mother's contract, and a single hand had ruled a line beneath them and added them into one sum.

"Four houses on this Schedule did not sign the petition," Sol said. Her voice was perfectly even. "Ours is one. Yours is another. Nobody asked either of us."

The leaf stuck to the next. Kael's hand went out and stopped. "The corner?"

"The corner."

He held it flat with two fingers, and she turned the leaf with thumb and forefinger. It took her two tries. Neither of them said anything about it.

"Auditor," said the Chancellor. "The instrument requires your certification, if the Accord's article is to be observed. Have you a finding?"

"I have a reading, Chancellor, and it's an hour old." He straightened. "I will not certify this instrument in this chamber, on this morning's reading, with a house's petition lying beside it. I ask leave to read every leaf with Isolde Vane, under a clerk's eye, and to give a written finding at the public sitting." He drew a breath. "And I ask that the record show the first useful observation came from her. The Schedule names four houses that did not sign. Hers is one of them. That is Isolde Vane's, not mine."

"Leave is granted, and entered as she found it," the Chancellor said. She let a moment pass. "The Council also notes what clause nineteen says of the clock. It runs from the ninth bell of this morning. The Council does not stop a clock it did not set. The vote on the petition will be taken at the public sitting in the third week of Cinderveil, the last before the thirty days are spent."

Thirty days from the twenty-eighth of Sunspire ended on the twenty-eighth of Cinderveil. Kael set that beside Renn's sum for the chain, nine by the middle of Cinderveil, eight by the end, and the two clocks lay together in his head like rules on a table. Neither would wait for the other. Both ran out in the same week.

Malrik Thorne rose without help. "The house is content to be read, Chancellor." He turned his head toward the auditor's bench, and the dry courtesy of the turn was worse than a glare. "And obliged to the auditor for his diligence. The box could hardly have been in better hands."

"It has been in the Council's hands, my lord," Kael said, and heard how thin the answer was.

"Yes," said Malrik. "That was the hope."

Aldous Thorne had not stood with his grandfather. He was looking at the Schedule, at the Lower Spine leaf lying open under the glass, and the color had gone out of his face the way it goes out of a man who finds his own name in another man's handwriting. His lips shaped a sentence. Kael was too far off to catch more than its beginning: *I did not.*

Then the steward's fingers settled on the young man's sleeve, and the sentence ended where it was.
