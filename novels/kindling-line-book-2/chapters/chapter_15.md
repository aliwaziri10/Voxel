The lamps did not come back. What came instead was a violet glow, leaking through the shaded glass of hand-lanterns as four figures descended the short stair one step at a time, in no hurry at all.

Sol stood in the alcove with the box against her ribs and did not move. Forty paces of black water lay between her and the landing, and the ward-line ran through it white now instead of blue, swelling and sinking on a beat she did not recognize. Kael had put himself half a step in front of her. She let him keep the half step. She would not give him the rest.

Three of the four wore violet wool and carried copper-tipped staves. The fourth was a small, upright woman past sixty, her grey hair pinned flat, a polished copper clasp at her throat: three strands, knotted. Sol had last seen that clasp on a hooded watcher in a ropewalk doorway. The woman stopped on the step above Varel, who had drawn his knees to his chest, and looked down at him with an expression close to tenderness.

"Clerk," she said. "You are cold. They should have left you the blanket."

Varel made a sound that was not a word. Renn, herded two steps down the stair by a retainer's stave, stood flat against the wall with his commission stamp clenched in his fist.

"I am the steward of House Thorne's works in the Lower Reach," the woman said, and her voice crossed the water without effort. "You do not need my name, and it would not help you. Auditor Ashworth. Isolde Vane. I will say this once, plainly, before anyone is hurt. The box you are holding is not a prize. It is a weight."

"Explain that," Kael said. His voice was even.

"You are standing in a room that has been balanced for three years. The pedestal is a scale, and the line reads the scale, and the scale has read the same weight every night since the Accord cut the Vault off from the houses that paid for it." She raised one hand and the lantern light ran down her sleeve. "Listen to the water."

Sol was already listening. She had been listening since the lamps died, with the part of her that her mother had trained before her hands, and what she heard was a note going flat and then sharp, the swell arriving early, then earlier. Eleven breaths had become nine. Now it was eight.

"Set it back on the plate and the line settles," said the steward. "Carry it up the stair and the tap surges into the Lower Reach. Ansa's tar-shop, the splicing halls, the tenements. Lights out above, water below, and the Reach will ask who carried it." She let that stand. "My house served as wards for three generations. We paid what the old trade demanded, in bodies, in years, in children who did not come home. We know what the cost is and who has been made to bear it. My lord said it to the Great Hall and you heard pride. It was an accounting. A house that has already paid the cost has earned the right to control who pays it next."

Nobody answered. In the stone the ward-line climbed a semitone.

Sol thought of her mother's hands unfolding a contract in a records room, and of what three generations of a family paying in bodies might do to what that family believed it was owed. The grievance was not false. That was the part that was hard to hear.

"I hear you," Kael said, "and I won't answer you alone." He did not turn his head. "Sol. Say what you want. I'll follow it, and I want it said aloud."

She nearly laughed. Even here. "Renn," she called. "Your copies."

Renn cleared his throat twice. "Every page of the ledger is copied and stamped," he said, and his voice cracked and steadied. "The first sheaf went up the stair at noon, and Warden Mara counted every page at the hatch and signed for it. The second went at dusk. Nothing in this room is the only copy of anything. Steward."

The steward inclined her head to him as to a colleague. It was the worst thing she had done yet.

Sol turned her back on the stair and spoke low. "We don't put it back, and we don't carry it out either. Not yet. The line reads weight. It doesn't read what the weight is." She looked at the pedestal and the clean pale ring on its top where the box had sat. "If the plate believes the box is still there, the tap stays balanced and the line sleeps. She's told us everything except the number."

"Twelve," said Varel.

Everyone looked at the stair. He had lifted his head off his knees, and his eyes went from the steward to Sol and did not settle, and then, terribly, they did. "Twelve of their pounds. They set an iron ingot on the plate twice, when they changed the wax on the lid. I wrote it down. I can see the page." His voice was thin and very clear. "It was twelve."

The steward said nothing, and Sol knew the number was right.

"Twelve pounds of iron," Sol said, "across forty paces of water, with the swell coming every eight breaths." She raised her voice. "Renn. What does a Council surveyor carry?"

Renn stared. Then his face changed, the look of a man who has found his trade in a room that had no use for it. "A chain," he said. "A hundred links and ten pins and a plumb. Twelve pounds, near enough. I carried it down that stair and felt every ounce." He was already unslinging it. "Near enough is the trouble."

"It's what we have," Kael said. "The rope."

The line Kael had carried over when he followed her still ran slack from the iron ring at the foot of the stair to the arch where he had tied it off. Renn knotted the chain into a loop at the near end with fingers that shook and then did not. The steward watched him do it. She did not stop him, and Sol understood why with a small cold turn of the stomach: her house needed the plate balanced as badly as they did.

"The drop is four breaths now," Sol said. "I call it. We pull on the drop and we let go on the swell. Nobody's hands stay on wet rope when the line rises."

"Yours first, or mine?"

"Both. Side by side. You have the weight, I have the count."

They knelt at the arch with the rope between them, and Sol set her palms on it and closed her eyes. The line breathed under the black water. Down, and down, and down, and she felt the last of the drop like a stair under her foot. "Now."

They pulled. The chain slid off the landing and hissed over the surface in a long grey arc, and at the edge of her hearing the note began to climb. "Off." Their hands opened. The chain lay a third of the way across, and the seam beneath it brightened, and a filament of charge crawled up the wall and vanished.

"Down. Now." Pull. "Off." Pull. Off. The chain came on like a slow animal, and Renn at the far end counted under his breath in a voice that had lost all its dryness, and Kael's breathing beside her was steady as a lock turning.

On the fifth pull, the seam did not wait.

It rose on the seventh breath, not the eighth, and the swell came up the wet rope faster than any voice could have called it. Sol's palms were flat on the rope. The charge reached her before it reached Kael, because his hands were a length farther back on rope that had stayed dry. It did not feel like fire. It was cold, white and enormous, a frost that arrived in her wrists and burned outward, and she heard herself make a small flat sound as her fingers locked.

"Let go," Kael said. "Sol. Let go."

She could not. The rope was the only thing between the chain and the water, and her hands had decided for her. Then the swell fell, all at once, and her fingers opened by themselves, and she was on her knees with her palms turned up in the witch-light. The skin was white, then red, then rising in long shining welts from the heel of each hand to the root of every finger.

Kael did not touch her. He took the rope from the place where her hands had lain and looked at her, and this time she saw what it cost him to ask instead of doing.

"Can I finish it?"

"Yes." It came out as a breath. "I'll call it. I don't need hands to count."

He hauled the last of the rope alone, on her word, drop by drop, and the chain came up onto the dry floor of the alcove in a wet grey coil. Together they lifted it, or Kael lifted it and Sol guided him, her hands held out from her body as if they belonged to someone else. Coil by coil it went onto the pedestal, over the pale ring, and Kael pressed the last link flat.

For a long moment the water did nothing. Then the seam dimmed by degrees. The swell that had come at seven, at eight, came at nine, then ten. It stopped at ten, and held.

"Ten," said Sol, staring at the line. "Not eleven. Near enough." Her voice shook. "It's gone back to sleep. It hasn't decided who owes it. Not yet."

Kael knelt in front of her. He tore a strip from the hem of his shirt, and then another, and looked up. "I'd like to wrap them."

"Do it."

He did it slowly, and she watched his face and not her hands. He did not say he had warned her. He said, very quietly, "I hate that it was you," and she said, "I know. Thank you for saying it," and neither of them said anything else.

They crossed on the drop, at ten. Kael carried the box against his chest, and Sol walked beside him counting for them both, and this time the black water let them through. At the foot of the stair the three retainers had swung their staves across the way up. The steward stood behind them with the lantern in her hand.

"You have kept my house's lamps burning, Auditor," she said. "I am grateful."

"We did it for the households," Sol said.

"So did we." The steward's eyes rested on the box. "For three years, so did we."

The tallest retainer took a step forward, staff angled. Kael did not look at him. He looked at Sol, and she nodded once, and they filled their lungs together.

"Joren!"

"Mara!"

Either voice would have brought the wardens; that was the order Kael had given at the hatch, with Sol beside him so they would hear it in both. Neither of them had used it until now. A fight on the landing of a flooded gallery would have put someone in the water, and they had chosen to make the call together or not at all. Boots hit the spiral above almost before the echo did, many of them and quick, and a hard voice called down the stair: "Coming, Auditor!"

The steward raised two fingers. The staves lowered, and the retainers drew back against the wall like a tide going out.

"The hearing is on the twenty-fourth," she said mildly, as they passed her. "In front of the Council. It will be decided there which of us the Reach can afford."

Varel rose from the bottom step and fell in behind them without a word. He walked as if the stair might be taken away.

They came up into the grey of the ropewalk gallery as the first bell began. Joren's scarred hand took the box from Kael, then gave it back, and Mara took one look at Sol's bound hands and said nothing and put her own cloak around her shoulders. Renn set the box on the low bench under the high window. From his satchel he took a strip of Council wax and warmed it, and laid it across the seam of the lid beside the near-black wax that lay there unbroken. The strip took his commission seal. Beside it he cut the hour and the date with the point of his stylus, and his hand did not shake at all.

"Twenty-three Sunspire," he said. "First bell. Unbroken."

Through the high window the Lower Reach was waking under lamps that had never gone out. Forty households, fifty. Every one of them had lived three years by the light of a debt no one had told them they owed. Sol stood with her ruined hands held out from her sides and looked down at Ansa's window over the tar-shop, where a lamp burned steady and yellow.

"What's it for?" Varel said behind her. He was not looking at the box. He was looking at the lamps. "All that charge. Three years. What was it for?"

Nobody answered him. The box lay on the bench between them, sealed, stamped, twelve pounds near enough. It was hers to carry into the Council chamber and nobody's to open before then. And Sol, who had crossed forty paces of black water to bring it out, did not know what it had cost the Reach to keep it there.
