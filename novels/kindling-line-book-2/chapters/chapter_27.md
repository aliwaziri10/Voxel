The healer had put Mara in the back room, where the window looked onto a wall and the wall kept off the noise of the stair. Sol had sat beside the cot since the small hours, in her coat, her gloved hands laid one over the other on her knee.

Mara's eyes were open. That was the part Sol could not get past. They rested on the ceiling beams and did not follow the lamp, or the healer, or the cup held to her lips, and the water ran back out of the corner of her mouth. Her breath came thin and slow. When Sol laid a gloved hand over hers, the cold came up through the wool as if she had touched an anchor rail in winter.

"The line went through her as it went through the Auditor," the healer had said at midnight, in her flat, tired voice, "and she had less to spare. She breathes on her own. I will not promise you the morning, or the week, or that she comes back the woman who went down. I can promise she is not in pain."

The note had been waiting against the teapot when Sol went home to change her coat. She had read it standing up. *Gone to look at the boiling-house cellar. Mara at the yard gate. Back before noon, and I will tell you all of it.* Every word was true. He had told her all of it besides, on the healer's step at half past noon, with his wet boot stretched out before him and his face gone the color of tallow, every step of his reasoning in order, without once asking her to take his view of it. She had listened to the end. She had said nothing, because she had found there was nowhere to put her anger. Had he lied, it would have known where to go.

The stick came first, knocking along the boards of the passage. Then Kael, his left leg dragging half a step behind, one hand on the doorframe. He did not come in.

"May I?"

"Come in."

He came as far as the foot of the cot and stood there, and sat only when she nodded at the stool. He held a folded paper out before she had looked at him. "Bram brought it at first bell of the morning. I read it before I brought it to you. I wanted you to know I had."

The petitioner's clerk wrote a fair hand. *Owing to an incident in the yard behind the old ropewalk, in which an officer of the Accord and a warden of House Ashworth were injured, carriage of the table's book is deferred to the noon bell of the fifth.*

"It's true," Sol said.

"Yes. That is what I noticed first."

"They did not have to bend a word this time. You wrote them the sentence yourself." She said it without heat. It was worse than heat, and she watched it land. "I heard you yesterday, Kael. All of it. Every step held. It's the total I cannot sign."

"I know."

"Do not ask me what I want you to do about it."

"I was not going to."

He had been going to. She saw it in the way his eyes dropped to his own hands. She let it stand, because it was small beside Mara's breathing, and because he had stopped himself where she could see him stop.

"I will not tell you what you owe, or what to feel about it," she said. "That is yours, and you will carry it whether or not I say so. I will tell you what I am doing. At noon I am going to the yard. I will count the line from the arch, and I will not cross it. I would like you on my left, where I can see you. If you want to be there."

"I want to."

"Then when you see a thing, say it before I have said mine."

"Before," Kael said. It carried, she thought, a good deal more cost than it had any right to.

At her belt lay the small linen bundle the healer had pressed into her hand at dawn without a word: her knife, barred to her since the burn. It was the healer's way of saying the rope was hers again. Sol had not decided yet whether she was glad.

Ansa's boy caught them on the rope bridge with the slate held over his head like a lamp. Sol read it aloud with her finger under the chalk, because chalk was the only thing that morning nobody had softened. "Two hundred and fifty-one by the lamps of the fourth. None in the day. And nobody has signed the leaving paper either. A."

"Nobody signs, and nobody leaves," Kael said. "Nobody knows what *advise* means, so they stay where they stand."

"They named three and froze four hundred," Sol said. "It's cheap, and it keeps."

The planks were wet from the night's haze. At the first of them Kael stopped, one hand on the rope. "I would take your arm on the bridge, if you will give it. Not for the stair after. The stair is mine."

She had held out her elbow before he finished. His weight came onto it, more than he had meant her to feel, and she did not say so. Halfway across he spoke without turning his head.

"I have not asked what you did with the night."

"I sat with Mara. I counted her breath until I lost the number."

"Did it help?"

"No," Sol said. "It gave my hands a job."

The yard behind the ropewalk had been swept for the carter, which told her who had paid for the sweeping. Joren stood at the gate in Mara's place, the old scar across the back of his hand gone white with cold, and behind him a warden leaned on the timber laid across the cellar door.

"Nothing in, nothing out since the Auditor's stair, my lady," he said. "The scratching stopped within the hour. I lifted the bar once at first light and looked. The lamp over the iron door was still burning. I put the bar back."

The dray came at the noon bell, a flat cart under a tarpaulin, with two clerks in grey edged with violet walking beside it. One was the clerk of the table, small and dry, the notice already in his hand. He held it out to Joren and said nothing. Joren looked at Kael. Kael looked at Sol, and did not speak.

It was hers to say, then, and he had made it so by keeping quiet. She noted that and put it aside for later.

"The office cannot stop a petitioner carrying its own papers up its own stair," she said, so that Joren and the warden and the clerk would all hear her say it, and hear why. "Lift the bar. Nobody goes past the arch without us watching."

The clerks went down with a lantern and a hand-barrow. Kael took the twelve steps one at a time, the stick on the stone and his right hand on the wall. She neither slowed nor hurried, and he did not ask her to do either.

The room under the boiling house lay as he had described it, the length of a chapel, the floor under two fingers of black water and the thin blue seam running through it. Sol stopped on the last dry step, where he must have stopped, and let her head go a little to one side.

The seam brightened. It dimmed. She began to count under her breath, and Kael beside her held his tongue with visible effort.

Nine breaths. Then eleven. Then nine.

"That is what I got yesterday," he said quietly. "Nine, eleven, nine."

"And then you stopped counting."

"And then I stepped."

The clerks did not test the floor. They stood with the barrow at the edge of the water until the seam dimmed, crossed in six unhurried strides, and reached the iron door as the light came up behind their heels. It opened for them without a knock. Sol saw a slit of lamplight, the edge of a table, a stool pushed back and empty. Then it shut. They had not looked at the water once. Sol counted a second cycle, and a third, and on the third she heard what she had missed.

It was not one line. Twice in every round the seam dimmed, and one of the dimmings was shallower than the other, as if a second line lay under the first and beat against it, out of step. The clerks were crossing where both went dark together, and that happened rarely, and they had known when.

"Nine and eleven," she said, and Kael was already speaking.

"Laid end to end they make twenty. Halved, ten." He had said it before she had. She saw him hear that himself. "Renn's chain in the sublevel keeps a ten-breath cycle. Nine and eleven averaged is ten."

"You said it first."

"I said it when I saw it. I did not wait to see if you had."

It was not the whole of what she wanted, and it was a great deal more than she had had ten days ago. She let the moment sit between them like a coin set on a table and turned back to the water.

The clerks came out through the iron door with the table's book wrapped in oilcloth and a flat leather case beside it, and crossed back on the same dark, and lifted the barrow up the twelve steps between them. At the top the clerk of the table paused in front of Sol. He looked at her gloved hands, and at Kael's stick, and did not trouble to hide either look.

"Who taught your clerks to cross it?" she asked.

"The house keeps its own count, my lady."

He said it with courtesy. The dray took the book up the yard and out through the gate, and Joren, who could not stop it, watched it go with the scar on his hand gone whiter still.

"Two lines," Kael said. "One of them was never on Renn's survey."

"One of them is under the boiling house. The other," Sol said, "keeps the same ten as the chain that balances Thorne's tap under the ropewalk. Either the house drew both, or it learned the second from the first."

"Renn said he would read the fifth and sixth books by evening." Kael's hand tightened on the stick. "It has been a night and a morning. He has sent no strip."

Sol looked at the empty place in the yard where the dray had stood. "Then we find Renn before Thorne's clerks do," she said, "and we go together."
