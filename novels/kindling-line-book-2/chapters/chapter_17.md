There were more of them at the gate than there had been. Sol counted six retainers in violet along the stair cut into the western face, one at each landing, and she counted them the way her mother had taught her to count anything that might matter: once for the number, once for what it cost them to stand so still.

"When I came there were two," Kael said, low, beside her.

"Then Lord Malrik has been busy."

The gate itself was a plain arch of dark stone set into the cliff with a lamp on either side, and beneath the lamps a table, a ledger, and a man in grey scholar's robes with violet piping at the cuffs. He rose as they approached. He was narrow and pale, his dark hair drawn back, with the courteous stillness of a person who spends his days among things that do not hurry.

"Auditor Ashworth. And Isolde Vane." He bowed to each of them exactly the same depth. "Cormac Vrell, keeper of the Precedent Archive. The pass admits a second bearer. Will the lady sign?"

His eyes went to her hands and away again at once, and she gave him credit for both. "I can't hold a pen," Sol said. "I'm told it will be a few days."

"Then permit me." He wrote her name in the ledger himself, in a small square hand, and turned the book so she could see it. *Isolde Vane, bearer, second.* "Your hands were spoken of in the city," he said quietly. "The house is sorry for the cost."

He said it without weight, as a matter of record, and she could not find the edge of it. That was the worst thing about Thorne. It had no edges she could find.

The archive lay behind the arch: one long vaulted chamber cut into the rock, shelves climbing its walls in tiers reached by narrow iron ladders, oil lamps set at even intervals, and at the far end a window with nothing behind it but air and the sheer drop to the Reach floor. It smelled of vellum and iron and cold. Sol had spent years in archives, and this was the first one that felt as though it were listening.

"Two days ago the auditor read the ward-service ledgers," Vrell said. "The lady may wish to see what he saw."

"I do." She looked at Kael. "Stand where I can see you."

He moved to the end of the reading table and stayed there.

Vrell set the first volume open on the table and stood back. Kael turned the pages at her word, and she read.

It was as he had said. Name after name in cramped clerks' hands, year by year, three generations of a house that had stood in the gap of the old trade and let it take what it took. A warden of the Lower Spine, dead at thirty-one, the cost entered in the same neat column as a grain delivery. Two brothers, one after the other, seven years apart. A girl of fifteen, contracted to the eastern line, with a note in the margin: *did not come home.* Beside each death a dot of red-brown ink had dried in the same place on the page, as if one hand had touched every entry with the same finger.

"It goes on for two more volumes," Kael said quietly.

"I know." Her throat was tight. "I don't need the other two. I believe it." She meant it, and it did not change what she had come to say. "Keeper. Thorne's petition claims the reform mandate by right of this. Is that the argument the house will make on the twenty-eighth?"

"The house will make whatever argument its lord chooses." Vrell's voice was soft. "I keep the records. I do not draft the petitions. But I was asked to say one thing, if the auditors came." He folded his hands. "Lord Malrik asks that you read all of it before the Council opens the box, so that you do not oppose him in ignorance of what the house has paid. He says he would rather be opposed by a person who has read it than admired by one who has not."

Sol looked at Kael. His face gave her nothing, and then it gave her exactly what she needed: that he had heard the trap in the courtesy and the truth in the courtesy in the same breath, and that he was going to let her say which of them weighed more.

"Thank the lord for me," she said. "I will read every page. And on the twenty-eighth I will oppose him anyway, and I would like it recorded that both of those things are true."

"It is recorded," said Vrell. The corner of his mouth moved and stilled.

She had promised herself she would not ask. She asked.

"The fourth tier. The contracts filed under Vane."

Vrell did not blink. "The auditor did not take it down."

"I know. He told me." She turned her head. "Will you?"

"Yes."

"Bring it to the table. Then open it. I can't turn the pages, and I don't want anyone else's hands on it."

He climbed the iron ladder, and she watched every rung of it. He came down with a slim volume in cracked green leather, a Vane spine, and set it beneath the lamp. He looked at her. She nodded. He opened it, and it fell open the way such books do, to the page most often read.

The contract was in a clerk's fair hand, the ink gone brown. *Ward-taker, House Vane.* The year of her mother's binding. A term of service: *until dissolution of the trade or death of the bearer.* Sol had known that. She had known the shape of it from a hundred conversations that stopped when she walked into the room. What she had not known was the schedule at the foot of the page, added in a quicker hand, in ink that had not faded so evenly. A list headed *Dependents*, and beneath it a single line.

*Isolde, daughter, age 8.*

Her mother's hand. She would have known it anywhere. She had grown up reading that small forward-leaning script on the backs of receipts and along the edges of maps. It had never occurred to her that her name might be here. It had never occurred to her that her mother had stopped, in a Thorne clerk's office, in the middle of signing away her years, to make certain someone knew there was a child.

Sol stood very still. The linen on her hands was white against the dark wood. Under it the welts had begun to knit; the healer had said so that morning, changing the grey salve, the skin closing at the fingertips first, a fist by the end of the week if she did not force one before then. She had forced one twice anyway, in the grey before first bell, and paid for it both times. Now her fingers curled inside the wrappings toward the name on the page, wanting to touch it, and could not. Kael did not move, and did not speak, and did not turn the page until she said, "Turn it."

The next page held the counter-signature. A steward's hand, flat and firm: *Received for the works of the Lower Spine, House Thorne.* Beside it the knot, three strands, in near-black wax. Her mother had signed on Thorne's line. Thorne had held the other end of the same rope.

"They were on the same line," Sol said. She had not meant to say it aloud. "Her and Thorne's wards. The same system."

"Yes," said Vrell. "I believe that is why the pass was extended."

She looked at him. He met her eyes with the patience of a man who had already lost the argument and did not mind. "Lord Malrik does not think the house and the Vanes were enemies," he said. "He thinks the trade made them neighbors. He would like you to have the page."

It was a gift and a lever, and it was both at once, and she could not put it down. "Kael. Ask the keeper for a copy under the archive's seal. The whole page, and the one after."

"It will be sent to the lady by noon," said Vrell.

They read for two more hours. She read the second volume and the third, and Kael turned the pages, and no one in the room spoke unless a page required it. At the end she asked the keeper one more question, because it had been sitting in her since the gate.

"Why are there six retainers on the stair?"

"The house has petitioned the Council for a custodial role over its own precedents." Vrell said it as he had said everything, evenly. "Until the Council rules, the house keeps watch on what is its own, and on those who use it."

"You are watching us."

"The house is watching everyone, Lady Vane. It learned the habit from being watched."

They went up together. The stair cut into the western face was long and the wind came at it sideways, and Sol climbed with her hands held close and did not let Kael take her elbow. She did let him climb one step below her, where she could see him. At the third landing she stopped.

"From here," she said. "No more passes I haven't seen. No more days I hear about after. I'm not asking for a signature on a Council roll. I'm asking for that."

"From here," Kael said. It was not a speech, and she could see he knew it was not, and that he had not reached for one. "Together. Before."

She set the back of her wrist against his chest, briefly, the way she had once set her whole hand, and took it away. It was all the touch her hands would allow, and it was enough.

A boy in Accord grey waited at the top of the stair with a folded card. He held it out to Kael, and then, after a glance at Sol's hands, he held it out to the space between them instead. Violet paper. Near-black wax. The knot. Kael broke the seal and read it and passed it to her, because she could not have held it otherwise, and she read it over his arm.

*Lord Malrik Thorne requests the company of Auditor Kael Ashworth and Isolde Vane at supper tomorrow, at the seventh bell, in the house at the top of the Reach. He asks that neither come alone.*

Sol read the last line twice. The first card, a week ago, had been addressed to Kael alone, and he had refused it. This one carried both their names. And it had learned, in less time than it took to climb a stair, exactly what they had promised each other on the third landing.
