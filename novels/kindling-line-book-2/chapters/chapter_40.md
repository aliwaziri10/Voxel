The caster's guild sat in a room that smelled of hot metal even cold, the forge banked but never fully out, and Dessa had the four names written on a single sheet before Kael and Sol arrived, which told him she already knew what the sheet would say before the guild's own master confirmed it aloud.

Dessa had come early enough to claim the seat nearest the forge, and had not moved from it since, her coat still buttoned against a cold the room had long since burned off. "Two are cleared outright," the guild master said. He was a heavy man with burn scars up both forearms, and he handled the two tally pegs on the table the way he might have handled a live coal he respected more than he liked. "The dead man cast nothing eight years and more before either peg left a mold. The woman who left the Reach was three hundred miles east by the date on the guild's own ledger when the second peg would have needed casting. That leaves two."

"The boy," Sol said.

"The boy apprenticed under the fourth name on my sheet and has never cast alone to this tolerance in his life. I'd stake the guild's word on it. That leaves one." The master set both pegs down, side by side, and did not touch them again. "Corwin. He cast the Thorne die eight years back. He's cast nothing for House Thorne since, that the guild's book shows. Whether he cast these two, I can't swear to. The tolerance is fine enough that only a handful of hands on the Reach could manage it, and his is one of them. That's as far as the metal will tell you."

Kael looked at the name written plain on Dessa's sheet, no rank, no house, a single ordinary word standing in for whatever the man had or had not done. "Where is he now."

"Working," the guild master said. "In his own shop, under his own name, same as every day this month. I sent a boy to look before you arrived. He's not run. He's not hidden. If he's guilty of anything, he doesn't know it's been noticed yet."

"Or he knows it can't be proven," Dessa said, "and he's decided the safest thing a guilty man can do is keep hammering horseshoes in plain sight." She folded the sheet once and handed it to Kael rather than keeping it herself. "The commission can question him. It cannot compel him past a question, not on this alone. A shared mold and an old commission is a thread, Auditor. It isn't a rope."

"It's the first thread with a name on it," Sol said. "That's more than we had yesterday."

"It is." Dessa gathered the two pegs back into their cloth. "I'll have him asked, gently, this week. Gently, because a man wrongly frightened talks less than a man left alone to wonder whether he should be frightened at all." She looked between them. "You have a reading to prepare for. I'd rather you spent the next ten days on that than standing over my shoulder while I ask a smith about a mold."

They left the guild-room with the name written on a strip of paper in Kael's coat, and neither of them said much on the walk back to the ropewalk floor, because there was a great deal to say and none of it belonged to a public stair.

***

Ten days passed the way ten days pass when a fixed date sits at the end of them, each one both too long and not nearly long enough.

Kael's written finding grew and shrank and grew again on the desk in the upper workroom, the four-line list still folded inside its ledger, the third line still unfinished, though he had come close to it twice and stopped himself both times, not because he doubted what he thought but because he had promised the day would choose itself. The caster's name went nowhere. Corwin kept hammering horseshoes. Dessa's gentle asking, when it came, produced a man who answered every question and offered nothing beyond what was asked, which proved nothing either way and satisfied no one.

Renn did not return. On the twenty-second the search widened past Tar Lane and the boiling house to the lower forges generally, on Sol's insistence and Joren's legs, and found nothing but a fisherman on the twenty-fourth who thought he might have seen a man answering Renn's description on a skiff heading south along the coast road a full week and more past, too far gone by the time the word reached anyone to be chased. Mara's color kept improving by the healer's account and her eyes kept seeing nothing, and Joren kept sitting with her some mornings before the gate, and stopped mentioning it because there was nothing new to mention. The withdrawal count held at two hundred and eighty-six through the twenty-fifth, then climbed to two hundred and ninety-one on the twenty-sixth, three men and a woman signing within an hour of each other for reasons Ansa could not account for and did not try to invent.

On the morning of the twenty-seventh, the day before the reading, Kael finished the fourth line.

He did it alone, at the same window, with the same pencil, and did not show it to Sol that morning, not because he was hiding it but because he wanted to be certain the words would still be true by evening before he let anyone else carry them. By evening they still were. He showed her then, at Ansa's table, with Joren gone to check the gate and the awning down to its two lamps.

*Mine to answer for, whatever the reading finds.*

Sol read it twice, the way she had read the first three lines, and did not fold it back along its own crease this time. She kept it a moment longer, her thumb resting against the fourth line specifically, as if testing whether the ink had truly dried.

"That's not about the petition," she said.

"No. It's about tomorrow." Kael turned his cup without drinking from it, an old habit he had picked up from Joren without meaning to. "Whatever the reading finds tomorrow, about the Deed, about the tap, about whoever cast that peg, my name is on the written finding, and I intend it to stay there whether the finding is comfortable for House Ashworth or not. That's the fourth line. It isn't about you at all. It's the one I told you it wanted to be."

"I know." She handed the page back. "I didn't ask you to keep it off the list. I asked you to be honest about what it was. You have been."

"The petition still waits."

"The petition still waits. Tomorrow isn't the day for it, whatever tomorrow turns out to hold. We agreed on that a week ago and nothing since has moved the date." She said it without heat, a fact restated rather than a promise renewed, which was somehow more solid than if she had said it warmly. "Tomorrow is for the reading. For your finding. For whatever the Deed's thirty days decide to mean when they run out."

"Twelve houses in the chamber. The Deed lapses if nine set it aside. We have eight signed and five who could still flip, and none of them have moved since the fourth."

"Then tomorrow we find out whether a written finding from the Accord's own Auditor is worth a ninth signature, or whether it's worth exactly nothing to a room that's already decided." Sol looked past him, toward the dark beyond the awning where the ropewalk floor lamps burned in their long uneven row down toward the sealed hatch. "Either way, we'll know by tomorrow night. Eleven days of not knowing ends whether we're ready for the ending or not."

Kael folded the page along its new crease, the fourth line safely inside with the other three, and put it back in the ledger's cover where it had lived for ten days waiting to be finished. He had thought, folding it, that finishing a promise felt like it should feel lighter than this. It did not. It felt instead like the first honest weight he had carried in a long while, the kind that did not ask to be set down.

"Eleven days," he said. "Renn's, not mine. Mine were only ten."

"He'd tell you that's a difference worth noting, if he were here to say it." Ansa came out to top the pot one last time before the lamps went down for the night, and neither of them said anything further about the fourth line, or the ninth signature, or the reading waiting for them at the top of the Reach with the morning.

The morning of the twenty-eighth came up gray over the Reach, low cloud sitting on the spires so that the highest banners could not be seen from the ropewalk floor, only guessed at by the color bleeding faintly through the mist.

The Council chamber at the top of the Reach had not been full in Kael's memory, not for any sitting he could recall, but it was full now, benches dragged in from three adjoining rooms and still not enough, clerks standing two deep along the back wall with their tablets held ready. Twelve house seats ringed the chamber floor in their old order, each marked by a carved sigil in the stone beneath it, worn smooth by three centuries of the same twelve families sitting in the same twelve places. Eight of the twelve chairs already held their lords or their lords' delegates when Kael and Sol came through the side door reserved for Accord officers. Four sat empty, and would stay empty, by long custom, until the house they belonged to chose to fill them or chose not to. Kael had watched those four chairs empty at every sitting he could remember, and had never once heard a clerk explain why custom permitted a house to simply not answer, only that it always had, going back further than the Accord itself, as if the Reach had decided long ago that some silences were safer left unquestioned than forced into an answer.

Lord Malrik Thorne sat in his own chair, upright without support, his plain silver clasp the only mark on him in a room otherwise thick with house colors. His steward stood at his shoulder, small and grey and composed, and did not look toward the side door when it opened, though Kael was certain she had heard it. She had stood at that shoulder through every sitting Kael had witnessed in three years as Auditor, through condolence calls and quiet offers and now this, and he found he could no longer picture Thorne's chair without her standing exactly there, a fixture as permanent as the stone.

Sol's own family had no chair left to sit in. House Vane's seat had gone to ash and rubble years before either of them had reached an age to remember it whole, and she stood instead in the officers' gallery with Kael, a place granted to Accord auditors and their attendants, one step above the floor and one step below the dais where the Chancellor's empty chair waited for the sitting to open.

"Eight signed," Sol said quietly, counting the occupied chairs against what she already knew. "Four empty. If even one of the four fills its chair today and signs against the Deed, that's nine, and the Deed falls on its own thirty days without needing another word from either of us."

"None of the four have signaled they will." Kael kept his voice as low as hers, the chamber's murmur rising around them enough to cover it. "House Estler declined to attend at all. House Farrow sent a junior cousin with no authority to sign anything. The other two are simply late, or mean to stay that way until the reading tells them which way is safer."

"Then it comes to the finding."

"It comes to the finding." He touched the folded pages inside his coat, the written report he had carried up the Reach that morning, four lines of private promise folded inside its own separate cover behind it. "Whatever it costs the house. That was the fourth line, and I meant it before I wrote it down."

The Chancellor's clerk struck the chamber bell once, a single low note that rolled through stone and quieted even the standing crowd at the back wall. The Chancellor herself, an old woman in unadorned grey who outranked every seal in the room and wore none of her own, came through the dais door and took her chair without ceremony.

"The sitting is open," she said, her voice carrying without effort, the way a voice does when it has never once needed to be raised to be heard. "The Ward Deed's thirty days lapse at midnight tonight unless nine of twelve houses set it aside before the bell. Eight have signed. The Auditor of Accord has a finding to read into the record before any further house may add its name." She looked toward the officers' gallery, toward Kael, and did not soften the look with anything resembling encouragement. "Auditor Ashworth. Read your finding."

Kael stepped down onto the chamber floor alone. Sol did not follow, because the finding was his to give, and she had told him so a week past, and he had agreed, and neither of them had argued about it since. He unfolded the report at the center of twelve carved sigils worn smooth by three hundred years, with the Chancellor above him and Thorne's flat grey eyes fixed on him from across the ring, and began to read.
