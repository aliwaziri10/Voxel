Mara's hands had not warmed since the fifth. Kael held one of them anyway, careful of the healer's linen strip still bound along her wrist, and told her the news, because he told her things now.

"Tam signed," he said. "This morning. The new standard, the one read whole at the house. He went to the awning himself, without asking either of us, and put his name to it."

Mara's eyes stayed on the beams. The healer, folding linen at the room's far end, did not look up. "No change since yesterday," she said, to the room rather than to him. "Nor the day before that. I would tell you if there were."

"I know." Kael laid Mara's hand back on the blanket, gently, the way Sol had taught him without meaning to, by never once doing it any other way. "I still tell her. It costs nothing, and it might cost everything if I stopped, and I would rather be the fool who talked to a wall than the man who decided silence was safer."

Sol stood by the door with her arms folded, watching him. He had not yet learned to read her face since the reading at the house. It was not anger. It was quieter than anger and harder to name.

They walked down to the awning together, because Ansa's slate would say what it said whether they went or not, and because neither of them had suggested going separately. His stick struck the boards of the rope bridge in a slow rhythm, and she matched it without comment.

The violet awning stood where it had stood for eleven days. The brazier was lit, the tin cups racked, the clerks in grey behind their trestle looking anywhere but at the Auditor. Ansa sat on her stool under her own window across the lane with the slate on her knee and her chalk in her fist, and she did not get up.

The younger of the two clerks rose as they came up, and offered a tin cup across the trestle with a courtesy so exact it had been drilled. "Auditor. My lady. The tea is fresh."

"Thank you, no," Kael said.

"The house would take it kindly if the Auditor were seen to be at ease among the signers."

"I am sure it would." He looked at the cup, and at the clerk's steady hand, and let the silence stand until the clerk sat down again. He did not add that he had read every word the house had put in front of him and would read the next as carefully.

The count stood at two hundred and fifty-two. One new name in seven days, and it was Tam's.

"He didn't tell me first," Sol said, reading the chalk. "He told the house first. I only heard it from you."

"He told me at the reading, in front of you. That is not the same as telling you first."

"No." She did not turn her head. "It isn't."

Ansa's voice came across the lane, dry as rope. "He told me before either of you. He came to my window at first light and said it plain, and I told him he was a grown man and my sister's fool, and I told him to read the last line a fourth time. He said he had. Three times at the awning and once more at the table last night."

Sol crossed the lane in four strides. Kael stayed where he was, because it was not his to stand between them, and because he had learned what it cost to be the one who decided how much a person needed to hear.

"You knew at dawn," Sol said.

"I did."

"And you sat here with the slate and let me hear it from Kael."

"He asked me to keep it till he'd done it." Ansa held the chalk still. "He said if you knew before he went, you would do the thing you did not mean to do, and he wanted to walk to that awning with nobody's hand on his back, not even a kind one. I gave him my word. I have known him since he was carried into this lane wrapped in a splicer's apron. I kept it."

Kael watched Sol take that in. He knew the look. He had worn it himself, on the far side of a table, the day he had learned that being told after was a thing that could be done to a person with the best of reasons.

"I would not have stopped him," Sol said, and her voice had a crack in it he had not heard before.

"Would you not?" Ansa said, without cruelty. "I do not think you know. I think that is what you cannot get past, girl, and I am sorry for it, because I would not care to stand in your boots."

Sol did not answer. She read the chalk again, as if it might change, then came back across the lane and stood at his shoulder, and did not take his arm, and did not step away from it either.

"She is right," she said quietly. "I told him it wasn't mine to decide. And then I sat at that table wanting it to have been mine anyway. One is what I said. The other is what I wanted to be true."

"You did not say a word to stop him."

"No. But I would not have thanked you for saying so." She looked at the slate, not at him. "You have spent a month learning the difference between deciding for me and telling me after. I found out yesterday that I have the same fault in a smaller box, and I have had one day to look at it."

"Then we are both still learning it."

"That is not comfort. That is just true." She turned from the awning. "I am not asking you to solve it for me. I have had enough of things being solved."

They did not go back to the archive, or to the Council office, or to any room where the audit's business waited. Kael found himself walking toward Tar Lane instead, and Sol matched his pace without asking why. The boiling-house cellar was three streets from where they stood, and neither of them had been back since the day the clerks carried out a stool and a book and left the iron door shut.

The cellar door still stood on its new hasp, unlocked. The timber bar Sol had ordered lifted lay where it had been set down. Kael pushed the door open onto the same twelve steps, and took them one at a time, the stick before the foot and his right hand on the wall. She neither slowed nor hurried, and at the bottom he was breathing harder than he wished and did not pretend otherwise.

The same black water lay under the same blue seam. He counted it without deciding to. Nine breaths, eleven, out of step as they had always been, and the single dark breath where the two went out together.

The iron door at the far end stood ajar. It was not forced or broken. It was simply open, the way a door is left by a person who meant to come back and has not yet.

"It was shut when the clerks left," Sol said quietly. "It has been opened since."

"On the drop, then," Kael said, and she nodded, and they crossed together on the dark, six strides, her gloved hand not quite touching his sleeve.

The chamber beyond was smaller than he had pictured it, a single table and the stool still pushed back at the same angle. On the table, where nothing had been before, lay a second brass tally peg, twin to the one in Sol's glove, laid down deliberately and squared to the table's edge, the way a man leaves a thing for a person he expects to come looking.

Sol did not touch it. She stood in the doorway and looked at it for a long moment.

"That is not Renn's hand," she said. "He would have chalked words. This is only the peg. Left."

"Left for us, or left because a man needed both hands to leave in a hurry."

"Or left to tell us a person is still down here, and wants us to know it, and does not want to say why yet." Her voice had gone very even, the tone she used when she was working hard not to let a thing frighten her. "I do not like being handed pieces of a message I cannot read."

"Neither do I." Kael looked past the table, to the dark beyond the ward-lamp's reach, where the back wall should have been and where the light did not quite find an edge. "We should send for Joren before we go further."

"Agreed." She said it without argument, which told him more than the word did. A fortnight ago she would have gone on alone rather than wait. "But we mark it first. Whatever is back there, it will not go anywhere faster than a boy can run."

He took the peg from the table and wrapped it in his handkerchief without touching it bare, and set it in his coat. Sol's was in her glove, and his was in his coat, and for the first time since Renn's board had held them together the two were in two different pockets, one on each of them. He did not know what it meant to hold one each instead of one alone. He suspected, in the ward-lamp's failing light with the dark still unmeasured ahead, that he would not like the answer when it came.

They climbed out into the grey afternoon and sent Ansa's boy running for Joren. Kael stopped on the top step and stood with his hand flat on the wall until his breath came back, and Sol waited three paces off and neither offered nor looked away, which was its own courtesy.

Joren came at a run with two wardens behind him, the scar on his hand livid with the cold. He heard it through without a word, and when Kael had finished he looked down the twelve steps for a long moment.

"Mara would have gone in," he said. "First. Without waiting."

"I know," Kael said. "That is why we wait."

Joren's jaw worked once. Then he nodded, and set one warden at the head of the steps and one at the door, and told them nobody went past the table, for any reason, unless the Auditor and the Lady Vane both said so aloud in the same breath.

"Both," Sol said, and Kael heard that she meant it as a promise to him as much as an order to Joren.

In the waiting, standing in the lane with the cellar door propped open behind them, neither of them spoke again of Tam, or of the standard, or of the smaller box Sol had named twice now and had not found her way out of.
