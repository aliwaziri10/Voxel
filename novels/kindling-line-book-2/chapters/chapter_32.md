The garden clung to the cliff face like a stubborn thing refusing to fall. Stone terraces stepped down from House Vane's living quarter, each level held by mortar older than the Accord and planted with herbs that smelled of salt and iron. Sol had come here to breathe. The wind off the Reach carried the scent of kelp and distant smoke from the lower forges. She stood at the edge of the third terrace, her fingers curled around the cold lip of the balustrade, watching gulls cut white arcs against the gray sky.

Footsteps on the gravel path behind her. Measured. Deliberate. She did not turn until the sound stopped at her shoulder.

"You left the audit session," Kael said. His voice was low, pitched for the wind. "Valerius asked for your read on the Thorne ledger corrections."

"I gave it to Mara," Sol said. She kept her eyes on the water. "She has a steadier hand with numbers right now."

Kael moved to stand beside her. His coat caught the wind, the Ashworth sigil embroidered at the cuff catching a flash of pale light. He looked tired. The bruises beneath his jaw had faded to yellow-green, but the line of his shoulders held a stiffness that had not been there three weeks ago. The fall at the Thorne ward site had cracked two ribs. The healer had said six weeks for bone to knit. Kael had given it ten days.

"Mara is thorough," he allowed. "She will catch what I missed."

"You didn't miss anything." Sol turned finally. The wind tugged at the loose strands of hair at her temple. "You didn't give her the chance to find it. You corrected the Thorne entries before the session opened. You flagged the discrepancies yourself. You presented the cleaned version to the table."

Kael's jaw tightened. "The discrepancies were errors. Transcription mistakes in the ward valuation columns. Thorne's scribes have been sloppy since the decree was announced. If I had waited for the full table to review them, the errors would have entered the record. Challenging them after the fact gives Thorne ammunition. They would claim we are manufacturing disputes to delay the ceremony."

"So you decided unilaterally what the record should show."

"I decided to prevent a dispute that would hand Thorne the narrative." He met her gaze, gray eyes steady in a face gone lean with strain. "I told you after. I told Mara after. I told the senior clerk before the ink dried. There was no concealment."

"No." Sol's voice was quiet. "There never is. You tell me after. You tell the table after. You tell the Accord after. The decision is already made by the time anyone else knows a choice existed."

The wind gusted, whipping her sleeve against her wrist. She did not flinch.

"Kael, listen to me." She stepped closer, closing the space between them until she could see the fine lines at the corners of his eyes. "I am not accusing you of lying. I am not accusing you of secrecy. That was the old pattern. That was your father's pattern. You broke that pattern. You have been honest every single time. You have told me the truth after you acted, every single time. And that is the problem."

His brow furrowed. "I don't understand."

"Honesty without shared authority is still exclusion." The words came out flat, not angry. "You make the choice. You bear the risk. You accept the cost. Then you inform me. You inform the table. You inform the Accord. The outcome is already fixed. My input, Mara's input, the senior clerk's input — none of it changes what you have already done. We are witnesses to your decisions. We are not partners in them."

Kael was silent. The gulls cried above them. Somewhere below, a forge hammer fell in a rhythm that shook the stone beneath their feet.

"You said you would not decide what I don't need to know," he said at last. His voice had gone rough. "In the archive, when you found the pass in my coat. You said that. I admitted it. I told you I would change."

"You have changed the timing," Sol said. "You have not changed the structure. The structure is still you alone in the room with the ledger, you alone at the ward site, you alone deciding which risks are acceptable and which are not. The only difference is that now you send a message afterward telling us what you chose."

"I am the lead auditor." The words were stiff. "The Accord named me. House Ashworth backs me. The ward trade dismantlement falls to me. If I bring every decision to a vote, Thorne will bury us in procedure. They will use every consultation as a lever to delay the ceremony, to rewrite the decree, to claim the Vault infrastructure for themselves under the guise of due process."

"Then bring me into the room before the decision is made." Sol's hands fisted at her sides. "Not after. Before. Let me argue. Let me disagree. Let me see the same ledger you see at the same time you see it. Let me stand at the ward site with you. I am not a child you are protecting. I am not a ward you are shielding. I am your counterpart. I have the same training. I have the same stake. I have the same right to be wrong alongside you."

Kael flinched. A microscopic movement, barely visible in the wind. "Sol. The ward site — you felt the backlash. You know what it cost. I could not ask you to stand in that again."

"You didn't ask." Her voice sharpened. "You decided I would not stand in it. You decided the cost was too high for me. You decided my risk tolerance for me. That is not protection. That is ownership."

The word hung between them. The forge hammer fell again. A gull screamed, diving toward the water.

"I love you," Kael said. The words came out raw, unguarded. "I said it in the infirmary. I meant it. I say it now. I love you. That is why I make the choices I make. Because the cost of losing you is the only cost I cannot calculate."

"And the cost of losing me as an equal?" Sol asked. "The cost of making me a person you protect instead of a person you trust? Have you calculated that one?"

He had no answer. She saw it in the way his throat moved, in the slight shift of his weight, in the breath he drew and did not release.

"Thorne's reform decree," she said, changing direction. "The Accord ceremony. When?"

"Two weeks." Kael's voice had gone flat again, the auditor's tone returning. "Twenty-fourth of Cinderveil. The full convocation. Thorne will present the Ward Deed of Succession as the legal basis for their administration of the absorbed ward contracts. They will cite the clause transferring ultimate authority to the house administering reform. They will argue that the Accord's own language permits it."

"And you will argue against it."

"I have already prepared the counter-argument. The clause refers to administrative authority, not sovereign authority. The Accord retains final oversight. The Keeper's seat — the institutional office — holds the veto. Thorne is conflating management with ownership. It is a legal fiction. A dangerous one."

"Will you bring me the counter-argument before you file it?"

Kael hesitated. The pause stretched, filled only by wind and the distant hammer.

"I will," he said finally. "I will bring it to you tonight. Before I file. Before the senior clerk sees it. We will go through it line by line."

Sol studied his face. The exhaustion. The stubbornness. The fear he would not name. She believed he meant it. She also believed he would forget, or decide the timeline was too tight, or decide the revision was too minor to warrant consultation. The structure held. The pattern held.

"Tonight," she said. "In the archive. Bring the full draft. Not the summary. The full draft."

"Agreed."

She nodded once. "Good. Then I will see you there."

She turned back to the balustrade, gripping the stone until her knuckles whitened. She heard him shift behind her, heard the gravel crunch as he stepped away. He did not say goodbye. He did not reach for her. He left the way he had come, measured and deliberate, and she let him go.

The wind off the Reach tasted of salt and iron and something else, something that smelled like rain coming in from the deep water. Sol watched the gulls ride the updrafts, wings outstretched, suspended in the invisible current. They did not flap. They did not struggle. They simply waited for the air to carry them.

She exhaled, long and slow, and wondered how many more decisions would be made before she was allowed to fly.
