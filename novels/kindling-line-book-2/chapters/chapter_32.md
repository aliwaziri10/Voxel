Mara's hands had not warmed since the fifth. Kael held one of them anyway, careful of the healer's line still taped along her wrist, and told her the news the way he had promised himself he would: first-hand, before anyone else heard it from him secondhand.

"Tam signed," he said. "This morning. The new standard, the one read whole at the house. He went to the awning himself, without asking either of us, and put his name to it."

Mara's eyes stayed on the beams. The healer, folding linen at the room's far end, did not look up. "No change since yesterday," she said, to the room rather than to him. "Nor the day before that. I would tell you if there were."

"I know." Kael set Mara's hand back on the blanket, gentle, the way Sol had taught him without meaning to, by never once doing it any other way. "I still tell her things. It costs nothing and it might cost everything, and I would rather be the fool who talked to a wall than the man who decided silence was safer."

Sol stood by the door, her arms folded, watching him with an expression he had not yet learned to read since the reading at the house. Not anger. Something quieter and harder to name.

They walked to the awning together, because Ansa's slate would say what it said whether they went or not, and because neither of them had suggested going separately.

The count stood at two hundred and fifty-two. One new name in seven days, and it was Tam's.

"He didn't tell me first," Sol said, reading the chalk. "He told the house first. I only heard it from you."

"Would you have stopped him if he'd told you first?"

She did not answer right away, and Kael let the silence hold, because he had learned, slowly and at real cost, that filling it for her was its own kind of taking.

"I don't know," she said finally. "That's the part I can't get past. I don't know what I would have done with the chance, and I'm glad I never had it, because I don't trust what I would have done with it."

"You told him it wasn't yours to decide."

"I told him that. And then I stood at that table wanting it to have been mine anyway. Those aren't the same thing, Kael. One is what I said. The other is what I wanted to be true." She looked at him. "You've spent two months learning the difference between deciding for me and telling me after. I only found out three days ago that I have the same problem in a smaller box, and I don't have two months of practice at fixing it. I have three days."

"Then we're both still learning it."

"That's not comfort. That's just true." She turned from the slate. "I'm not asking you to solve it for me. I've had enough of things being solved."

They did not go back to the archive, or to the Council office, or to any of the rooms where the audit's business waited to be done. Kael found himself walking toward Tar Lane instead, and Sol matched his pace without asking why, because she had likely worked out the same thing he had: the boiling-house cellar was three streets from where they stood, and neither of them had been back since the day the clerks carried out an empty stool.

The cellar door still stood on its new hasp, unlocked, the timber bar Sol had ordered lifted still lying where it had been set down. Kael pushed the door open onto the same black water, the same two ward-lines gone dark together for their single useless breath, nine and eleven, out of step as they had always been.

The iron door at the room's far end stood ajar. Not forced. Not broken. Simply open, the way a door is left by someone who meant to come back and has not yet.

"It was shut when the clerks left with the book," Sol said quietly. "Someone's opened it since."

Kael crossed the two fingers of standing water with care, his stick finding the floor before his weight did. The chamber beyond was smaller than he had pictured it from the outside, a single table, the stool still pushed back at the same angle, and on the table's surface, where nothing had been before, a second brass tally peg, twin to the one Sol carried, laid down deliberately, squared to the table's edge the way a man leaves something for someone he expects to come looking.

Sol did not touch it. She stood in the doorway and looked at it for a long moment.

"That's not Renn's hand," she said. "He'd have chalked something. Words. This is just the peg. Left."

"Left for us, or left because someone needed both hands to leave in a hurry."

"Or left to tell us someone is still down here, and wants us to know it, and doesn't want to say why yet." Sol's voice had gone very even, the tone she used when she was working hard not to let a thing frighten her. "I don't like being handed pieces of a message I can't read."

"Neither do I." Kael looked past the table, to the dark beyond the ward-lamp's reach, where the chamber's back wall should have been and where, instead, the light did not quite find an edge. "We should send for Joren before we go further."

"Agreed." She said it without argument, which told him more than the word itself did. Three weeks ago she would have gone on alone rather than wait. "But we mark it first. Whatever's back there, it isn't going anywhere faster than we can send a boy running."

Kael took the peg from the table, wrapped it in his handkerchief without touching it bare, and set it in his coat pocket beside the first, the two lying together for the first time since Renn's tally board had held them both. He did not know yet what it meant to hold two instead of one. He suspected, standing in the ward-lamp's failing light with Sol beside him and the dark still unmeasured ahead, that he would not like the answer when it came.

They climbed out into the grey afternoon and sent Ansa's boy running for Joren, and did not go back down until he came, and in the waiting, standing in the lane with the cellar door propped open behind them, neither of them spoke again of Tam, or of the standard, or of the smaller box Sol had named for the first time that morning and had not yet found her way out of.
