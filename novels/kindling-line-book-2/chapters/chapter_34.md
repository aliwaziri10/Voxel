Kael did not go up after her.

He told himself, standing at the mouth of the new stair with the water climbing past his boots, that giving her the distance she'd asked for was itself a form of listening, and he almost believed it. Then he heard how that sounded, the same reasoning dressed in new clothes, and he made himself stop pretending the two things were different.

He climbed anyway, an hour later, once the water had crept high enough that staying became its own stubbornness. Corren was gone from the top step. Joren stood there instead, arms crossed, saying nothing, which was its own report.

"She went toward the infirmary," Joren said finally, when Kael's silence had stretched too long to be comfortable for either of them. "I didn't follow. Didn't think you'd want me to say I had."

"You did right."

"I didn't do anything. That's the part I'm not sure was right."

Kael left him with the stair and the rising water and walked the long way to the infirmary, not because it was shorter, but because he needed the length of it to decide what he meant to say when he arrived, and did not manage to decide anything at all.

Mara had not changed. The healer said it without being asked, the way she said it every day now, a kindness disguised as routine. Kael sat in the chair beside the bed anyway and took the cold hand from the blanket and held it, because Sol had taught him that much without ever once telling him to do it.

"I did the thing again," he said. "The one I promised I'd stopped doing. Not the same shape as before. I didn't hide anything. I told Joren where I was going before I went. I just didn't ask her first, because an old, ugly part of me still believes that if I get to the danger before she does, I've spent a coin I'm allowed to spend and she hasn't lost one she isn't allowed to lose."

Mara's face did not change. It never did.

"She named it," he said. "Not just my part of it. Hers too. Said she'd wanted to hold Tam back the same way, silently, and hadn't, and that not acting on it didn't mean the wanting wasn't real. I don't know what to do with that. I built the whole shape of my apology around being the only one who does this, and she just took that away from me, and I don't know if I'm supposed to feel less alone in it or more afraid, because if it isn't only mine to fix, I don't know whose it is."

He sat with that a while. The healer moved paper behind him. The window's light shifted, thin and coppery, the copper cast Cinderveil's haze always put on a room at this hour.

"I told her I loved her six days ago," he said, quieter. "On Tar Lane, at the forge, with Renn's chalk still on the wall behind us. She kissed me first that morning, and I haven't stopped feeling it since, which is part of why I'm afraid of what it costs to say the words again only now, in the middle of a thing I can't otherwise defend. I don't want it to be an apology. I want it to just be true, sitting there, not doing any work at all."

Mara's hand stayed still and cold in his. He set it back on the blanket, gently, and left before the healer could ask him anything he didn't have an answer for.

***

Sol came home after the lamps were out. He heard the latch and did not turn his head, and then the boards, and the small sounds of a woman undressing in the dark with her back to him, and the bed took her weight on the far side.

A hand's width of linen lay between them. He lay on his back with his eyes on the beams and kept both hands flat on the blanket at his sides, and it took a decision, as keeping them on the arms of a chair had taken one in the dark before the forge. He could feel the warmth of her along that width, the way the needles had gathered toward her palm. He wanted to reach across it so badly that his shoulder ached. He did not, because a reach was a way of asking to be forgiven, and he had promised himself he would ask for nothing that night he could not also do without.

Near the small hours she said, into the dark, "You are not asleep."

"No."

"Nor am I." A long breath. "I am not going to turn over."

"I know."

"It is not that I do not want to."

He shut his eyes. "I know that too," he said, and lay awake until the window greyed, listening to her not sleep.

***

He found her the next afternoon, not at the house, not at the awning, but within sight of the Tar Lane forge itself, on the low wall across from it where the heat still reached her in the cooling afternoon. It was the lane they had walked together six days ago, her sleeve against his at every stride, toward a door and what had waited behind it.

She did not turn when he arrived. She had heard his stick on the gravel long before he reached her.

"I went to Mara," he said. "I told her what I did. I don't know why it's easier to say it to a woman who can't answer."

"Maybe because she can't tell you it's not enough."

"Maybe." He came to stand beside her, not touching, keeping the distance she had asked for without her having to ask for it twice.

Her hair was still damp at the temple from the haze, and a strand of it had come loose and lay along her jaw. He knew exactly how it would feel under his thumb. He set his hand on top of his stick and kept it there.

"You did not reach across last night," she said, still watching the forge. "I lay there waiting to be angry at you for reaching, and you did not."

"You said you were not going to turn over."

"I said I was not going to. I did not say I wanted you to be so good at listening."

It was the nearest thing to a joke either of them had made in two days, and neither of them laughed. "I wanted to," Kael said. "The whole width of the night."

"I know. I could hear you wanting to." She drew a breath. "That is not a promise of anything, Kael. It is only true."

"I will take true."

"I'm not going to tell you I've solved it," he went on, because it had to be said and he would not let the warmth of her nearness say it for him. "I haven't. I climbed down that stair alone yesterday morning for the same reason I've climbed down a dozen stairs alone before you ever asked me to stop, and I don't have a clean account of why yesterday was different from the days before it, when I asked first."

"I don't need a clean account. I need to know you're not going to hand me one and call it finished."

"I'm not handing you anything. I'm telling you I don't know if I can promise the shape of me is done shifting back into that habit when I'm frightened enough. I want to promise it. Wanting isn't the same as being able to."

Sol was quiet a long moment, her eyes on the forge smoke rising across the lane, thin and grey against the copper haze.

"I don't have a clean account either," she said finally. "I know what I wanted to do with Tam, and I know I didn't do it, and I don't know if that was strength or only luck, because the chance to do it never quite closed all the way and I never had to find out what I'd choose if it had."

"Then we're both standing on the same unfinished ground."

"That's not comfort, Kael."

"I'm not offering comfort. I don't have any to give that would be honest." He looked at her, finally, and did not reach for her hand, though the wanting to was plain enough on his face that she could have read it without him saying it aloud. "I only have this. I heard you at the stair. I've been sitting with it since, and I don't have a better version of myself to offer you yet. Only the truth that I heard it, and it hasn't left me since."

The forge across the lane rang once, iron against iron, and neither of them spoke for a while after, the silence holding a weight that was not yet repair and was not yet its opposite either, only the long, uncomfortable middle of a thing neither of them had finished saying.

"Renn's commission sits in two days," Sol said eventually, because the silence had gone on long enough that naming another thing felt like its own mercy. "I still have his second peg to bring, and yours."

"I'll have mine ready." Kael's voice had gone careful again, grateful for the smaller, solvable thing. "The stair under the boiling house is still rising. Joren's posted it closed until the water settles. Whatever's past it will still be past it in two days."

"Or it won't be, and we'll have lost whatever it was."

"Then we'll have lost it honestly, together, deciding to wait rather than one of us deciding alone to go and look."

Sol almost smiled at that, the ghost of one, gone before it fully arrived. "That's the first thing you've said all day that sounded like you actually believed it."

"I'm trying to believe more of them." Kael looked back at the forge smoke, the copper light, the Reach's long fall beneath them. "I don't know yet if trying is the same as changing. I only know that stopping trying isn't a thing I'm willing to do, whatever it costs to keep failing at it in front of you instead of in private, where it would be easier and worth less."

Sol did not answer that directly. Before she rose, she set her right hand flat on the warm stone between them, an inch from where his rested. He looked at the inch. He did not close it, because she had not asked him to, and she left her hand there as long as it took to draw one breath, and then she took it away.

When she finally turned to go, she did not tell him whether the ground between them had shifted or only held its place, and he did not ask her to say more than she had.
