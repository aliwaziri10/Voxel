Kael did not go up after her.

He told himself, standing at the mouth of the new stair with the water climbing past his boots, that giving her the distance she'd asked for was itself a form of listening, and he almost believed it. Then he heard how that sounded, the same reasoning dressed in new clothes, and he made himself stop pretending the two things were different.

The lantern had burned low on its oil. He took the chalk stub from his coat and, on the third step below Corren's thumb-mark, scratched a mark of his own at the water's edge, so that whoever came after him would have a measure and not a guess. Then he stood a while longer and watched the wet edge creep, a slow dark tongue taking the stone a grain at a time, until he understood that he was waiting for it to stop and that it had no intention of stopping for him.

He climbed anyway, an hour later, once the water had crept high enough that staying became its own stubbornness. Corren was gone from the top step. Joren stood there instead, arms crossed, saying nothing, which was its own report.

"She went toward the infirmary," Joren said finally, when Kael's silence had stretched too long to be comfortable for either of them. "She didn't run. I've seen her run from less. I didn't follow, and I didn't think you'd want me to say I had."

"You did right."

"I didn't do anything. That's the part I'm not sure was right." Joren uncrossed his arms and looked at his own scarred hand as if it belonged to a man he was not sure of. "You told me where you were going, and I let that be enough. Telling isn't asking. I stood on this step and knew it, and I let you past because you're the Auditor and the words came in the right order. Next time you say it to me, I'm going to ask where she is."

"Good," Kael said, and found it cost less to say than he had braced for. "I marked the water. Third step, below Corren's line. Write the hour beside mine when the watch changes."

"Hour and a thumb's width," Joren said. "And I'll say it to the both of you, since we're saying things."

Kael left him with the stair and the rising water and walked the long way to the infirmary, not because it was shorter, but because he needed the length of it to decide what he meant to say when he arrived, and did not manage to decide anything at all.

Mara had not changed. The healer said it without being asked, the way she said it every day now, a kindness disguised as routine. Kael sat in the chair beside the bed anyway and took the cold hand from the blanket and held it, because Sol had taught him that much without ever once telling him to do it. The linen strip along the wrist had been changed since yesterday. Under his thumb the skin was still cold at the pulse, colder than the hand around it, as it had been since the fourth, and no one in the room had offered him a reason.

"I did the thing again," he said. "The one I promised I'd stopped doing. Not the same shape as before. I didn't hide anything. I told Joren where I was going before I went. I just didn't ask her first, because an old, ugly part of me still believes that if I reach the danger before she does, then it's mine to lose and not hers."

Mara's face did not change. It never did.

"She named it. And then she named her own, about Tam, and took away the one thing my apology was standing on, which was being the only one who does this. I don't know if I'm meant to feel less alone in it or more afraid, because if it isn't only mine to fix, I don't know whose it is."

He sat with that a while. The healer moved paper behind him. The window's light shifted, thin and coppery, the copper cast Cinderveil's haze always put on a room at this hour.

"I told her I loved her five days ago," he said, quieter. "On Tar Lane, at the forge, with Renn's chalk still on the wall behind us. I haven't stopped feeling it since, and that's why I'm afraid to say it again in the middle of this. I don't want it to be an apology. I want it to be true, sitting there, not doing any work at all."

Mara's hand stayed still and cold in his. He set it back on the blanket, gently, and left before the healer could ask him anything he didn't have an answer for.

***

Sol came home after the lamps were out. He heard the latch and did not turn his head, and then the boards, and the small sounds of a woman undressing in the dark with her back to him, and the bed took her weight on the far side.

A hand's width of linen lay between them. He lay on his back with his eyes on the beams and kept both hands flat on the blanket at his sides, and it took a decision, as keeping them on the arms of a chair had taken one in the dark before the forge. He could feel the warmth of her along that width, the way the needles had gathered toward her palm. He wanted to reach across it so badly that his shoulder ached. He did not, because a reach was a way of asking to be forgiven, and he had promised himself he would ask for nothing that night he could not also do without.

Near the small hours she said, into the dark, "You are not asleep."

"No."

"Nor am I." A long breath. "I am not going to turn over."

"I know."

"It is not that I do not want to."

He shut his eyes. "I know that too," he said, and lay awake until the window greyed, listening to her not sleep.

***

By first light her side of the bed was empty. He laid his palm in the hollow she had left, and the linen gave back the last of her warmth and then nothing, and he understood, lying there with his hand on the place, that this was the cold she had woken to yesterday. He had spent a day telling himself he understood what he had done to her. He had not understood it until he was the one reaching for a warm place and finding it gone.

He went to the boiling house because it was the one place in the Reach where he was permitted to stand and be useful without deciding anything. Joren had changed the watch. On the third step, beneath Kael's scratch and Corren's thumb, two more lines had been chalked in a square warden's hand, each with an hour beside it, and the black water had taken the first and was lapping at the second. A hand's width above his own mark, in a night. It was slower than Sol had guessed in the dark, and no comfort for that. Renn's line at the stair's mouth had gone under at the foot. The wet had reached the last word, *FOUND*, and stood there as if it meant to read it.

"Posted closed," Joren said from the top step. "Council seal on the hasp, and I've said it to Corren and the day watch, both, out loud, that nobody goes past the table until you and the Lady Vane say so in the same breath. They looked at me as though I'd asked them to pray. I told them it was cheaper than the other thing."

"Thank you."

He did not go down. He wanted to, badly, in the way his hand had wanted the warm linen, and he stood with the stick in both fists and let the wanting go by, and did not follow it.

He found her that afternoon, not at the house, not at the awning, but on the low wall across from the Tar Lane forge, where the heat of the banked fire still reached her through the stone. It was the lane they had walked together six days ago, her sleeve against his at every stride, toward a door and what had waited behind it.

She did not turn when he arrived. She had heard his stick on the gravel long before he reached her.

Across the lane, on the soot-dark wall, Renn's chalk was still there: thirty-nine rounds in their tidy rows and the fortieth begun and left, one upright stroke with nothing after it. Eight days. Kael made himself read it as he would read any entry that did not balance, and found that the last stroke would not let him.

"I went to Mara," he said. "I told her what I did. I don't know why it's easier to say it to a woman who can't answer."

"Maybe because she can't tell you it's not enough."

"Maybe." He lowered himself onto the wall beside her, a span of warm stone between them, and kept the distance she had asked for without her having to ask twice. Her hair was still damp at the temple from the haze, and a strand of it had come loose and lay along her jaw. He knew exactly how it would feel under his thumb. He set one hand on the head of his stick and the other flat on the stone, on his own side of the span, and kept them both there.

"You did not reach across last night," she said, still watching the forge. "I lay there waiting to be angry at you for reaching, and you did not."

"You said you were not going to turn over."

"I said I was not going to. I did not say I wanted you to be so good at listening."

It was the nearest thing to a joke either of them had made in two days, and neither of them laughed. "I wanted to," Kael said. "The whole width of the night."

"I know. I could hear you wanting to." She drew a breath. "That is not a promise of anything, Kael. It is only true."

"I will take true."

"Joren stopped me on the step yesterday," he went on, because it had to be said, and he would not let the warmth of her nearness say it for him. "He told me telling him isn't the same as asking you. Next time, he says, he'll ask where you are before he lets me past. I thanked him. I meant it. I still can't tell you it will hold when I'm frightened enough."

Sol watched the forge smoke. "That's more than either of us had at the bottom of the stair," she said. "It isn't the whole of it."

"No."

"I keep going back to Tam. To whether it was strength that I never reached for him, or only that the chance closed before I had to find out."

"Then we're both standing on unfinished ground."

"That's not comfort, Kael."

"I'm not offering comfort. I don't have any to give that would be honest." He looked at her, finally, and did not reach for her hand, though the wanting to was plain enough on his face that she could have read it without him saying it aloud. "I only have this. I heard you at the stair. I've been sitting with it since, and I don't have a better version of myself to offer you yet. Only that I heard it, and it hasn't left."

The forge across the lane rang once, iron against iron, and neither of them spoke for a while after, the silence holding a weight that was not yet repair and was not yet its opposite either, only the long, uncomfortable middle of a thing neither of them had finished saying.

"The commission sits in two days," Sol said eventually, because the silence had gone on long enough that naming another thing felt like its own mercy. "They'll want both pegs on the table."

"Mine's in my coat."

"Mine's in my glove." She flexed the gloved hand once, as if it might still be closed around the brass. "I keep finding my fingers on it."

"So do I," Kael said. "I hadn't told you that."

"I saw the square of it in your coat, in the cellar. You carry it like a wrapped egg." She said it flatly, without looking at him, and it was the second nearest thing to a joke of the week, and again neither of them laughed.

"The stair under the boiling house is still rising," he said. "Joren's posted it closed until the water settles. Whatever's past it will still be past it in two days."

"Or it won't be, and we'll have lost whatever it was."

"Then we'll have lost it honestly, together, deciding to wait rather than one of us deciding alone to go and look."

Sol almost smiled at that, the ghost of one, gone before it fully arrived. "That's the first thing you've said all day that sounded like you actually believed it."

"I'm trying to believe more of them." Kael looked back at the forge smoke, the copper light, the Reach's long fall beneath them. "I don't know yet if trying is the same as changing. I only know that stopping trying isn't a thing I'm willing to do, whatever it costs to keep failing at it in front of you instead of in private, where it would be easier and worth less."

Sol did not answer that directly. Before she rose, she set her right hand flat on the warm stone, crossing most of the span between them and stopping an inch short of his. He looked at the inch. He did not close it, because she had not asked him to, and she left her hand there as long as it took to draw one breath, and then she took it away.

When she finally turned to go, she did not tell him whether the ground between them had shifted or only held its place, and he did not ask her to say more than she had.
