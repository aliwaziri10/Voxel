<!-- chapter_date: 28 Cinderveil, Year 4 of the Reckoning Accord -->

Sol counted the chairs a second time. It was the only work the gallery left her.

Eight filled. Four not. The carved sigils under the empty ones had worn to the same soft dish as the rest, and none of the four looked more abandoned than its neighbors, which she supposed was the point of a custom that let a house stay away without ever saying why.

Kael read standing in the ring with the report held level at his chest and the stick hooked over his forearm, and in the whole first minute he did not look toward her once. She had asked for that, in her way. A man who reads to the woman he loves reads a different document from the one he reads to the Council, and she wanted the Council's.

The ferrule of the stick shifted against the stone twice as he began. She knew what it meant. The feeling in his dead leg came back as needles now, worst when he stood still, and he would stand until the last word was read before he let the weight go to the stick or to anything else.

"The finding of the Auditor of the Accord, entered at the public sitting of the twenty-eighth of Cinderveil." It was the ledger voice, dry and level, the voice that had frightened her when they met because nothing in it moved. She understood now that nothing in it moved because the weight was spread evenly. "It has three parts. I will read them in the order I found them, which is not the order that flatters me."

A clerk's pen scratched and stopped.

"First, the tap. Under the ropewalk gallery in the Lower Reach, in a sublevel the register lists as contested, the Vault's trunk line has been tapped and metered for three years. The lock plate bears the Thorne knot. For those three years House Thorne kept the Lower Reach lit at its own cost, and for those same years it kept the man who read the meter behind a locked door. I find that the light was a kindness and the lock was a crime, and that a Council which wishes to speak well of the first must be willing to speak plainly of the second."

Nothing moved on the benches. Sol watched the eight who had signed, one by one, the way she had once watched a ward for the first hairline of a fault. The lord nearest the dais studied the Chancellor's sleeve. The woman two seats from Thorne had folded her hands so hard the knuckles had gone the color of tallow. Thorne himself sat as he had sat when the bell rang, upright without support, and only the steward at his shoulder had changed, from patient to attentive. At the words *locked door* her eyes went to Thorne's face for the length of a breath, not to learn what he felt, Sol thought, but to confirm that he had let nothing show. He had not. She looked away first, which Sol had not once seen her do in a month of watching.

"The tap holds because a length of survey iron stands in for a Thorne ingot on the plate. Iron loses weight in wet air. The sum was Renn's, entered in the commission's own hand, and it brings the plate to its surge mark before the month is out." He turned a leaf. "I have not been told the chain has been replaced. The house filed its order for shutting the tap safely, in its master smith's hand, under seal, to be read by the Council surveyor alone. The Council surveyor has been missing since the first week of Cinderveil. I do not say the house arranged that. I say that the only page which shuts the tap in order can today be read by no one, and the Council should weigh that before it weighs the house's offer to keep the lamps lit."

The name went through the chamber the way a dropped tool goes through a workshop, a small hard sound and then everyone looking at the floor. Sol found Tam without meaning to. He stood at the back wall between Ansa and Bram with his sleeves still rolled from the morning's tar, and he was looking at Kael the way he had looked at the withdrawal paper before he signed it.

*First.* It was the word she had not said to Kael, or to Ansa, or to herself with the door shut. A person would have to be the first to sign the new standard, whoever finally wrote it, and stand in front of the Reach and prove it could be survived, and for weeks she had carried the certainty that the person would be her. Tam had put his name to the withdrawal paper on his own, before anyone asked, and the certainty had come loose without finding anything else to be. It sat in her chest at the same weight.

"Second, the instrument. Clause seven passes every contract named in its Schedule to the house administering reform. Clause twelve makes that house's reading of any question touching those contracts final, and bars review. The Accord's article on transfer lets a contract change hands on two conditions and no others, the holder's consent or the sitting auditor's certification. Clause twelve does not repeal the article. It hands the article to the one reader with cause to read it kindly." Kael lowered the page an inch. "I decline to certify. I find that no holder named in the Schedule was asked."

*Named in the Schedule.* Sol's mother was in it, ruled into a single sum with Thorne's own line by one hand. Her mother had signed under the old trade in a hand Sol still knew, in a room where no one asked what she would do if the ward failed, and no one had asked this time either. Kael was already reading that too, evenly, as if it were a weight and not a wound. "Under the Lower Spine the Schedule enters Thorne and Vane side by side and adds them together. House Vane has no chair in this chamber to say whether it consents. I record that the four houses which did not sign the petition were first counted in this room by Isolde Vane, on the twenty-eighth of Sunspire, and that the count is hers."

He had asked her the night before, at Ansa's table, whether he might enter her sums under her own name. She had said yes.

"I enter one thing that is mine." His voice did not change. "The instrument takes effect upon its opening before this Council with its own seal whole. It was opened here, in sight of every house, because I carried it here and guarded it and called that care. The thirty days that end at midnight began with my care. I enter that as my error, under my name, and House Ashworth may answer for it as it likes."

A breath went out in the officers' gallery behind her, let go by a clerk who had not known he was holding it. In the Ashworth chair the eldest of the elders sat with his cane across his knees and his eyes on the far wall, and behind him Ferra opened her steward's book, wrote a single line, and did not look up. On the benches, a lord of the eight lifted his head and looked at the Auditor for the first time since the reading began. Then the woman with the tallow knuckles looked at her own hands, and then at Thorne, and away. At the back wall the junior cousin House Farrow had sent, given no chair because he had been given no authority, wrote three lines on a scrap, folded it, and passed it to a page, who left by the side door at a walk that was trying not to be a run.

Sol tallied. The two houses that had sent to the clerks' office a month ago for copies of their own lines were looking at the floor. The woman made three. She needed five, and there was one part left to read.

"Third, the pegs. Two brass tally pegs, cast from one mold, were found in the cellar under the boiling house. Isolde Vane found the first in the outer chamber on the day the first ward-line was mapped. The second lay on the table in the inner room, past an iron door, after that door had been opened. The commission finds that the second was placed, and placed where any searcher would be certain to find it." He drew a breath. "The commission also finds a second ward-line under that cellar which appears on no plate this office holds. An older surveyor recalls talk of such a line forty years ago, before the Accord, when no one thought it worth chasing."

The Chancellor had not moved since the reading began. Her hands rested flat on the dais rail, and Sol thought she could see her listening in the set of the shoulders, the way a good ward-taker listens to a line before touching it.

"The caster's guild has narrowed the smiths able to cast to that tolerance to one. He is a smith who cast a ceremonial die for House Thorne eight years ago and, by the guild's book, nothing for it since. I give his name to the commission and not to this Council, because a name is a thread and I will not hand a room a rope I have not got." In the commissioners' row Dessa's mouth twitched at the word, the way a teacher's does when a pupil returns her own phrase without knowing it. Kael looked up then, finally, and the look went across the ring to the Thorne bench and stopped there. "I say only this. A house that filed its instrument to the letter of the Accord's own safeguard had, on record, the means to place a peg. The peg was placed. I do not know what it means. I know that it was meant."

He folded the report along its crease.

"That is the finding. It is signed Kael Ashworth, Auditor, and it is entered without the leave of my house."

He looked up at the gallery then, once, and she gave him a nod exactly the size she would have given a colleague who had read a difficult measurement correctly.

Sol had wanted, for perhaps ten heartbeats, to look at her hands, and did not, because the eight were still looking at him and a Council watches whoever is watched. She kept her eyes on the ring and her breathing slow and found that her left leg had gone to sleep under her.

"The finding is entered," the Chancellor said. The thin voice reached the back wall without effort. "The clerks will record the hour."

The clerk called it aloud, and Sol did the sum that everyone in the chamber was doing. From that hour to the midnight bell there was one afternoon and one evening, and five houses to turn.

Malrik Thorne rose without help. He stood a moment with his hand resting on the chair back, not for support, and when he spoke it was in the dry courtesy that Sol had learned was worse than a glare.

"The house has heard the finding, Chancellor, and will answer it when the houses are called." He turned his head toward the ring. "Auditor. I have known a good many men who could enter another man's error against him. I have not often watched one enter his own against his own house in front of every seal in the Reach. It is either very foolish or very deliberate, and I find I cannot tell which."

"Then the record will hold both readings, my lord," Kael said, "until a reader can."

The steward's mouth did not move. Her fingers did. They rose from her folded hands and reached toward the young man at Thorne's other side, the way they had reached in this same chamber a month ago, when a sentence had begun on his lips and ended under the pressure of her hand on his sleeve.

Aldous Thorne stood as they reached him.

He was still pale. Sol had last seen that face over the open Schedule a month ago, the color draining out of it as he found his own name in another man's handwriting. He stood the way he had lowered his grandfather into the chair that morning, carefully, as if the floor might object, and when the steward's hand closed on his sleeve he took it in his own and set it back in her lap. He did it gently. She looked at her hand as though it belonged to a stranger.

"Chancellor," Aldous said. His voice cracked on the title and he began again, louder. "I ask leave to speak to the finding."

The old woman on the dais turned her head. In the gallery Sol felt Kael, a single step below her, go entirely still, and understood that he had not been told, that no one had been told, and that the steward, for the first time in all Sol's watching, had nothing to do with her hands.
