The Kettle Room had steamed itself blind by the time the sledge had been unloaded, and Hester sat at the long table nearest the stove with her hands under her arms and did not look at the man across from her.

She had not meant to be here. She had meant to go home. She had stood on the quay with her fist round the end of her own scarf and heard her name said in a voice she had not heard in nine winters, and then Mina Cray had come down the stones with her sleeves shoved above the elbow and taken her by the arm, and the other one, the one in the grey-blue jumper, by the sleeve, and said, "Tea. Both of you. Now." She had said it in the voice she used to a pan that was about to boil over. Nobody on the quay had argued. Hester had been walked up the lane and in at the door like a lamb in a market, and sat down, and the window had run white behind her.

Mina set the chipped blue teapot on the table. The tea came out of the spout in two threads. She poured one cup, and another, and did not pour a third. She stood with the pot in her hand and looked at the man across the table for the whole length of a breath, with the stillest eyes in Larkmere, and he looked back at her with a rueful tilt to the corner of his mouth and bore it.

"You've grown a belly," said Mina.

"I have grown a great many things. Most of them are on paper."

"Hm." She put the pot down. "I'll be at the counter. I'll be able to hear every word and I'll pretend I can't." She went.

Hester looked at her tea.

Piers Ellory had put his coat over the back of the bench. He sat with his elbows on the scrubbed boards and his hands round the cup, and she did not look at the hands, because she knew what was on them. They were ink to the second joint, stained into the grain of the skin so deeply that no soap would have found the bottom of it. They had been that way when he left. They had been that way when he first came, off a ferry in the last of an autumn, with a roll of paper under his arm and a case of pens. She had thought then that it was the most honest thing about him, a man who could not wash off his own work.

"You haven't changed," he said.

"I have."

"Not at the mouth." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was the same one, wide and tired, with the apology folded up in the corner of it like a handkerchief. "I had a speech. I made it on the sledge, in the dark, under about eleven blankets. It was very good. I've forgotten the whole of it."

"You always did make speeches on sledges."

"I never made one on a sledge in my life. I made one on a ferry, once, and the rail was wet." He laughed at himself, easily, and the laugh was warm and tired, and she found that it had not worn at all. That was the unforgivable part. She had been ready for a stranger. She had been ready for a man gone hard, or fat, or sly, and he had come back exactly himself, a little greyer at the temple, and all her readiness had no place to stand. "I'll say the plain thing. I haven't got another."

"Say it."

"I've come to finish the map of the lake."

She said nothing. Outside, under the window, a child went by shouting, and was hushed.

"I left it half drawn," said Piers. "The north shore and the deep water. I have had it on my table for nine winters, a lake with half its edges missing, and I could not finish it from anywhere but here. I tried. I've drawn a great many other lakes since, very good ones. They were all the wrong shape." He turned the cup. "That's the first thing. I'd like it noted that it is a perfectly good reason, and that I would have come for it alone."

"And the second."

"I wanted to see you."

He said it plainly, to the cup, without the rueful tilt. She looked at him then, directly, for the first time since the quay, and saw that he was frightened. It was a small fright and he was bearing it well, as a man bears a stone in his shoe on a long road, but it was there. He had not put the second reason on the sledge. He had been afraid he would lose his nerve and not come.

She did not know what to do with that. It was not a thing she had made room for.

"The lake is where it was," she said.

"That's the trouble with maps. They keep telling you so."

His sleeve had ridden up as he lifted the cup. It was the left one, and she saw the cuff of it.

It was grey-blue, the worn blue of a slate roof in rain, and it had been knit in a plain rib that had gone to a shine at the edge with nine winters of lying on a drawing table. The wool had frayed along the turn. The ink had got into it, a faint stain along the ribbing, the shape of a hand that has wiped itself on a cuff ten thousand times without thinking. It was the one warm jumper she had ever made. She knew every inch of it. She had made it in the first autumn, in a month of nights, with the lamp low and her breath held, and she had never made another.

She had not meant to put out her hand. It went. It went across the boards between the two cups, the right one, and the thumb came down on the cuff of his sleeve, along the turn of the rib, in the place where she tested every yarn that came into her house.

It was warm.

Not from the tea. Not from his wrist, which was a man's wrist, a living one, with a pulse in it she could feel beating a slow even stroke under the wool. It was warm in the wool itself. It was warm in the rib, in the second row from the edge, where her own hand had set it, and it ran into her thumb at once with a plain steady warmth, and she knew whose hand had put it there. She knew the hand as she knew her own name. It was a little looser at the turn than she knit now. It had a habit she had since put down, a small easy slack on the purl side, the hand of a woman nine winters younger who had not yet been taught to count.

Nine winters. It had been over that ground in a sledge, and under that sun, and in the rain of cities she had never seen, and it was warm at the cuff. It had not gone cold. It had not dimmed. It had not been unpicked by the years or worn thin by the wearer's forgetting. Warmth, once set, was set. That was the whole of the third thing, and she had known it all her life, and she had not known it until that moment with her thumb on the proof.

"It has never once been cold," said Piers. He watched her hand with a kind of wondering patience. "I want you to know that. Not on the road. Not in the ice storms, not on the hill passes. I've been quite insufferable about it. I wear it in the rain and tell people it's the sweater."

"It is wool," said Hester.

"It's a good deal more than wool."

She did not answer. She sat with her thumb on his cuff, and the warmth coming up through it into the white of her hand, and the whole of her mind went down to the bottom of a well.

*Warm things go.*

She had built nine winters on it. It lay in her like the foundation row of a garment, flat and low and not to be examined. A warm thing was a thing to lose. It went out of the door on a man's back, or across a lake in the cold. And here was the warm thing, nine winters on, as warm as the day she cast off, and it had not gone. It had stayed set in every thread of itself. It had been faithful to the last stitch. It was only the man who had gone.

The warm thing she had made once had not left her.

The man had.

She sat there with that, and it was very large, and she could not get it into any shape she could hold. It was not a comfort and it was not the opposite. It was a plain fact, as true as gauge, and it undid something in the middle of her as a pulled thread undoes a row, quietly, a stitch at a time, all the way down.

"Hester," said Piers, very gently. "I owe you an account of the thaw."

"Not today."

"No. I didn't think so." He did not take his arm away. He let it lie where it was, cuff under her thumb, as a man lets a bird sit on his hand. "I wanted to say that I would."

The door opened.

A blade of cold came in off the lane and went round the room, and the steam went white and sideways, and a head came in round the frame, a head in a lumpy blue scarf with snow on its hair.

"Mina! I'm so sorry, I won't stay, I have been sent." Ansel's face was pink with cold and his eyes were bright, and he was laughing before he had got the door fully open. "Your letters. The post has left them in a heap on the quay stones and the children have appointed themselves guards. I believe the smallest girl has a stick. I am told nobody is to touch them until the proprietress comes in person, on pain of—" He saw the table. He stopped.

He took it in. She watched him do it. He was a man who read a room as he read a face, quickly and without any fuss, and he read it now from the doorway: the two cups, the stranger in the thin jumper, the ink on the hands, and her own right hand lying across the boards with the thumb pressed to a cuff. His eyes went to it and away in the space of a blink. And he smiled.

It was a very good smile. It was open and delighted and without a thread of anything behind it, and she hated it, with her whole heart, at once.

"I beg your pardon," he said. "I did not know there was company."

"Ansel Brandt," said Mina from the counter, without turning round, "the schoolmaster. Piers Ellory. He's come on the sledge."

"So I saw." Ansel came across the floor with his hand out. The chalk dust lay white along both of his cuffs and a little on the back of his wrist, and the hand was warm and large and plain, and it went out to a stranger as it would have gone out to a child dropped on his doorstep. "How do you do. I'm afraid I am covered in chalk. It comes with the post."

"Ink," said Piers, and took it, and laughed. The two hands met over the end of the table, the one stained in and the other dusted over, and held. "I'm afraid we are in the same line, you and I. We mark surfaces for a living."

"I have always said so! Though mine rubs out."

"That is the great virtue of yours."

She watched them. She sat with her thumb on the cuff and the two hands clasped above her tea, and she could not help it, she saw them side by side. She saw the ink, dark and deep, gone into the grain to the second joint, which would not wash off in a lifetime. She saw the chalk, white and dry and light as a breath, that would be on the nearest coat by the end of the day and in the lane by evening. One stayed and one went. She saw a hand that marked things so that they lasted and a hand that gave them away before they could be marked.

She hated the comparison. She hated it more than she had hated anything since the quay, the plain cold fairness of it, the way her mind had set the two down on the table and weighed them without asking leave. It was a thing a person did to a skein. She had no right to do it to a man. She had no right to do it to two.

"You must be frozen," said Ansel, still holding the hand. "Have you eaten? You have not eaten. Mina, soup, I think, I will answer for it."

"He's had tea."

"Then soup after the tea. Do you have a roof? If the quay has not found you a roof, I have a stone cottage by the school with a stove in it and a very cold floor, and nobody to mind either one." He said it quickly, easily, as he said everything. "It is no trouble at all. I would be glad of the company."

"That's very kind," said Piers. He was looking at Ansel with open wonder, and under the wonder, with something warmer. He had liked him in the first breath. She could see that. Everyone did. "I think I've a bed at the Kettle Room. But I'll remember it."

"And you must have my scarf, if you want it. You are in a jumper."

"I'm perfectly warm, thank you. I'm told it's the sweater." Piers looked at the blue scarf, at the great knot under Ansel's chin. "That's a fine scarf."

"It is the worst in the county." Ansel laughed, and this time the laugh had a flat clear note in it, like a coin on a slate. "I am extremely proud of it."

He let go of the hand. He turned to the counter and said something to Mina about the stick, and she answered him gruffly, and he laughed again, and moved to the door. He was going. He stopped with one hand on the latch and looked back across the steaming room at the table, at the two cups, at her face, and for the space of a breath his own face did not say anything at all.

"Good day," said Ansel, and went out.

The door shut. The cold went with him. And she felt it, as plain as a hand on her sleeve: a pull toward the door, quite distinct, from somewhere under her ribs, as a thread is drawn through a seam from the far side. It was a small tug and a steady one. It said that he had gone out of the warm room into the white lane in his three coats with his chalked cuffs, and that she was sitting here. She stayed where she was. Her eyes went to the dusting of white he had left on the boards where his sleeve had brushed the table's edge, and then to the cuff under her thumb, and she kept her eyes there.

"He's a good man," said Piers, quietly.

"Yes."

"He looked at you."

"He looks at everyone."

"Hester." He did not move his arm. His voice was very kind. "I don't think he does."

She did not answer. The window ran white. At the counter Mina was very busy with a cup, and was very careful not to be.

Across the table the warm cuff lay under her thumb as it had lain for nine winters, faithful to the last stitch, and she felt it go on being warm, and she knew it would go on being warm when she was gone. She had put it there. She had set it herself. It asked nothing and it did not change and it had never once left her.

She lifted her thumb from the cuff. She did it slowly, as one lifts a needle from a finished row. He did not stop her, and he did not follow her hand with his, and she saw that he would have let her keep it there until the lamps were lit.

She was the one who let go first.
