The girl came into the workroom holding a flat tin out in front of her in both hands, with her elbows locked and her eyes on the far end of it, the way a child holds a skein for winding.

Hester did not look up from the rack. She had a length of grey lambswool across her thumb and was drawing it through, slowly, feeling for the thin place, and she finished the draw before she said, "You are late. The lamps were lit an hour ago."

"I went for the blankets."

"That was yesterday."

"I know." Tilda came farther in. The tongue was between her teeth, and she took it out to speak and put it back. She had been crying at some point on the way, or had been cold, or both, and her nose was red at the tip like a thumb pressed on a seam. "I didn't mean to look. I want that said. I went for the blankets, and they were in the chest, and the chest was at the foot of the bed, and I had to lift the pillow to get my arm round the corner of the lid. And it was there. It's not as if I went hunting."

"What is?"

The girl brought the tin the last three steps and held it out across the bench.

It was a plain flat tin, the sort that holds a dozen cough lozenges, dented along one long edge as though it had been sat on, or dropped from a height onto a quay. The paint was worn through to the metal at the corners where a thumb went. Hester set the lambswool down. She did not take the tin at once. A bad feeling had come up in her at the sight of it, out of the same low place under the ribs where Mina's sentence had gone in, and she stood with her cold hands flat on the bench on either side of her work and looked at the thing in the child's hands as one looks at a dropped stitch three rows below the needle.

"You opened it."

"I shut it again." The words came out in a rush. "It isn't stealing if you bring it back, and I couldn't bring it back to him, could I, he'd have laughed, he'd have said it was nothing, and it isn't nothing, there's wool in it. It's your wool. Isn't it? I thought it was. I knew it. It's got your first row on it." She stopped and swallowed. "That's the wrong sort of first row. Nobody does it like that, except a person who's only just learned."

"Give it to me."

The tin was lighter than she had expected and, along the bottom, warm.

She noticed that before anything else. She had thought it would be cold, tin and iron and the thaw standing in the walls, and the metal had in it the faint steady warmth of a stone that has been lying all day in the sun. It came up through her fingers. It was not much. It was the warmth of a thing that had been kept for a very long time against a body and had not quite let go of the habit.

Overhead, in the loft, a cane struck the boards once.

She did not look up. She knew who was on the stair. She had known for a minute, as a person knows a cat has come into a room, by the stopping of a floorboard, and she had said nothing about it, and Gran had said nothing back. Hester turned the tin over in her hands, and found the lid with her thumbnail, and lifted it.

It was a small scrap. It lay at the bottom on a bed of nothing, folded once, no bigger than the palm of a child's hand. Undyed grey. The honest grey of a ewe's back before anyone has had an opinion about it. She lifted it out between two fingers, and it opened as it left the tin, the way a leaf opens, and she saw the edge.

The first row was twisted.

She would have known it in the dark. It was a cast-on in which every stitch had been put onto the needle the wrong way about, so that the base of the piece ran in a little corkscrew ridge instead of a clean chain. Everybody did it who had ever learned. It was the mistake of the first week. A knitter made it once, was shown it once, and never made it again for as long as she lived, and Hester had made it exactly once. She could see the place where she had known she was wrong and gone on regardless. She could see the little tight knot at the join where a small angry hand had pulled the yarn harder than it needed to. It was her own mistake. It had her whole signature on it, as a letter has, and it was the winter she was twelve.

She remembered nothing of giving it.

She tried. She stood in the lamplight with the scrap spread across her flat palm and went back through the winters as she went back through a garment for a fault, row by row, looking for the place. There was a quay somewhere in it. There was cold, the cold of the early morning with the ferry coming in. There were a great many people. She could not find a boy. She could not find her own arm going out. It was a blank, a smooth place like a section of fabric knit in a plain stitch and then pulled flat, and the harder she looked the smoother it got.

But she remembered how it felt to make it.

That came up in her instead of the other, and it came all at once, like a held breath released. She felt the hours of it. She felt a child's hunched back and a lamp too low, and the needles too long for her hands, and a great fierce small wish that went into every loop. It was only that. It was the wish that a thing she made should be enough. She had not known, at twelve, what she was wishing it for, and she had not cared. She had pushed it into the grey with her thumbs, row on row, as hard as she could, and the scrap lay on her palm now, twenty-odd winters old, and it was warm.

It was warm as a bird's back is warm. Faint, and steady, and quite undiminished.

Wool holds the honest feeling of the hands that made it. She had told a hundred customers so, in a voice she kept for the purpose, and she had never once stood in a room and felt it hold. The wool had waited all this while. It had lain in a tin under a man's pillow in a stone cottage through a whole winter of weather and kept a child's meaning at the temperature of a body, as faithfully as the day she had cast off, and nobody had told it that the child was gone.

"Is it yours?" said Tilda.

"It is a mistake of mine."

"I know that. But is it yours?"

"Yes."

"He had it under his pillow." The girl's voice had gone very small. "Why would he keep it under his pillow?"

Hester closed her hand.

The scrap went into the dark of her fist, and the warmth of it spread into the heel of her palm and the root of her thumb, the first place in her whole hand to be warm without anyone else's help. She stood very still and let it. Then she opened her fingers, and folded the scrap in half and in half again exactly as it had been folded, and laid it in the tin, and set the lid down with her thumbnail until it clicked.

"Go and sleep," she said.

"But"

"I will see to it. Go on."

The girl went. She went backward at first, watching Hester's face, and then she turned and ran, and the door banged on the lane.

Hester stood with the tin in her hands.

"Take it back to him yourself," said Gran, from the top of the stair.

She had not heard her come down a step. She turned, and the old woman was there in the dark at the stairhead, a small bent shape in a shawl with one hole in it, with her two hands on the ash cane and the lamplight finding the tip of her nose. She did not say another word. She looked at the tin, and she looked at Hester, and the whole of her long hard face said that she had known where it was for a good while and had been waiting for a child to be nosy.

"It is not mine to return."

"Then it will be a pleasure to be told so." The cane tapped once. "Go on, girl. He will be at his slates."

She went.

The night was clear and soft and loud with water. The thaw had come in properly while she was at the bench, and every roof along Spindle Lane dripped into the dark in a hundred different notes, and the snow in the lane had gone to grey slush that took her boots to the ankle. Down on the lake the ice spoke. It was a long deep note, with a crack at the end of it like a thread parting, and the sound came up through the soles of her feet and was gone. She went along the shore road with the tin inside her coat against her ribs. It lay there the whole way, and it was warm, and she could feel it at every step like a hand.

The lamp was lit in the school.

She did not knock. She opened the door and stood in it, and the stove ticked, and the long room lay in its brown light with the desks in rows and the slate at the end washed clean. He was sitting at the master's table in his coat with his back to the board and a pile of children's slates at his elbow. He had the chalk in his fingers and had not been using it. When he looked up, the whole of his face went still, quite slowly, as a lamp goes down when the wick is turned.

He was wearing the Grey. She saw that first. She saw the collar of it above the coat, the flawless undyed neck, and under his eyes the color she had hoped not to see again.

She crossed the room and set the tin on the table between the slates.

He did not touch it. He looked at it. He looked at the dent along its edge and the worn corners, and then he looked up at her, and she saw him not say any of the things that were ready in his mouth. It was the strangest sight she had seen in him yet, the laugh standing up and being refused.

"Tilda brought it to me," said Hester. "She found it when she went for the blankets. She did not take it. She asked me to say that."

"I see."

"I opened it."

"I thought you might have."

"I know this row," she said.

She opened the lid. She took out the scrap and laid it across her palm in the lamplight, where he could see the little corkscrew of the first row and the clean ordinary rows above it, and the grey with the lamp in it, and she held it out to him. She did not push. She held it at the length of her arm and let him see it whole.

"I made this."

"Yes."

"It is the winter I was twelve. I do not remember giving it to you." She heard her own voice, flat and level and dry, and under it the other thing, the long unspooling of it. "I remember nothing about you. I have looked. I have stood in my own workroom for an hour trying to find a boy on the quay and he is not there, and I can tell you the weight of the needles. I can tell you what I wished while I made it. I cannot tell you who it was for."

He stood. He stood slowly, with the table at his hip, and his hands came down to his sides and stayed there, loose, open, the palms a little forward, the hands of a man who has put down everything he was carrying and is waiting to see what happens. She had never seen them still. She had only ever seen them doing something, moving a chalk or a cup or a coat, giving it away. They hung beside him now with the fingers a little curled, empty, like two things he did not know what to do with.

"I was on your quay for one winter," said Ansel. "When I was a boy. The cold boy, the one nobody could warm. A girl came down to the water with this in her fist and she put it in my hand. She did not say a word. She went back up the quay." He drew a breath. It shook a little at the top. "I was very cold, Hester. And then, for a while, I was not."

The thaw dripped on the roof. The ice spoke out on the lake, long and low.

"You give away everything," she said. It was hardly more than a breath. "I asked you yesterday to keep one thing. And you stood there and said there was nothing."

"I know."

"And it was under your pillow."

The scrap lay on her palm between them, warm, in the lamp. His hands were open at his sides. He was looking at it, and not at her, with an expression she did not have a name for in any trade she knew.

"I have never given it away," said Ansel.
