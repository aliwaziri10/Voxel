The lanterns had begun to gutter by the time the sky changed, and Hester had been awake for every minute of the night that led up to it.

She sat apart. She had gone out of the ring when the town lay down, without a word, as a person leaves a room where a decision has been taken, and found the nearest of the Ice Road poles and sat at the foot of it with her back to the post. She knew the foot of that pole. It was packed round with gravel and a turn of rope, frozen now into a hard collar, and she did not think about who had done it.

Behind her the ring had closed. That was the way of the longest night. They stood until the stars turned, and then the old sat, and then the children slept in their mothers' laps, and then without anyone seeming to decide it the whole circle lay down together on the ice in their Winter Knits and their blankets, close as skeins in a basket, and slept until the sun came up. It lay behind her now in one long soft heap. She could hear it breathe. A great many sleepers make a sound like a tide going out over shingle, slow and uneven and entirely without anxiety. Tilda lay curled in the crook of Mina's arm with her patched boots drawn up and her mouth open. Mina lay flat on her back with her arms folded, even in sleep, as though she were guarding the lantern at her head. Gran lay under two blankets with the cane across her chest like a sword on a tomb, and was snoring in a high, thin, satisfied note.

Hester had her hands in her lap and the grey scarf at her jaw, and she was ashamed.

It was not pride. She told herself that, and went over it, and found that it held. She had made the Grey in a single night, and it was the best piece of work she had ever put on a needle. Every stitch lay in its place. Not a row of it was out of true. It had stood on that man's back through the whole of the longest night while the color went out of his lips, and a child of eleven who could not keep an edge straight had walked across the ice with a lump of blue wool and put it round his neck, and he had begun to smoke like a kettle. She had watched the steam go up. She had known which of the two would win from the moment Tilda took her first step into the ring, and she had stood where she stood and let it happen, with her hands folded, because there was nothing in the whole of her art that could have stopped it.

Wool kept what was in the hands that knit it. It kept it row by row, whether the knitter would or no. Tilda's rows were crooked and every one of them was meant, and the Grey's rows were straight and not one of them was. That was the whole of the matter. She had known it since before she could hold a needle steady, and she had never been made to look at it in front of a town.

And it did not change. That was the other half, and the worse. A knit did not learn. It did not wear down to a kinder temper with the years or take on the warmth of the body it lay against. It stayed what it had been made. The Grey would be as cold in a hundred years as it had been on the first morning, and Tilda's scarf would be warm when the child was grey herself, and nobody had ever found a way to argue with that. She had tried to find one all night. She had sat at the foot of the pole with her cold hands in her lap and tried every road, and every road came out at the same plain fact: it was warm, and she could not say that it was not.

She heard him before she saw him.

He came across the ice from the north lantern with a careful step, the step of a man who has learned the surface by falling on it. There was no hurry in it. He had his coats on, all of them, open now at the throat, and under them the edge of the Grey, and round his neck, knotted in a fist under the chin, a thick blue scarf with a lump in it the size of an apple. His color had come back. It stood in his cheeks, high and a little ragged, and the blue had gone from his mouth. He looked like a man who has slept a whole winter in a single night and been woken by a friend. She hated how well it suited him.

He carried two cups. They were thick tin cups with the steam going up out of them in two thin threads, and he held them in front of him in both hands as a boy carries a full bowl, with his eyes on the brim.

He did not ask. He lowered himself onto the ice at the foot of the pole beside her, which took some doing in so many coats, and held one of the cups out.

"Mina left a can by the north lantern," he said. "It is still very nearly hot. I have been drinking it since the stars turned, and I find that it is the first night of my life I have been glad of a cup." He laughed, short and easy, before she had said anything at all. "I am told it is tea. I have my doubts."

She took the cup. Her fingers had been cold all night, white to the nail, and the tin burned them, and she held on.

"You did not sleep," said Hester.

"No. I stood until the stars turned, and then I found that I was warm, and I could not think what to do with it." He looked out at the ice. "It is a great nuisance, being warm. I had quite forgotten."

She said nothing. She drank. The tea was very strong and tasted of the can.

Behind them the ring shifted. It was a small sound at first, a rustle of blankets, the grunt of a sleeper turning over. A boy of seven or so, who had been lying a pace off with his head on his arm, rolled in his sleep and came to rest against the sole of Ansel's boot, and lay there. A net-mender on her other side, a broad man in a brown jumper, turned heavily toward the warmth of the two cups and the two living people and drew in his knees, and his back came to rest against her shoulder like a bolt of cloth set down against a wall. A blanket edge crept across her lap. The ring was closing. It did it without any waking, as a sleeping body draws in toward a stove, and in the space of a dozen slow breaths the little clear place at the foot of the pole had filled up with the town, and there was no room left between her and Ansel at all.

They sat shoulder to shoulder. She felt his arm lie along hers from the shoulder to the elbow, through two coats and a jumper, a long solid weight. She did not move away. There was nowhere to move to.

"I am sorry," said Ansel.

She waited. He was looking at the cup in his hands, and when he spoke again it came low and plain, without any lift in it.

"I am sorry the scarf was not yours."

It went into her like a needle going into a row. She did not turn her head. She sat with the cup between her cold hands and the sleeping town breathing round her and looked at the line of the far shore, which was beginning to be a line, a grey seam sewn between the white of the lake and the paler white of the sky.

"It was a good scarf," she said.

"It is dreadful. It has a knot in it the size of an apple."

"It is warm."

"It is very warm." He drew a breath, and she felt it in his arm. "I have been standing in it for a great many hours, and I have not once been cold. I do not understand it. I should like very much to understand it. But I am sorry, all the same."

She did not ask him what for. She knew what for. It sat between them on the ice, plain as the cups.

She saw it, then, quite clearly, as she saw a finished row: her own blue wool, the Blue, the one that lay at home on the long table under its cloth, with its shoulders warm and its sleeves unknit, round his neck and shoulders in the first light, and the steam going up off him, and his face. She saw it as a person sees a thing she has been refusing to look at, all at once and in full. She would rather he had worn hers. That was the plain fact, and she set it down in front of herself and looked at it. She would rather, a great deal rather, that the thing which had done that to his face had come out of her own two hands, and she did not want to want it. It was the wanting she could not bear, more than the loss. A person who wanted a thing was a person who could be made to hold it out, and be seen to hold it out, and wait.

If she gave him a warm one she would have to mean it.

That was the whole of it. A warm jumper was a thing that could not be made politely. Every row of it would say what she was thinking when she knit it, in a hand that any person with a palm could read, and she would have to stand at his door in the cold with it in her arms and let him put it on. She would have to let the whole town see what it said. Tilda could do it. Tilda was eleven, and had not yet learned there was anything to be afraid of in a plain row.

"Your hands," said Ansel.

She looked down. She had the cup in both of them, and the white was in her fingers still, bloodless to the second joint. They were shaking a little, with the cold or with something else.

"They are always cold," she said.

"They were not, in the lane."

She did not answer. In the lane the wind had screamed over the top of them and her fists had been closed in his coat, and her fingers had been warm. She had not said it then, and she did not say it now. But she did not take her hands from round the cup, and he did not look away, and after a moment he lifted one of his own hands from his knee and laid it over both of hers, lightly, over the cold knuckles and the hot tin, and left it there.

It was warm. It was a large plain hand with chalk worked into the creases of it and a strip of grey linen round the wrist, and it was warm to the bone. She had held that hand's cold on the roof. She felt the heat go through the tin and through her skin and into the white of her fingers, slowly, as the blood comes back to a limb that has been lain on, with the whole ache of it, and she sat still and let it.

"It is the scarf," said Ansel, quietly, to the ice. "I know it is. It is not mine. I have not earned a thread of it, and I know it. But it is a great deal warmer than I have been, and I thought you might have some of it."

She almost smiled. It was a thing in her face that began and was put down. "It is a bad scarf."

"The worst in the county."

"The knot is a disgrace."

"A scandal." He laughed, softly, and it went through his arm into hers. "I intend to be buried in it."

The first thin line of light came over the far shore.

It came flat and gold, a single blade of it laid along the ice from the east, and everything it touched took fire. The poles of the Ice Road, which had stood in the dark all night with their frost-capped lanterns, flushed one after another down the long line of the lake as though a hand were passing along them. The guttering lanterns in the ring went pale and foolish in it. A sleeper stirred and flung out an arm. Far off the ice gave a single clear note, high and long, like a plucked string, and was still. The whole lake blushed under it from edge to edge, white to rose, and the snow along the shore lit up in a thin bright line.

The sun cleared the far shore. It came up all at once, round and enormous and without any warmth in it, and laid its light across the faces of the sleeping town, and across the hands that held the cups.

His shoulder was warm against hers. It had been warm for some while, she saw, through all the layers, a plain deep heat that was not of her making and was no less true for that. It lay along her arm from the shoulder to the elbow. She did not lean into it. She did not move away. She sat at the foot of the pole with her hands under his and the sun coming up in her eyes and the town sleeping round her, and let it be warm.
