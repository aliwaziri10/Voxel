The Heron went into the water on a morning so mild that the Net Sheds dripped from every shingle.

Hester stood at the head of the slip with a brown paper parcel under her arm and watched the ferry come down on her cradle, slowly, with a great deal of noise and no apology, as she had come into the harbor in the autumn. The doors of the Net Sheds stood open to their full width. Beyond the slip the harbor ice had gone the color of old tallow, and along the shore edge it had stopped being ice at all. A lane of black water lay open there, wide as a boat and bright as a needle. The quay folk had come down in twos and threes to see it. They kept to the far end of the planks with their hands in their sleeves, and not one of them looked toward her.

The hull took the water with a long breath. She rocked, and rode, and the lines went taut and slack and taut again, and the sound came back off the shed wall, the hollow knock of a thing that floats against a thing that does not. She would be tried down the lane and back. By the look of the lake she would be tried on open water before the poles were out of the Ice Road.

Hester's hands had gone cold. She noticed it as she noticed a dropped stitch, late and all at once. The morning was warm enough that the town stood about with its collars open, and her fingers had gone the white of undyed wool. The thaw was coming into every doorway in Larkmere, and it had gone round hers.

She had been holding her breath. She knew it by the ache under her ribs, and she let the breath out, and it came short and hard and would not settle to any gauge. All winter the lake had held him. The toll was the lake's grip and the Winter Knit was the town's, and the shut door of the whole season had been a plain fact: a man who came off the boat in the last of autumn stayed in Larkmere until the thaw, whatever he thought of the arrangement. Now the boat was in the water. The cold was coming off him from the edges in, and she had put her own mouth on the edge, and she stood on the planks with her heart pulling like a row cast on too tight to work.

She shifted the parcel under her arm. It was square and light and weighed more than anything she had ever carried.

He came along the quay at the pace of a man who has been told where to be. The Green hung from his shoulders, and the left cuff, she saw, was a shade darker with the damp, and the collar lay open at his throat. He wore no coat. He saw the Heron and his face did what it did, and opened, and she watched it close again as he saw her. He laughed before he spoke. It was a flat laugh, a ruled line with nothing under it to push against.

"She floats," said Ansel.

"She was always going to."

"I had hoped for a dramatic leak." He stopped an arm's length off. It was exactly an arm's length, and she thought that it was a very good one and that she had taught it to him. "Have they said when?"

"The ice has to speak first." She heard her own voice, level as a rule laid along a seam. "I have a thing for you."

She held the parcel out in both hands, by the far edges of the paper, so that the near edge came toward him and her fingers stayed well back. It was the correct way to hand over a parcel. She had handed over a great many.

He took it. His hands came up and closed on the paper where she had left room for them, and he took the weight with great care, as a man takes a sleeping animal out of a basket. Not one finger touched hers. She watched the hand's breadth of morning air between them stay a hand's breadth, and then her arms were empty and cold to the elbow.

"A jumper," said Ansel.

"It is."

"I have two."

"This is the third."

He turned the paper back at one corner with his thumb. Charcoal wool lay folded beneath it, close and flat and even, each stitch the twin of the one before. She had knit it in the nights since the evening in his cottage, at the long table, with the tape's numbers in her head, and every number was correct. The shoulder, at the very point where her mouth had been. The sleeve to the wrist bone. The breadth of the chest under her two palms. She had put each of them into the wool at gauge, and not one of them had been given. The cold of it came up through the paper and she saw it reach his wrists. He did not flinch. That was the worst thing she saw that morning. He took the cold of it into his bare hands as though it were a kindness, and went on holding it.

"It is perfect," said Ansel. "Thank you."

"It is what it was made to be."

"I'm sure it is." He smiled. It was a good smile and it had been very carefully put on. "I shall wear it at once, at the first opportunity. Please tell no one how pleased I am. I should hate to be spoiled."

It was the voice from the quay. It was the voice of the man in four coats who had given a blanket away before he reached the end of the planks, and she had spent a winter taking it apart a row at a time, and she heard it go back on, whole, in the space of a breath. She stood in her own scarf with her chin in the wool and her hands up her sleeves and was precisely what she had been when the Heron came in. Correct. They were both exactly themselves. It was like seeing a ripped row put back on the needle, every stitch seated and nothing in it holding.

The Heron knocked against her cradle. Out on the lake the ice spoke once, low and long.

"Well," said Ansel. He did not bow. He tucked the parcel under his arm against the Green, where the paper rustled, and she saw the cold begin to go into him through the wool and the shirt. "Good morning, Hester."

"Good morning."

He walked back the way he had come. She watched his back, because she knew the measure of it, the breadth between the shoulders, and it grew smaller with every plank until the quay folk closed round the place where he had been. Then she turned and watched the Heron ride. She did not see her at all.

The workroom had no lamp lit when she came in, only the stove, opened a hand's breadth, and its red slit lay across the long table and the bare boards. She did not bank it. She sat on the end of the bench with her coat still on and her hands between her knees and listened to the house, and it made the sound a house makes when nobody in it is knitting.

The second hook by the door held nothing. She had hung her coat on the first. She looked at the empty one for a long time without deciding anything about it.

Gran came down the loft stair one tread at a time, the cane going ahead of her like a feeler. She lowered herself to the stool by the stove. The copper kettle ticked on the hob, and the red light found the hole at the edge of her shawl.

"You gave him the cold one," said Gran.

"I gave him the one I could finish."

"Mm. And he thanked you."

"He said it was perfect."

"He would." Gran folded her hands on the crook of the cane. "I will tell you a thing once, and then I'll go up."

"You always say that."

"And I always go up." Her hand went to the shawl. One bent finger found the edge of the hole and rested in it, as it might have rested in a ring. "A man came off the Heron once, in the last of autumn, with the whole lake in him. I was the Winter Knitter. I was younger than you are and a good deal surer of myself, and I knit him a jumper to gauge. Not a flaw in it. He wore it and told the town it was wonderful."

Hester did not move.

"It was cold as a doorstep. He was cold in it all winter, and so was I, because I was afraid of him. Not of what he was. Of what he would do. Men go. They stay, and then they go. They die, and that is a going too, and neither of us is asked." The finger moved in the hole and was still. "Wool does not go. Wool stays exactly where you put it."

"Gran."

"The next winter I knit him another. I dropped a stitch. On purpose, at the left seam, where his arm would lift it. I stood at this table with half of Larkmere waiting on me, and let one loop slide, and watched it run down a row, and I have never been so frightened in my life, or done a better day's work."

The stove ticked. Hester found that she was looking at the finger in the hole.

"And that."

"I put that in myself, the winter after he died. With a needle, in my own shawl." Gran's voice did not change. "I have not mended it and I will not."

"He died."

"He did. Warm things do not leave, Hester. People do. It is the one fact in the trade, and I should never have had it if I'd kept on knitting cold. I had that winter, and the ones after. His jumper is in the chest upstairs, and it is warm at the cuff." She lifted her chin, and her eyes were as dry and clear as ever they had been on the quay. "And I put you in a cold room with that boy on purpose. I stood on the planks and told the whole town. You would have knit him a Grey and handed it over with your mouth shut."

"I did."

"You did. I wanted you too close to be correct."

"You might have asked me."

"I might. You'd have said no with the door shut." Gran took up the cane and got to her feet, by stages, with the iron patience of a very old woman who has had her say. "You have read the page. I'll not read it to you twice. The cream is on the third pole, at the end. It has been there since the autumn. I put it there."

She went to the loft stair. The treads took her weight, one and then the next, and a door closed above, gently, as though the house were being asked to keep another confidence.

Hester sat on with her hands between her knees. She sat until the red slit on the boards had crept a hand's width toward her, and then she stood.

The third pole stood at the end of the racks, past the leaf green and the fawn, where the light from the stove just reached. The cream hung there in a long soft loop, undyed, untouched, the color of milk with the day's skin still on it. She lifted a hank down. She drew a length of it across the pad of her thumb, slowly, as she did with every yarn that came into the house, and it was cool and dry and a plain two-ply, a little soft in the twist, exactly what wool is. There was nothing in it to fault. There was nothing in it at all.

She wound it into a ball. Her turns were not quick and they were not even, and she let them be uneven and did not correct them. The ball came out lumpish and fat in the middle. She looked at it in her palm and found she did not mind.

The needles lay where she had left them, in the long table's drawer. She took out two. She stood in the red light with the ball in one hand and the iron in the other and thought of a man in a charcoal jumper, going down the shore path under a thawing sky, with the cold of her work in his bare arms and every kind word he owned laid on top of it. She had not told him one true thing. She had measured him twice, and he had stood still for it both times with his arms out, and she had given him nothing back.

She did not name what she was about to make. The name came by itself, and she let it stand.

She made the slip knot. It was a thing her hands knew, and they had never needed her eyes for it in all her winters. She watched them. She watched the loop close, and the first stitch go onto the needle, a small cream curve against the iron, and a voice at the long table she had heard once in this room said, Do you ever look?

"Yes," said Hester, to the empty workroom.

The stitch lay on the needle. It was not warm yet. It was not cold either. She put her thumb on it and did not take the thumb away, and behind her the stove stood open, and she cast on the next.
