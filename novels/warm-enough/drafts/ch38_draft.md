The last of the Open Door came off the needles a little after the lamp guttered, and Hester did not trust it.

She laid it across the long table and made herself look at it as she would have looked at any other jumper that came off her hands, from the hem up. It was cream, the undyed cream from the third pole, and it lay on the scrubbed boards in the red light of the stove with its sleeves folded in against its ribs like a sleeping thing. She had cast on with her thumb on the first loop and had not unpicked a row since. She had knit each one with her attention on the wool and her mind on a man in a green jumper walking down Spindle Lane without a coat, and the rows had come out different from each other, a hair looser, a hair tighter, as a hand's grip varies with what is in the hand. The gauge wandered. She had let it. Her fingers could tell which rows she had knit for the work and which she had knit for him, and she did not ask them to be more exact about it than that.

She drew the cuff across her thumb.

It was warm. Not faintly, as the cut length of yarn had been in her coat pocket, nor in a single row that had frightened her. The warmth ran through the whole of the cuff and up the sleeve and across the shoulder, a plain steady heat set in the wool by the hands that made it, and it did not stop at her skin. It came into her fingers. They went pink at the ends and then nearer red, and she sat with her hand lying on the sleeve and her heart going hard in her wrists, and did not take the hand away.

The left side seam was last.

She had left it open for the length of a hand, below the arm, with a loop held on a pin. She took the pin out. The loop sat on her fingertip, a small cream curve, one stitch of the thousands, and she knew everything about it. She knew that a stitch let go would run. She knew the way it would run, down the fabric and then no farther, because she had made the same drop on a swatch a hand's width wide and it had run exactly the length of her finger and stopped. She had stood in this room and watched it run, and been afraid, and put the swatch in his hands.

It was the hardest thing she had ever done with her hands, and it took no strength at all. She opened her fingers.

The loop slipped. It ran down one row and then the next, and held, and lay there as a small open place at the edge of the seam where the wool did not close, where nothing had been made tight. The jumper was flawed. It was not correct. Anyone in Larkmere could have found the fault from across the room, and she looked at it with her hand flat on the sleeve beside it and felt, instead of shame, the warmth come up through the gap and lie against her wrist like a breath.

She sewed the seam to either side of it and not across it. She wove in her ends. She stood.

The house was quiet. The loft above was quiet. Gran slept, or lay with her eyes shut and gave a very good account of sleeping. Hester wrapped the jumper in brown paper, as she had wrapped the charcoal one, and found that her hands shook and that she could not for her life have said whether it was fear or the other thing. Beyond the window the lake was making a noise like a very large animal turning over in the dark.

She did not decide to go. She found that she had taken the coat from the first hook and put the parcel under it, against her ribs.

The shingle was the way with no doorways. It was black and wet and loud with water, and the town lay up the slope behind her with every window dark. The thaw had gone on all day, and by night it had begun to hold its breath. The ice out on the lake had changed color. In the light of the lanterns on the poles of the Ice Road it was no longer white but the dull blue-grey of a fleece left out in the rain, and it sagged between the poles, and along the shore edge a wide black seam of open water ran away from her in both directions like a split along a hem.

She walked to the end of the path where the shingle gave way to the old boards at the foot of the school. It was the place where she could see the whole length of the Ice Road run out into the dark, and she did not know she had chosen it until she saw that another person had chosen it before her.

He stood at the water's edge in the Green, with the lumpy blue scarf wound at his throat. He had his back to her. His breath went up in a thin white thread and the lantern on the nearest pole lit the edge of his collar, and the left cuff, and the chalk dust at the wrist. He stood with his shoulders a little hunched, and she saw one of them was dipped lower than the other, the way a man stands who is holding a thing behind him and does not wish it to show.

She stopped. Her feet knew it before she did.

The ice spoke. It was a long groan from the middle of the lake, low and rising, the sound of a great weight shifting on a bed that could no longer hold it. He turned his head at it. Then he turned the rest of himself and saw her.

His face did what it did. It opened, all at once, as it had on the quay and in the lane and over a pie in a dark cottage, and she watched it, and watched him remember a morning on a different quay with a charcoal jumper in his arms, and she watched it settle into a careful look that was not a laugh.

"You are out late," said Ansel.

"The ice."

"Yes. I could not sleep." He had not moved his arm. "I thought I might be the first to hear it go."

"You will not be. It will wait for light."

"It has been very reliable about that."

"The Heron will want trying again once it is out," he said. "I am told she takes the post across."

"And passengers."

"So I am told." He looked at the lake and not at her. "A great many people will be on this shore at first light, I imagine."

"The whole town. It always comes to see the ice go."

"And the knitter?"

"The knitter stands at the front."

"Of course she does," said Ansel, and she heard the whole of what he was not asking go by, as a stitch goes by that nobody has caught.

They stood. There were ten paces of shingle between them and then there were five, and she had not been aware of crossing the first five. Her own right arm had gone behind her, the parcel held flat against the small of her back under the open coat, and she held it there as she would have held a stitch she meant to keep on the needle. She saw that he saw it. His eyes went to her shoulder, and to the line of it, and came back to her face with nothing in them.

She came on. She did not know why. She had meant to stop where she was and say a thing about the weather, and she came on until there was an inch of cold air between his breath and hers, and there she stopped, because there was nothing left to cross.

The lantern was behind him. It put his face in shadow and her own in light, and she knew she could be read. His breath came out white and went over her mouth. Her own went over his. They stood in the one small breath together, neither of them warmer for it, and she looked at his collar, where the blue scarf made a thick lump under his jaw, and at a thread of color caught in the cuff of the Green.

It was rust. A short fiber of it, no longer than her fingernail, wound round the edge of the cuff where it had been snagged and left. She knew wool as she knew her own name, and this was hill wool, a hard two-ply, dyed in the brick red that nobody in Larkmere had asked her for in her whole life. It was the worst color in the county. She had it on a rack at the far end, behind the leaf green, and had never found a use for it.

He did not own anything that color. She looked at the thread, and she looked at the line of his shoulder, and she understood that his hand behind him was not empty.

She looked at his other hand, which she should not have been looking at. It hung at his side with the fingers curled, and in the lantern light she saw on the pad of the forefinger a small round dent, of the sort a needle leaves when a beginner holds it too hard. She had seen that dent on every child who had ever been put to a needle in Larkmere. A pen did not leave it. Chalk did not leave it. She told herself it was a thorn off the shingle. She was an exact woman and she told herself so exactly, and did not believe a word.

"You have wool on your cuff," said Hester.

He glanced down. "So I have." He did not brush it off. "I have been in a room full of it."

"Which room?"

"A warm one."

It was nothing. It was not an answer. She did not ask again, and her whole body wanted to, and the parcel was a hard flat edge against her spine. If she brought it round and held it out between them she would have given him a thing she had made in three nights and a lifetime, and he would take it with great care, the way he had taken the charcoal. He would say it was perfect. She could not tell, with the lake groaning and her own heart pulling at her wrists, whether he would say it of this one in the voice he had used on the other.

A gift was a risk. It was the plainest thing in the trade and she had never once let herself feel it. A garment was a long argument with a body, and the body might say no. She had once given him a swatch and watched him take it as a man takes a lit lamp, and that had been a scrap a hand wide. This was a jumper. She had made winters of jumpers that could not be refused, because no one had ever been in them, and she had called that skill. Now she stood in a thin white breath with a thing behind her that could be refused, and the refusal would not be in his words, which would be gracious, but in his shoulders, which would take it like armor, and she would see it, and she would know.

And he had a parcel behind his back. She had no way to know what was in it. She knew only that he held it as she did, tight against the spine, with his elbow bent and his whole arm gone still, and that his breath was shaking. He was cold. It was in the edge of his jaw. The blue scarf kept him warm at the throat and the toll sat in the rest of him, and he stood in the freezing dark with his hands hidden behind him and did not ask for anything.

"Hester," said Ansel.

She waited.

He drew breath. She saw it go in, and saw it stop at the top of his chest, and she saw him not say it. His mouth closed. His hand behind his back moved, once, and the paper of the thing in it rustled, and was still.

"Nothing," he said, very low. "Only that it is a fine night."

"It is."

"For a thaw."

"Yes."

Neither of them moved the inch. It was the width of a thumb, and it held between them as a frozen sheet holds a weight: not by strength, but because nobody has yet stepped on it. His hands stayed behind him. Hers stayed behind her. Her fingers on the parcel had gone numb, and she felt every thread of the wrapping paper, and the soft give of the wool beneath it, and the small open place at the left seam where the warmth got out.

Out on the lake the ice spoke again.

It was longer this time. It started far off in the dark beyond the last pole and ran toward them across the whole breadth of the water, a long cracking groan that she felt in the boards under her boots and then in her knees. The lanterns shivered on their poles. A sheet of grey ice as wide as the harbor slid a hand's breadth against its neighbor with a sound like a heavy cloth being torn, and was still.

Neither of them looked. They stood in the cold with the breath going between them and the thing in each of their hands, and the ice went on groaning in the dark, and neither moved.
