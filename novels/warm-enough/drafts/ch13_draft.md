The town went out onto the ice at dusk on the longest night, and it went out in silence, which Ansel found harder to bear than any noise.

There was no procession and no leader. People simply came down from the quay in ones and twos, and from the Net Sheds, and along the shore path, and stepped off the planks onto the frozen lake as they would have stepped over their own thresholds. Every one of them carried a lantern. Every one of them wore a Winter Knit. They were grey and brown and the dull red of old brick, jumpers and mufflers and long mittens, each with the plain close look of Hester's hand upon it, and in the failing light the whole shore seemed to be walking out of its own doorways in one soft color.

He walked with them in the Grey and every coat he owned.

The Ice Road ran away ahead of them into the dark, a line of lantern poles with the lit glass on each one, and where it crossed the middle of the lake the lanterns had been set down on the ice in a ring. The ring was wide. It was very nearly the width of the schoolroom's whole shore path, and the lanterns stood on the ice at the spacing of a long stride, so that the light fell from one to the next and made a hoop of gold on the black. The town filled it as water fills a bowl. By the time the last stragglers had come in from the shore, the ring was two deep in places, and then it was one deep, a single line of people standing shoulder to shoulder with a lantern at each pair of boots, facing in.

"I had expected a ceremony," said Ansel.

"It's standing," said Mina Cray, on his left.

"I am very good at standing."

"You are not."

She said it kindly, in her flat voice, with her arms crossed over a grey jumper that had flour in the weave. He did not argue. He found a place in the ring between her and a net-mender who nodded to him without turning his head, and set his lantern down on the ice by his boots, and stood.

Nobody spoke above a murmur. That was the rule of the night, and he saw at once that it was kept better than any rule he had ever laid down in a schoolroom. A child coughed and was hushed with a hand. The lanterns hissed. Far off down the length of the lake the ice gave a long low groan, as it did at night, and the whole ring of faces turned a little toward the sound and turned back. The stars were out, thick and hard, and there was no moon.

He stood.

He had known it would be bad, and he had not known how bad. On the schoolroom floor he could walk the benches and write on the board and the cold had to run to keep up. On the shore path he could carry baskets. Here there was nothing to carry and nowhere to walk, and the whole of the lake lay under his boots, a great black plate of it, with the winter's whole cold waiting in it like a held breath. It came up through the soles. It came up through the long bones of his legs and into the hips and spread there, slow and entire, and the Grey lay over his ribs like a sheet of frost, level and exact, and gave nothing back.

At the end of the first hour his hands went. He found it out by looking, as one finds out a thing about a pupil. They hung at the ends of his sleeves, bluish in the lantern light, the right one still bound at the wrist with its strip of grey linen, and he could not feel the fingers. He put them in his armpits. At the end of the second hour his lips had a taste to them, a thin metal taste, and his jaw would not stay quiet. He clenched it. Mina, beside him, did not look round, but she shifted her feet a little to put her shoulder against his arm, and kept it there.

"You have gone the color of a plum," she said, to the dark.

"It is the lantern."

"It is not the lantern."

"No," said Ansel, since there was nobody to be funny for, and since she would know. "I do not suppose it is."

Across the ring, in the full light of the lanterns, Hester stood in her grey scarf with her hands folded in front of her. She had been there since the first of them came on to the ice, and she had not moved, and she had not looked at him. Her face was closed. It was the face she had worn on the quay on the night of the Heron, a plain pale shut thing with the eyes lowered, a face that had no light of its own and took what the lanterns gave it and gave nothing back. Her grandmother sat on a stool at her elbow with the cane across her knees and her shawl drawn up to her ears, and her eyes were open, and they went from Hester to Ansel and back with the patient interest of a woman watching a pot she did not intend to stir.

He had not spoken to her since the lane.

He had not known what to say. They had stood in the drift until the squall passed, and it had passed, and the last of the white had gone down the lane like a curtain drawn, and she had taken her hands out of his coat and he had taken his from her waist, and neither of them had said a word. She had picked up her fleece out of the snow. He had said, at last, in a voice he did not know, that he believed the tiles would hold, and she had said that she believed so too, and they had gone each to their own door. He had lain awake all that night in the cold cottage with his hands open on the blanket, trying to remember what her fingers had felt like through four layers of wool. He had not been able to do it. He remembered only that they had not been cold.

He took his eyes from her. He looked at the lanterns. They had begun to swim.

It was somewhere in the third hour, when the ring had begun to loosen at its edges, that the first of the old people sat down. The stars had turned a little. The town, which had stood, was beginning to settle, a stool here, a folded blanket there, a child gone slack against its mother's side. A murmur of cloth ran round the circle. He did not sit. He did not trust himself to get up again, and he did not trust the ice, and he stood on, with Mina's shoulder at his arm and the lake coming up into him in slow tides, and the shaking began.

It was the roof again, and the drift again, and worse. It started in his shoulders and ran down. His knees went loose with it. He bent his head so that no one would see his face, and fixed his eyes on the ice by his boots, and made a remark to himself about the quality of the lantern oil, and it did not even come out as a joke. The cold had him by the whole of his body now, as a hand has a bird. He knew exactly how this ended. It ended with the master on his knees on the ice in front of the entire town, and a murmur, and a stool pushed under him, and the Winter Knitter's jumper on his back as cold as the day it was made.

Somebody was crossing the ring.

He saw the boots first. They were small boots, much patched, and they came across the ice from the north side of the circle with a quick uneven step, not a child's skip but a march, and the light from the lanterns lifted them one after the other out of the dark and let them fall. Heads turned. A murmur began and was hushed. The boots stopped in front of him.

He raised his head.

Tilda Brook stood before him in the lantern light with her chin up and her tongue caught between her teeth. She was eleven, or near it, with a scrubbed hard face gone red at the nose with cold, and she had one arm crooked behind her back as she had had it in the schoolroom, in the door of the Kettle Room, in the whole of the winter. She looked at him for as long as it took to be sure of him. Then she brought the arm round.

It was a scarf. It was blue.

It was the bluest thing he had ever seen. It was not the blue of good ink, as he had told her. It was the blue of a child's best mitten, thick and bright and uncompromising, and it was the most dreadful piece of knitting that had ever been laid on that lake. It was lumpy. It was wide at one end and narrow at the other, as though it had been cast on in one frame of mind and bound off in another. It had holes in it, and the holes were in places where holes should not be. A great fat knot sat halfway down it, where something had gone wrong and been ignored. She held it out in both hands as a clerk holds out a thing for inspection, and he saw her thumbs, which were red and chapped, and which were shaking.

"It's lumpy," said Tilda.

"I can see that."

"Hester says I knit like I'm angry at the wool." She said it quite loudly, so that the whole ring heard, and the whole ring went quiet in a way he could feel through his boots. "I wasn't angry at this one. You said blue. You said it was the best color for a scarf." She looked at him sternly. "I told you it wasn't for anyone."

"You did."

"I lied."

And before he could say any of the things that came up in him, before he could find the bow or the laugh or the remark about the finest piece of work in the county, she stood on her toes and put the scarf round his neck.

She did it quickly and badly, with her small cold hands, and the end went over his shoulder and she hauled it round again and knotted it under his chin in a knot like a fist. The wool lay against his throat. He braced.

He had braced against every warm thing since the Heron. The pies, the blanket, the Blue. He had stood in front of each one as a man stands in front of a door and put a laugh across it. He drew breath to do it now, and found that he did not know how. There was nothing to put across. It was Tilda. She was eleven years old and she had told him a lie in a schoolroom and had spent a winter of evenings ruining row after row in the corner of a loft, and every row of it had been meant. Every one. The whole of it was meant, the knot and the holes and the great uneven stretch where she had lost count and gone on, and the meaning was in the wool as plainly as a name in a book, and it came up out of the scarf into his throat like a hand laid flat there.

He let it in.

It was warm. It was warm as bread is warm, as a loaf taken out of an oven and set down, a deep slow plain warmth that came out of the lumpy blue wool and went into his neck and down into his chest and did not stop at the skin. It did not stop at the wrists, as the pies had. It did not stop in the middle of him, small and exact, as the soup had. It went on. It spread down through the ribs and out along the long bones of his arms, and the bluish hands came back to him finger by finger as the blood found them, and the lake, which had held him by the whole body since the Heron, loosened its grip. It was a very great deal smaller than the lake. It was bigger than he could stand.

Steam rose off him.

It came from the shoulders of the brown coat and the collar and the top of his head, a thin white veil lit gold by the lanterns, rising straight up into the still air where every breath in the ring stood. It rose where the warmth met the cold. A child said, very loudly, "He's smoking!" and a hand covered the child's mouth, and a sound went round the ring like one breath drawn through a hundred mouths. Somebody dropped a mitten. Mina Cray, at his shoulder, said nothing at all, but her arm came away from his as if he had grown too hot to touch.

Tilda took a step back and looked at him with her head on one side. She looked at the knot. She looked at his face. "It's warm," she said, in the voice of a clerk entering a figure.

"It is," said Ansel.

He could not say anything else. He stood in the ring in front of the whole town with a bad blue scarf at his throat and a column of steam going up from him into the stars, and he was warmer than he had been since the ferry. He was warmer than he had been in a great deal longer than that. And he was ashamed. It came up with the heat and sat beside it. He had done nothing. He had stood there and been cold. He had not earned a thread of this, not the knot nor the holes, and a child had taken a winter of her evenings and put it round his neck, and he had let it in, and under it the Grey lay against his ribs as it had lain since the first morning, cold as the day it was made.

They had all seen it. That was the whole of the horror of it. The town stood in a ring of lantern light and looked at the master with the steam coming off him, and they looked at the blue scarf, and then they looked, one face after another, at the plain grey jumper under his coats that the Winter Knitter had made him with her own hands, and at what it was not doing. He felt them work it out. He felt it go round the ring like a draught. A child's scarf, a lumpy thing, a bad thing, had done in the space of a breath what the best knitter in Larkmere had not done in a season.

He looked across the ring.

Hester had not moved. She stood where she had stood all night, in her grey scarf, with her hands folded before her and the lantern at her feet, and the light came up from below and lay along the line of her jaw. Her grandmother, on the stool at her elbow, had her eyes on Tilda with something like a smile at the corner of her mouth, and did not look at anyone else.

Hester was looking at him.

He could not read it. He had read a great many faces in his life. He had read a roomful of them at a time, for the first sign of a wrong answer or a joke or a lie, and he could not read this one. It was not closed. It was not open. It was a face lit from below by one lantern in the middle of the dark, still as a held breath, with the steam from his shoulders drifting between them, and nothing in it that he knew how to name.
