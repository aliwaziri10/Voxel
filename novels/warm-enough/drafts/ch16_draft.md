The Blue went onto the shelf at the back of the workroom on the first morning of midwinter, folded in its cloth, and Hester did not look at it again.

She had not decided to put it there. She had carried it in her arms from the long table to the shelf and laid it down between a crate of spare needles and a basket of undyed ends, and drawn the cloth over its shoulders and its unfinished neck, and by the time she turned round she had done it. It lay there with its cable and its warm shoulders and its armholes open like two small mouths. It would keep. Wool kept. It would be exactly as it was now in a hundred years, and so would the knitter who had put it down.

The light came in white off the snow and lay on the boards. She stood in the middle of it and was not at all certain what to do with her hands.

The racks hung full along the poles, hank on hank, in every color she had made since the autumn. She walked down them with her fingers trailing, not touching. Grey. Brown. The dull brick red the town wore. A cream she had never found a use for. At the end of the last rack, where the light was poorest, hung a single hank of moss green, a plain damp-stone green, the color of the north side of the Net Sheds in a wet spring. She had dyed it in the first week of the autumn because the pot had been left over from a larger lot, and had not thought about it since. She took it down.

She drew the end across the pad of her thumb. It ran dry and even and told her nothing.

She would do something plain. She would do it in seed stitch, which asked nothing of anyone, a knit and a purl in turn and the next row the other way about, a stitch with no opinion and no flourish and no road in it to be lost on. A jumper in seed stitch was a floor. It was a thing you stood on. She cast on at the long table with the tape round her neck under the scarf and did not take it off to measure, because she had the measure already, and began the hem.

She knit it correct. She made every stitch lie square, every purl sit back into its knit like a tile into a floor, and when she laid her palm across the first handful of rows she felt the cool of it come up, flat and dry and exact, the cool of a thing made by hands that were being careful. That was as it should be. That was the whole of her art, and she had had nine winters to learn it. The hem would be cool. She would knit it first and she would knit it politely, and it would lie at the bottom of the jumper like a stone at the bottom of a pocket, and whatever came after, it would not be able to reach down and be warm there.

The stove ticked. Snow slid from the roof in a soft thump and lay in the lane. The green climbed.

Somewhere past the hem, in the plain unthinking middle of the work where the needles go on without the knitter, she stopped being careful.

She did not know it at the time. She knew it afterward, as one knows the stair that was not there in the dark. There was a row coming off her hands, and she had a moment of looking at nothing, at the light on the wall and the long white lane in the window, and her mind went where it went. It went down the length of a shore path. It went to a man in a doorway with chalk on his cuffs and a laugh he put across everything like a hand across a flame. It went to a snowdrift against a wall and to the weight of a hand at her waist, and she let it go. She did not stop it. She meant it. For the length of one row she let her hands be what they wanted to be, and nothing else, and the needles went round.

When she looked down, the row was warm.

It lay across her fingers in the lamplight, moss green and plain, and the warmth in it was not the warmth of the stove. She knew that warmth. She had felt it twice, under her palms, at his shoulders, in a room with its stove going, and she had pulled the whole of it off him and put him out into the snow. It came up into the first joints of her fingers. She felt the white go from the ends of them. The stitches lay in their seed pattern as flat and honest as before, and every one of them held it.

She put the needles down.

She stood up and went to the stove and held her hands to it, and did not need to. She came back. She laid her palm flat across the new row. It was warm. She drew a length of the green from the ball across her thumb and found it plain and dry, and put her palm on the row again, and it was warm. She told it, sharply, in her own head, as she would tell a skein that would not lie, to be cool. It went on being warm.

A warm row does not change. That was the third thing, and the one she had been refusing to think of since the Longnight ice. Whatever was in a stitch when it left the hands was in it until the wool wore away. It did not cool when the hands left. It did not learn to be polite. It would lie there in the green, a single row of it above the cool hem, when she was an old woman and the jumper was a rag, and it would not forget what had been meant.

She could rip it. That was the thing. Her hand knew the motion, the needle drawn out and the yarn hauled loose and the whole row coming away in curled loops, and it would take less time than the kettle. She had done it before, in this room, with a cable, and had not regretted it. She laid her palm on the row and thought about the needle.

She did not rip it out.

She did not know why. She sat very still with her hand on a handful of moss green stitches and let it be a thing she had done. The warmth came up her wrist, steady, a little more than she could comfortably hold, and she held it.

The ceiling creaked. The loft stair spoke under a heavy, uneven, stopping tread, and there was the sound of the cane going down, one tread and then the next. Gran came into the workroom in the white light with the copper kettle in one hand and the cane in the other and her shawl up round her ears, and the hole in it showed dark at the edge. She set the kettle on the stove. She did not look at the long table. She lowered herself onto her stool with her hands and her knees and the rest of her, one stage at a time, and sat with the cane across her lap.

"Cold in here," she said.

"The stove is lit."

"I did not say the stove." Gran took up the cup at her elbow and found it empty and put it down. "You've started something."

"It is a plain jumper. It is seed stitch."

"Mm." Gran's eyes, which had been resting on the stove, came round to the table and went to the work as a bird's go to a seed. "That the green?"

"It was left over."

"It's always left over." Gran got to her feet. It took the three stages. She crossed the boards with her cane tapping and stood at Hester's shoulder, and she did not ask. She put out her bent hand, the knuckles knotted like old rope, and laid it flat across the row.

Hester did not breathe. She sat with the whole of her skin tight and watched her grandmother's face. It did not change. The eyes closed. The old mouth drew in at the corners, and stayed there, for as long as it takes a kettle to think about a boil. The hand lay on the green. She could see the thin skin of it over the bones, spotted and ridged, and it was entirely still.

"Well," said Gran.

"It is a row."

"It's a row." Gran lifted her hand. She did not say another word about it. She looked at the green and the hem below it and the warm band above, and her face did a thing Hester had not seen it do for years: it went quite gentle, and then, rapidly, it put the gentleness away. "I've something for you."

She had it under the shawl. She brought it out against her chest, with both hands, the way a person brings out a thing that does not like the light: a book. It was a thick old book bound in cloth gone soft as a glove, the corners worn round, the spine mended with a strip of linen so many times that there was more mending than book. A faded ribbon hung from the foot of it. It was red once. It lay along the page-edge now the color of a dried petal, marking a place somewhere in the middle, and the rest of the pages bulged round it, swollen with the hands of a great many knitters.

"It came with the shop," said Gran. "It's had four women's hands on it, and it'll have yours." She put it down on the long table beside the needles. It sat there and filled the table. "I never opened it past the first third. Never had call to."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know where it goes." Gran's hand rested on the cover for a moment, as if the book might stir. "The ribbon's for when you need it. Not before."

"I do not need it."

"No," said Gran, quite mildly, and took up the kettle, and went away to the loft stair with the cane going on before her. The treads took her weight one by one. At the top the door closed, gently, the way a house closes on a confidence.

Hester sat with the book.

It was not a large book. It lay between the green and the empty cup with its swollen leaves and its mended spine, and the ribbon lay out along the edge like a thing put out to be seen. She knew the page it marked as a tongue knows the gap where a tooth has been. She did not turn to it. She did not so much as lift the cover. She laid her hand flat on the cloth, over the old mending, and it was cold. Everything in the room was cold that she had not made that morning. She left the hand there a moment, and then took it away, and put the book on the far end of the table with the ribbon turned toward the wall.

She looked at the door.

It stood in its frame in the white light with the latch he had not mended, because she had never let him near it. The first hook held her coat. The second hung beside it, as it always did, and held nothing. A coat had hung there once, a man's coat, heavy and brown, and the man in it had gone out through that door at the thaw and had not come back, and the hook had stayed. She had not hung anything on it since. She did not know why she looked at it now. She looked at it as one looks at a dropped stitch several rows down, quite clear, quite small, and not to be reached without undoing everything above it.

Warm things go.

It was not a thought. It was lower than a thought, a plain flat fact, with no more feeling in it than gauge. She had never needed to put it in words. She had built the whole of her work on it for nine winters, as one builds a house on a rock, and it had held. The warm ones went. They went out of the door on a man's back, or across a lake in the cold, or on the first thaw, or into a ground that had been hard in the autumn. A person who made nothing warm kept everything she had. A person who made one row warm had made one row to lose.

She looked at the green on the table. It lay there, one handful of stitches above its cool hem, and she was afraid to touch it.

It was only one row. She told herself that. It was one row, and she had been careless, and it was the lightest thing she had ever done. She could stop. She could bind off and let the hem stand and give the thing to the first person who asked for a jumper. It would be warm for one row. Nobody would know. And she had meant it. That was the whole trouble, and she could not get round it or under it or through it. She had meant one row, and it had held, and now she knew what it was like for the next row to be unmeant, a plain cool row laid over a warm one like a lid. She was afraid to mean the next. She sat with her hands open on her knees and her cold fingers curled and could not make them take up the needle.

The stove ticked. The light moved a finger's breadth across the boards.

She did not know how long she sat. It was past the noon, when the white had turned yellow and the shadows of the racks had begun to lie along the floor, that she heard it. It came from a long way off, thin and clear and brittle, over the frozen lake, over the roofs of the Net Sheds and the quay and the whole white length of the town, and she lifted her head.

It was a bell.

It was a small flat bell, the kind that is tied to a sledge shaft, and it rang at the pace of a trotting horse, one stroke and one stroke and a stroke between. It was out on the Ice Road. She sat quite still with her hands on her knees and listened to it come, the sound growing a little with every ring, fading and strengthening on the wind, and far off down the lantern poles in the cold, coming closer, the winter post.
