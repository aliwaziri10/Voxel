The Grey was on his step again at first light, with frost across the top of it.

He knew it before he had the door fully open, the way a master knows who has been at the board before he has read what is written there. It lay folded square on the flagstone, one fold, sleeves tucked under, no string and no note. He stood over it in his shirt with the cold coming in round his ankles and said nothing to it for a good while. He had left it on a chair in the workroom the night before. He had walked home through the snow with every coat he owned over his arm, and he had not once looked round to see whether her window was lit. Now the jumper had come home ahead of him.

He put it on. It was the only thing to do with a returned thing. It lay against his ribs, level and exact and cold all the way through, and he put the other coats over it one by one and said "Wonderful" to the empty cottage, and the word came out in a voice he did not care to hear twice.

The day went as the days did. The children came up the shore path in ones and twos with their breath going ahead of them, and a boy tried the latch, and the stove sang its one high note. He taught the long sum about the ferry again because the benches begged for it, and got his laugh at the third landing, and spent the rest of the morning with his back against the warmest wall of the room and his hands pushed up his sleeves, moving. That was a thing he had learned since the Heron. If he stood still, the cold took him by the long bones. If he walked the benches and bent to a slate and wrote on the board, it had to run to keep up with him. He had not stood still in earnest since he stepped off the quay.

At the end of the afternoon the smallest girl, the one who had eaten his pies and named him, stopped at his desk with her red muffler up to her eyes. "Gran says come tonight," she said. "She says the wool wants winding and her hands won't hold a skein."

"Winding," said Ansel. "Is it a thing one can fail at?"

"She says bring your arms."

She went. He stood looking at the door she had left open, with the board behind him and the whole evening in front, and his first thought was of a blue jumper warm on his shoulders for the length of a breath, and his second was of the door he had shut on it. Neither was a thought a man could do anything with before supper. He cleaned the board. Then he went, because it had been asked of him by an old woman with a cane, and he did not know how to refuse a plain request. That was the reason he gave himself, and he gave it twice on the way up Spindle Lane.

The workroom at night was another room. The lamp on the long table had been turned low, and the stove threw a red light up the front of the racks and left the roof of the room to the dark, and the hanks hung in it pale and still, row on row, like a class sent home. Gran sat in her chair in the corner with the cane across her knees and the copper kettle ticking at her shoulder, the shawl with the hole in it drawn up round her ears. Hester stood at the far end of the table. She did not look up when he came in. She looked at the skein on the boards in front of her as though it had said a rude thing.

At the end of the table beyond her a cloth lay over a long shape, and one corner of it had slipped and showed blue. He looked at it once and looked away. He had stood in that blue in this room with her hands flat on his shoulders, and for one breath the whole of him had let go. Then he had done what he always did, which was to put a laugh across it like a desk across a doorway, and she had put him out into the snow. He did not blame her. He was not sure he had the right.

"You've brought your arms," said Gran.

"I understood they were required."

"Stand him, Hester. Don't sit him. A man who sits slumps."

"Gran."

"The winding wants two. My hands are old gloves, and the child has gone home to her supper. It's raw wool, off the rack, and it wants winding before the damp gets to it." Gran put her chin down into the shawl. "I'll supervise."

Hester looked at her grandmother for the length of a long breath. Then she looked at the stove, and the racks, and a point a little to the left of his ear. "Stand there," she said.

He stood where she pointed, at the near end of the table, with the lamp between them. The hank she lifted was a great loose ring of undyed wool, the grey of an unwashed fleece, and she held it open between her two hands like a hoop.

"Cuffs."

He pushed them back, all of them, one under another, the brown and the grey-green and the one with the missing button and the Grey's own sleeve and the shirt's last of all, until his wrists came out bare into the lamplight. It took a while. He did not fill it with remarks. She watched his hands the whole time, and when the last cuff was up she said, "Arms out. Level. Wider than that," and he put them out, and she slid the loop over his hands and settled it round both wrists.

He stood like a boy holding a rope for a game, with his arms wide and a ring of wool about them. Hester took up the loose end, found the beginning of it, and began to wind.

"Turn," she said, and he turned his wrists a quarter turn, and the loop let out a length of thread the way a lock lets down a measure of water. She drew it to her ball. "Back."

He turned back. The thread came off in a long smooth run, over the inside of his left wrist and then the right, and it was warm.

He took it for the stove at first. Then he took it for the lamp. But the stove was behind her and the lamp was a full arm's length off, and neither of them could lay a line of heat the width of a thread across the skin where his pulse was, and hold it there, and move it on. It crossed his left wrist and went up to her hand. It crossed his right and went up. The warmth was not in the room. It was in the wool. It ran from her fingers down the thread to him, and each time the loop turned it laid itself against the same two places, the way the sun lays itself on the one shallow of a cold river that the shade has missed.

He said nothing. That was strange in him, and he let it be strange.

Hester's face gave nothing. She drew the thread and turned the ball and kept her eyes on her hands. But he saw her jaw set at the third turn, when the thread left his wrist and went into her fingers, and he saw her thumb press down on it once, hard, the way you press a thumb on a blot to stop it spreading. The thread went on being warm. It crossed him and went to the ball, and the ball sat in her left hand and grew, and the warmth went into it with the rest, whether she liked it or not. She did not like it. He could tell. He had watched a great many faces try to keep a right answer from showing.

"Turn."

He turned. "Is there a way to do it wrongly?" he said. "I should like to know the rules of the school."

"Don't talk."

"I teach. It's a condition."

"Then talk to the stove."

He talked to the stove. He told it that the sums were going well, that the smallest girl had a future at the bar, that a boy in the back row had found out the hinges no longer squeaked and put his ear to the door every morning to be sure. It was easy at first. The jokes came out at the speed they always did. But the thread kept crossing his wrists, and the lamp kept burning, and the turns came slow and even as a ferry at the end of a long crossing, and his sentences grew shorter, and then rarer, and then he reached the end of one and found no other waiting behind it. He stood in the quiet. The stove ticked. The ball in her hand was the size of a small apple.

Gran's chin had gone further down into the shawl. A breath came from the corner, slow and a little whistling, and the cane slid a finger's width on her knees and was caught again.

"Slower," said Hester, low. "She'll wake."

"She is asleep."

"She sleeps like a cat. Turn."

He turned, slower. The warm line crossed his wrist. He watched it go, the pale thread lifting off the ring and crossing the bare skin and rising into her fingers in one steady fall, and he thought of the last time those fingers had been on him, two of them laid across this same wrist at this same table, cold as iron and entirely steady, reading him. She was reading him now, he thought. She was doing it with a thread.

"Are you cold?" she said.

She said it to the wool. She did not look up.

"A little," said Ansel.

She drew the next length to the ball and said nothing, and neither did he.

The ring on his wrists grew thin. The ball in her hands grew round and dense, until she held it in both palms like a loaf, and at last she drew the final length off him and turned the end in and set the ball down on the table. "Another," she said. "On the chair."

He lowered his arms. They ached, a clean ache in the shoulders. He had held them out the better part of an hour, and in all that time he had stood still. He had not once looked about for something to do with his hands. The thought passed him like a boat on the far side of a river, close enough to see and too far to hail, and went on.

The second skein hung over the back of the chair by the stove, where the red light lay on it. He took it down in both hands and carried it to her, a loose heavy ring, and held it out, and she took the loop from him across the table.

Her fingers crossed his palm.

It was no more than a hank changing hands: three fingertips and the ball of a thumb drawn once over the middle of his palm as the wool left it. He would have sworn it was an accident. He would have sworn it was the first thing she had done all evening without deciding. They were cool at the first touch. He had expected that, having once measured them for cold. And then they were not. A warmth came up through the skin of his palm as quietly as a stove catches in a room you have stopped watching, and was there, and was gone as she drew the loop away.

His hand went to his chest.

It went by itself, the right one, up under the open edge of the brown coat to the inside pocket and the flat shape that lay against his ribs there, dented at one corner, and pressed it once. It was there. It had been there all evening. He took the hand away and put it down at his side, and Hester, who was drawing the second loop open between her hands, did not look at it, and had, he was nearly sure, seen it all the same.

"Cuffs," she said.

"They are up."

"Then arms."

He put them out. She slid the second loop over his wrists. The thread began to come off in its long even run, crossing the bare skin, warm, and he let it. He stood with his arms out and the lamp burning down, and for the whole length of the second skein, while the ball grew in her hand, he did not once think of moving.

When the last of it came off his wrists the lamp had sunk to a bead and the stove had settled to a red eye, and the ball she held was the size of two fists. He was aware that he stood a long way from the man who had come in at the door. He did not know how long a way, and he did not look for a name for it. Hester held the ball in her cupped hands with her head bent over it. The scarf had slid a little from her jaw. He looked at the line of her cheek in the red light, and then, because he was a man who watched a room before he left it, at the corner.

Gran's eyes were open.

She had not stirred. The shawl was drawn up round her ears, the cane lay across her knees, the copper kettle ticked at her shoulder. But her eyes were open in the dark, bright as two wet stones, and fixed on the pair of them across the table, and she was smiling.
