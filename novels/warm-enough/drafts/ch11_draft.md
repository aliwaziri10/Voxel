The market came to the shore on the first clear morning after the snow, and by the time Ansel got there half the town was already carrying baskets.

The shingle had frozen so hard it rang underfoot like a schoolroom floor. Along the foot of the quay the quay folk had set out boards on trestles and thrown cloths over them, and the cloths were heaped with loaves in long brown rows, and apples in baskets, and cards of mending wool, and a damp cloth that steamed when it was lifted and showed meat pies packed close as a class at prayers. The sky was scoured and high. Every breath in the market stood up white and hung there, so that the whole shore seemed to be talking in small clouds, and the lantern poles of the Ice Road stood out from the white lake behind it in clear black strokes, each one with its cap of frost.

He had come down to buy bread, and had not got as far as buying any. A woman with a basket on each arm and a third on her hip had looked at him over the top of it, and said, "Mr. Brandt, would you?" and he had said he would, with a bow. After that the thing had run on of itself.

By the middle of the morning he had carried a basket of loaves from the quay to the third trestle, and a basket of apples from the third trestle to a cart, and a great flat basket of something wrapped in cloth that nobody would name for him from the cart to a doorway on Spindle Lane and back. The stout woman who had once tucked a brown shawl round his neck handed him a basket without a word and patted his arm as though he were a good horse. A boy ran ahead of him shouting "Make way for the Onion!" and the quay folk made way, and some of them laughed, and one old man took off his cap. He had a basket on each arm and a third in the crook of his elbow, and he was warm, or near enough to it, in the way that a man is who has not stopped moving since dawn.

It was the best arrangement he had found since the Heron. A man with a basket on each arm cannot be handed a pie. He cannot be given a blanket, or a seat, or a word. He can only be thanked, in passing, at a walk, and a thing said at a walk goes by him like a bank on a river. He had been thanked fifty times since sunrise, and not one of them had gone in.

The man with the covered basket found him at the fourth trestle.

It was the same man from the quay, the one who had put three pies into his gloves on the night of the Heron and watched them leave again one at a time. He lifted the cloth and the basket breathed out its steam, and he took a pie from it and held it out across the baskets on Ansel's arms with the look of a man who has been thinking about this for some time.

"Oh, I couldn't," said Ansel. "My hands are full. Look at them. I am a mule."

"Put it on top."

The man set the pie on top of the loaves in the nearest basket, square in the middle, where it sat steaming under Ansel's chin. "For after," he said. He did not say what after. He watched Ansel's face with great patience.

"Splendid," said Ansel, and heard himself, and could not stop. "I shall guard it with my life."

He guarded it as far as the next trestle, where a small boy with a red nose stood gazing at the baskets like a convict at a window. The pie went into the boy's hands without a word. The boy's whole face opened. Ansel was already walking, and he did not turn round, but he felt the pie man's look arrive between his shoulder blades like a thrown glove. It had the weight of a man who has found his plate empty twice.

He laughed at nothing and walked faster.

She came through the market the way a master comes through a noisy room, with nothing said and no hurry about it, and the noise dropped in front of her and picked up again behind. He did not see her until she was at his elbow. Hester wore the long coat and the grey scarf wound to her jaw, and her hands were bare, and the ends of her fingers were white. She did not say good morning. She took the basket off his left arm and held it out to the nearest quay woman, who took it as though it were a thing she had been waiting all day to be handed.

"Hold this," said Hester.

Then she took his sleeve.

She took it above the wrist, in finger and thumb, through the brown coat. It was a very light hold. A child could have shaken it off. But it was the first time she had put a hand on him in front of the town, and the quay saw it, every trestle of it, and went still the way a bench goes still when a name is read out.

"I have a great many baskets still to take," said Ansel.

"They will keep."

"They are loaves."

"Then they will keep better than you." She had not raised her voice, and she was already turning, and the sleeve went with her. "Come."

He went. He took his remaining basket with him because his hand did not seem to have been told, and at the door of the Kettle Room she took that from him too and gave it to a girl on the step, and the girl said, "Yes, Hester," in the voice of a person who has been handed a live thing, and held on to it. Then the door opened on warmth and steam, and he was inside.

The Kettle Room was full. It was always full on market morning, and every bench was crowded and every table steamed, and the window ran with damp from the sill up. The noise of it came at him like a wave. Then it stopped. It did not die away. It stopped, all at once, in the middle of a sentence at the third table, and fifty faces turned toward the door and the two of them standing in it with a woman's hand on a man's sleeve.

Behind the counter Mina Cray was drying a cup in a flour-white apron with her sleeves shoved above the elbow. She did not look round. She had never needed to.

"Sit him by the stove," she said.

Hester sat him by the stove. It was done with the same light hold, a turn of the sleeve and a nod toward a bench, and he sat, because to stand would have been to make a scene, and he was a man who lived in terror of making a scene. The bench was warm from the wall. The stove ticked at his shoulder. He put his hands on his knees and looked about for the joke, and it was a long way off.

"It is very kind," he said to the room at large. "But I have eaten. I have been eating all morning. Ask anyone."

"He has not," said Mina, to the cup.

A bowl came down in front of him. It was thick china with a blue line round the rim, and it was full to the line, and steam went up from it into his face. A spoon lay across it. Behind it, with a gentle chink, Mina set down the chipped blue teapot and one cup. The spout had a piece out of the lip, and the tea would come out of it in two threads. She went away again without a word, like a woman who has laid a charge and expects it to go off.

Ansel looked at the soup. The soup looked back.

"I could take it with me," he said. "In a basket. I have all those baskets."

"You will eat it here," said Hester.

She had not sat down. She stood at the end of the bench with her arms folded and her scarf at her jaw and her face as shut as a school on a holiday. He looked up at her, and tried the grin, and it would not come whole. "And if I do not?"

She did not argue. She did not threaten. She looked at him, and he understood from the look that if he said no she would say nothing more. She would unfold her arms and go back out into the white morning, and leave him to his baskets and his coats and his thanks at a walk, and she would not come after him again. She was very good at leaving. He had watched her do it on the quay on the first night with her whole back. She would not even be angry.

And he found, with a plain astonishment, that he did not want her to go.

It was not a large thought. It sat in his mind like a stone in a shoe. He did not want her to turn from the bench and walk out through that door. He did not want to be let go. He had spent the whole winter being a man whom no one needed to take care of, and it had gone so well that she was ready to believe it, and it was the one success of the winter that he would have given back.

On the roof, in the first grey light, she had told him from the foot of a ladder to stop doing a thing. He had not known what the thing was. He had gone about for days wondering how it was done, and what a man did with his hands once it was done. He looked at the spoon.

Perhaps it was this.

He took it up. It was heavier than he had expected, and his hand was not quite steady, and the spoon rang against the bowl. Nobody in the Kettle Room said a word. He lifted the first spoonful and blew on it, as a child does, and put it in his mouth.

It was hot. It was plain, and it was good, and it went down.

He had always held a warm thing at the edge of himself and let it go by. The pies on the quay, the blanket, the shawl, the hot cup at Gran's hand: he had taken each of them in his fingers and felt the heat come up to the wrist and stop, and he had passed it on before it could be a debt. This was a bowl of soup. Nobody had put a thing of themselves into it that he would owe for. It had been made for whoever sat on the bench, and he had sat. He did not hold it off. He did not know how to hold off a spoon.

The warmth went down through his chest and sat in the middle of him, small and exact, the way a lamp is lit in a schoolroom before the benches have filled, the corner first and then the desks. It did not reach his hands. It did not reach the bones of his legs, or the place behind his eyes. The cold of the lake sat round it unmoved, a great deal bigger. But there was a lamp in the room, and it was his, and it was lit.

He took a second spoonful. The room, which had been holding its breath, let it go in a long soft noise, and talk came back at the third table, and then the fourth, with a scrape of benches and a clatter of cups. A thin voice at the door said, "Is he eating?" He turned his head. Tilda Brook stood on the step with one arm crooked behind her back and her face pushed round the frame, and her eyes were round as plates.

"Out," said Mina, without looking, and the face was gone.

He ate. He did not talk while he did it, which was the second thing about that morning to astonish him, and he did not make a remark about the barley or the weather or the state of the china. He ate steadily, with his head down, and now and then the heat in him moved and settled like a cat on a stair. Across the table Hester had sat at last. She had a cup of tea between her bare hands, and the white had begun to go from the ends of her fingers. She was watching him. She did not look at the soup. She watched his throat, plainly, with her whole attention, as he swallowed, and when he looked up she did not look away at once. She held it for a breath, level, with the cup halfway to her mouth. Then she turned her face to the window and the running glass, as if there were a thing out there that wanted checking.

The spoon found the bottom of the bowl.

It made a small bare sound against the china. He looked down. The bowl was empty. Scraped, with the blue line showing clean round the rim and nothing in it but a smear of the last of the steam. He looked at it with real astonishment, as a boy looks at a sum that has come out. It struck him slowly, one fact after another, like the benches filling. He had not finished a bowl of anything since the ferry. He had been given a meal every day by a town that fed him whether he asked or not, and he had passed two parts of it down the quay to children and eaten the third on his feet, in a walk, and called it plenty. He had not sat. He had not finished. He had stood on a deck in every coat he owned with the whole lake in front of him and a letter against his ribs, and he had not once let a thing go all the way down.

The pies, in the end, had gone to the children in one breath. He had not tasted one.

He put the spoon in the bowl. It was done, and nothing had happened to him, and the room went on. He sat with his hands round the warm china and could not think of a single remark.

Mina came by with the blue teapot and filled his cup, and it came out in two threads. She set the pot down. She looked at the bowl, and then at him, and one of her eyebrows went up, slowly, a good way, and stayed there.

Hester did not look at him at all. She was looking at the window, with her cup in both hands, and the color had come up the side of her throat, above the scarf, as far as the ear.
