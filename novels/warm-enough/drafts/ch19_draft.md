She had said the window because the light was best. It was a fact about the window, and Ansel accepted it as a fact, and put the other fact, that the whole of Spindle Lane could look in, in the drawer where he kept the things he did not intend to think about.

The Spindle House kept its shop at the front and the shop kept its window, a broad old pane of greenish glass with the snow banked against the sill, and in the middle of it on that grey afternoon of midwinter stood a low crate with a scrubbed top. He stood on the crate in his shirt. His coats lay over the counter in a brown and grey-green heap, with the one with the missing button on top, and beside them, folded square, the Grey, cold as ever, not asked for. Tilda's scarf was still at his neck. She had looked at it when he came in and said nothing and walked round it.

"Arms out," said Hester.

He put them out. He felt, in front of the window, rather like a boy about to be examined in the catechism by a bishop who had not yet decided to ask. The room smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke and cold glass. At the back of the shop, through the half-open door, the stove ticked in the workroom, and overhead nothing stirred at all, and he understood that the loft above them had been very carefully emptied of its grandmother. He decided that this was a coincidence. He had decided a good many things that winter.

She came round the end of the counter with it over her arm.

It was a jumper, or the beginning of one. It was moss green, a deep plain wet-stone green, and it was knit in seed stitch, a pebbled fabric of tiny knots with no pattern in it and nothing to hide behind, and it was not finished. The body was whole as far as the shoulders. The right sleeve was no more than a few rows on a needle, hanging by its stitches from the armhole like a half-written line. But the left sleeve was done, to the wrist and the cuff, and she held the whole thing up between her hands in the light from the glass and looked at it, and then, for a moment, at him, and her face did what it did when she was working out a figure.

"Head," she said.

He bent. The wool came down over his hair, and dark, and close, and smelling faintly of the wet stone it was dyed to match, and his head came through into the light, and his hair stood on end, and the laugh was already waiting in his face. He stopped it. He did not know why. He stood on the crate with the green settling down over his shoulders and onto his ribs, and he braced.

It was a habit. It was a thing his shoulders did of themselves, since the Heron, since the quay, since a great deal before either. A warm thing came toward him and the whole of him went up like a hand, and he drew a breath to put the joke across it, and he thought, quite clearly: no.

He did not know where it came from. It came from the soup, and the lumpy blue scarf, and the Longnight ice with the steam going up off him like breath from a horse. It came from a morning at the foot of a ladder, and a voice that had told him to stop doing a thing he had not known the name of. He let the breath go. He let his shoulders down.

The warmth went in.

It came across the top of his shoulders first, a slow broad depth of it, as the sun comes across a shallow bank of a river where the shade has missed. It went down over the blades of his back and across the front of his chest, and it was not the small exact lamp of the soup or the plain bread-heat of the scarf. It was wide. It was a whole warm room. It went down his ribs to the waist and stopped there, at a clean line, and below the line, where the hem lay across his hips, the green was cool and dry and as correct as a ruled margin. He felt the difference through his shirt. It was like standing in a river at the place where a warm spring comes in and feeling the cold close round the legs again a foot farther down.

He heard himself breathe out. It was long and ragged and it shook at the end.

Hester did not look up. She had gone very still with the hem in her hands, and he saw her read it: the line of his shoulders, the drop of them, the breath. She read it as she had read his wrist, with her whole attention, and her mouth set, and she did not say a word. But the white had gone out of her fingers where they lay on the wool. He saw it go. He saw the first joint of each finger color up in the grey light of the window like the end of a candle, and she took the hem a little tighter in her fists, and he thought: she can feel it from her side.

"It is long in the body," she said, to the wool.

"It is perfect in the body."

"It is long. Hold still."

She went round him, once, with her eyes on the green and her hands moving over it, drawing a seam straight, turning a fold under the arm. He turned his head to follow her and she said "Don't," and he did not. He looked out through the greenish glass instead at the lane. It lay white and empty under a low sky, the snow trodden into brown ruts, and across it stood the Kettle Room with its small windows steamed to the sill. In the one nearest the door a clear patch had been rubbed in the white, an oval the size of a face, and there was a face in it.

He saw it at once and it did not matter. He saw that the man had a cup in one hand, and ink on the fingers that held it, and that he was watching the shop window with a pleasant patient look, the look of a man who has found something quiet to watch on a cold afternoon and has not decided what he feels about it. It was Piers Ellory. Ansel lifted his chin to him, helplessly, in the way that a man does who has never in his life passed another man at a window without a nod, and across the lane the face broke into its warm tired smile, and the hand with the cup came up.

He made himself keep still.

"Left arm," said Hester.

She had come round in front of him. She took his left wrist in her right hand, and turned it palm up, and the sleeve of the green came down it, in the seed stitch, to a cuff that sat a hand's breadth below where it should. He saw her frown at it. She did what she did to every yarn that came into the house. She laid her thumb on the inside of his forearm just below the crook of the elbow and drew it down, slowly, along the long soft channel of the arm to the wrist, pressing a little, with her eyes closed.

He did not breathe.

It was the length of his forearm. It was nothing. She was finding the place where the cuff must come, the bone at the heel of the hand, and her thumb was drawing the measure on his skin and not on the tape, as one draws a finger down the margin of a page. But it had been a long winter. He felt each inch of it separately. It went down through the inside of the arm like a lamp carried down a dark corridor, a dry cool thumb and then a warmer one, and it was the same thumb. It came to the wrist. It lay across the pulse on the inside of the bone, where she had laid two fingers in the first week, cold as iron and entirely steady, and read him, and this was not that.

The cuff was warm.

He had not been braced for the cuff. It lay round his wrist where the sleeve ended, a ring of the green not a finger wide, and it came up around the bone like a hand that closed. It was not the warmth of the shoulders. That had been broad. This was narrow and bright and exact, and it was set into the wool as a seal is set into wax, and it said, in a plain hand that any wrist could read: *here.* He felt it go in through the skin of the wrist and run up the inside of the arm to the elbow and across the shoulder and down into the middle of him, where the lamp of the soup was, and light it. And her thumb lay on the inside of his wrist between the cuff and the bone.

He gasped. It was a small clean sound, and he could not have stopped it for a school.

She opened her eyes.

He had not seen her face so near since the drift. It was a hand's breadth from his own. The lashes were pale and the eyes beneath were a grey he had never got the shade of, dark and level, and there was a white line of the scar of a pin on the lid of the left one. She looked at his mouth. She looked at the wrist. Her face was perfectly white. Her thumb had not moved, and her fingers had closed over the back of his hand without her leave, and he felt them tighten, once.

The pins fell.

She had held them in her left hand, a paper of pins, pressed against the green. He heard them go. They went from her fingers all together and struck the boards in a thin bright rain, a hundred tiny strokes, and ran in all directions across the floor. One rolled against the crate. One went under the counter. A great many of them lay about their feet and winked in the grey light, and the silence afterward was entire.

"That will do," said Hester.

She said it very fast. She took her hand from his wrist as if it had been burned, and her thumb came away, and he felt the cold of the shop come in where it had been. She took the green by the hem and hauled it up over his head in one sweep, with the wool snatching at his ears and his hair crackling, and when his face came out she was already on her knees on the floor with her back half to him, gathering pins with both hands.

"I'll help."

"No."

"I will. You cannot possibly find them all, they are everywhere." He went down off the crate and onto his knees on the boards, in his shirt, with the heat of the green draining out of him by the second and the cuff's ring fading from his wrist like the print of a hand in snow. He picked up a pin. He picked up another. His hand and hers went down together for the one at the edge of the counter, and met, and she snatched hers away so quickly that two more pins flew. "I am sorry. I am extremely sorry. Were you counting?"

"No."

"Then there is no harm done."

"No," said Hester, to the floor.

He knew what he had seen. That was the worst part. He had seen her white face and her open eyes and her thumb on his wrist, and he had seen what her face did, and he knelt on the shop floor picking up pins with a hand that would not stop shaking, in a warm room, and saw it all the way through to the end. She had come within a breath of something. She had stopped at the edge of it, as she had stopped in the lane. And across the way in a steamed window a man with ink on his fingers had watched the whole of it with a cup in his hand, and had smiled, and lifted the cup.

He did not look up at the window. He did not need to. He saw it in his mind: the clear oval in the steam, the quiet face, the old jumper worn to a shine at the elbows. A good man, kind and easy and honest to the last button. A man who had known her before. He had come in off the winter post with a roll of paper and a rueful mouth, and Ansel had liked him in the first breath, and liked him now, and could find no fault in him anywhere with both hands. He had come back to be loved by the woman Ansel was falling for. That was a plain fact, and the facts of it lay on the floor round him like pins, and every one of them was bright.

He had never in his life been jealous. It was not a thing he had been taught. He knelt among the pins and found the feeling quite new, a small cold stone in the middle of him where the warmth of the cuff had been, and he turned it over and could not make it cruel. It would not hold a grudge. It only lay there and ached.

When the pins were done, and she had put the paper away without a word, and he had put on every coat and the Grey on top of the shirt and the scarf, he stood at the door of the shop with the latch under his hand, and she did not look at him. She stood at the long counter with her back to him and the green over her arm, and said, "Thursday." She said it to the wall. "No. I mean soon."

"Soon," he agreed.

He went out into the cold.

It was dusk already, or nearly, a blue thick winter dusk with the first lamps coming on along the lane. He stood on the step a moment and breathed in the cold, and the lantern pole at the head of the lane threw its small yellow room onto the snow. Across the way the Kettle Room's door opened, and a man came out onto the step with his coat over his arm and ink to the second joint of both hands, and looked at the sky, and found him.

"Mr. Brandt," said Piers Ellory, and raised a hand.

"Ansel. Please."

He had crossed the lane before he thought. He did not know why. He had not decided it. He found himself in the middle of the trodden snow with his hand out, and the hand was taken, warmly, in a grip that was dry and quick and as plain as a rope, and the man smiled at him with a great and rueful kindness. Ansel felt the cold stone in his chest turn, and wince.

"You've been fitted," said Piers. "I saw. I'm afraid I rubbed a hole in Mina's window to do it, and she'll have my life. It's a handsome green."

"It is not mine yet. It is hers. I am only the frame."

"It's yours." He said it quite simply. "I know her work. It had your shoulders in it before it was ever on them."

That was too much. Ansel looked down at the snow. When he looked up the man was still smiling, wide and tired, a little greyer at the temple than he was in the first light of the sledge, and with the great stain of the ink on his hands and the old grey-blue jumper under the open coat. He was not cold. He stood in the lane at dusk in a jumper, with a coat on his arm that he had not troubled to put on, and the wind went round him as water goes round a stone. He looked like a man who had come home.

"I owe you an apology," said Ansel, "for the floor."

"The floor?"

"I told you I had a stone cottage with a cold floor. I did myself a great injustice. I have a cot. It is a perfectly good cot, with a blanket, and it has not been slept in since the Heron. It is in the room beside the stove, which is the only warm side of the house." He heard himself, quickly and warmly, and each word was true. "You would be most welcome. I should like it very much. It is a long cold winter for a man to come back to, and there is no reason in the world that you should lie in a room above a tea shop with a stranger's bed. I mean it. I mean every word."

And he did. That was the thing. That was what he could not get past, standing in the lane with his hand still in Piers Ellory's and the dusk coming down. He meant it with the whole of himself. There was not a thread of falseness in it. He would have given the man the cot and the blanket and the stove-side wall and his own place on the bench at the school, and he would have been glad to see him warm, and it would have cost him nothing at all to do it. He hated himself for it. He hated it clean through, with a plain fierce small hatred, because a man should at least be able to dislike his rival a little. A man should have one small mean door in him that stuck shut. He had gone through his whole house on the road across the lane and found every door standing open, and he could not so much as be petty. He could only be kind to the man who had come to take her.

Piers Ellory looked at him for a long moment.

He had seen it. Ansel saw that he had. He saw it go across the open face, quick and quiet and without any triumph in it, as a cloud goes over a field in March and leaves the field the same. He put his free hand on Ansel's arm, lightly, above the elbow, through three coats, and kept it there.

"That is more than kind," he said. "It's the kindest offer I've had in nine winters, and I'll remember it when I am an old man and there are no more cots. But Mina has a room above the Kettle Room, and she's put a hot brick in the bed already without asking me, which is the only argument I have never once won against her."

"Of course. Of course, I only thought—"

"I know what you thought." The hand tightened and let go. "I'm a very lucky man to have been thought it by you."

He smiled then, and it was a good smile, the best of the man's face, wide and tired and warm, and it was easy, and it was a little sad. It went all the way up into the lines about his eyes and stayed there as the light went out of the lane, and the dusk blued behind him, and the first lamp in the Kettle Room window came on at his shoulder, small and yellow, and he went on smiling at Ansel like a man who knows exactly what he has been given, and exactly what it cost.
