Ansel stood in the Spindle House, five coats heavy on his shoulders, a letter from a child burning against his ribs, and the strangest sensation in his glove, the ghost of warmth, the memory of a pulse that had traveled from her needles through grey yarn into leather and skin.

He had not been warm in weeks. The crossing had frozen him layer by layer. The quay folk's generosity had slid off him like water off oilcloth.

Now, in the workroom of the Winter Knitter, with Gran's cane tapping on the cobbles outside and the stove breathing fire into the room, Ansel Brandt felt the first crack in the ice.

He laughed. The sound was quieter than the ones on the quay. It did not echo.

"Well then," he said. "Teach me the pattern."

Hester turned. The firelight caught her face full on. Her mouth was no longer a thin line. It was not a smile. It was something between, something unnamed, the way a slate holds both the lesson and the erasure.

"First," she said. "You eat."

She gestured to a table by the window. A bowl steamed there. Bread sat on a wooden board. A pot of tea, the chipped blue teapot Ansel had glimpsed through the window from the quay, Mina's teapot, though he did not know that name, waited with two cups.

Gran's voice drifted through the door, muffled but clear. "Eat."

Ansel looked at the bowl. The bread. The tea. The woman standing by the stove in a grey wool scarf, her hands at her sides, her face open in a way that had nothing to do with welcome and everything to do with truth.

He unbuttoned the second coat. The third. The fourth. The canvas layer hit the floor. The wool layers followed. He stood in his shirt sleeves, chalk dust on his cuffs, the cold in his marrow exposed to the fire and the woman who had knit a strand of warmth into grey yarn and let it touch him.

He sat. He lifted the spoon. The broth was thick, salty, rich with root vegetables. It burned his tongue. He swallowed, and the heat traveled down his throat, spreading through his chest, reaching places no blanket had touched.

Across the table, Hester poured tea. Her hand did not shake. The chipped blue teapot tilted. Steam curled. She set a cup before him, then one for herself.

Gran entered behind her, cane tapping the flagstones. The old woman's shawl hole gaped. Her eyes moved from Ansel to Hester and back, sharp as a teacher scanning a room for the one child who hasn't understood.

"Good," Gran said. "Now the fitting."

Hester lifted her cup. The tea was dark as the harbor at midnight. She blew across the surface, a breath that stirred the steam.

Ansel wrapped his hands around his cup. The ceramic was hot. The heat seeped into his palms, his fingers, the gloves long forgotten on the floor beside the coats.

Outside, the harbor lanterns burned steady. The Heron slept in the Net Sheds. The ice thickened on Larkmere Water, whispering against the hull, a sound like fingernails on a slate.

Ansel drank. The tea was bitter, hot, perfect. He did not say thank you. He did not joke. He held the cup and let the warmth in, and for the first time since the ferry left the mainland, the cold in his marrow did not feel like the whole lake.

It felt like a thing with edges. A thing that could be measured. A thing that might, stitch by stitch, be met.
