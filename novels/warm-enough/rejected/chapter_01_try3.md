The Heron shuddered against the quay as the last of the light drained from the harbor. Thin ice whispered along her hull, a sound like chalk snapping under a careless hand. Ansel Brandt stood at the rail in every coat he owned, the collars stacked to his ears, and watched the town rise up from the water in a tier of slate roofs and smoke.

A boy on the dock shouted something about the freeze coming early. Ansel laughed, the sound pulling tight in his chest. "Early is relative," he called back. "Where I come from the river laughs at early."

The gangplank groaned under his boots. He stepped down onto the planks and the cold struck him at once, not the bite of wind off the water, but a deeper ache that settled in the long bones of his legs, the curve of his ribs, the knuckles of his fingers. It felt like the whole lake had been poured into his marrow.

He pressed a hand to his inner pocket. The folded letter sat there, its edges softened by a child's round hand. He touched it once, through wool and linen, and let his fingers fall away.

"Master Brandt!" A woman thrust a blanket into his arms, its weave rough and smelling of lanolin. "You'll catch your death in that wind."

"Thank you, truly." He turned and draped it over the shoulders of a porter hauling crates, the man's own coat worn through at the elbows. "You'll make better use of it than I will."

The porter blinked. "Aye. Right. Thank you, sir."

Ansel was already moving, his stride eating the distance between lantern poles. A shawl found his hands next, pushed by an old man with a beard white as frost. "For the schoolmaster. The cottage is drafty."

"Kind of you." Ansel folded it over the arm of a woman huddled near the Net Sheds, her hands tucked into her armpits. She pulled it close without a word, her eyes on the ice forming at the water's edge.

Two meat pies, steaming in a paper wrap, were pressed into his palms by a girl no older than his youngest pupils. Her fingers were red with chill. "Mam sent these. For the new master."

Ansel broke the first pie in half and handed both pieces to a pair of boys chasing each other along the rope line. The second pie went to a toddler clinging to her mother's skirts. The paper wrapper he tucked into his pocket, empty and warm.

"I'm fine," he told the quay at large, spreading his arms. "Fine as a slate on a sunny morning. Who needs coats when you have the welcome of Larkmere?"

Laughter answered him, scattered and low. The harbor lanterns were being lit one by one, their flames catching in the glass like chalk dust in sunlight. The Heron's ropes creaked as the tide shifted. Somewhere a child coughed, the sound sharp in the damp air.

Ansel walked on. The cold in his bones did not ease. It rode him like a passenger who had paid full fare and intended to stay the season. He made his jokes wider, his smile brighter, and felt the strain at the corners of his mouth.

The Net Sheds loomed ahead, their timbers black against the gathering dark. The quay narrowed here, the planks worn smooth by generations of boots and barrows. At the far end, where the lantern light thinned, a bench sat against the shed wall.

A woman occupied it. She wore a grey wool scarf wound twice around her throat, the ends tucked neat into her coat. Her hands moved in her lap, needles clicking, grey yarn flowing between fingers that did not hurry. A ball of yarn rested at her feet, caught in the groove between two planks.

She did not look up. Her face was closed, the mouth a flat line, the eyes shaded by the brim of her hat. She knitted as if the work were a sentence she had diagrammed and found wanting.

Ansel slowed. The cold in his ribs pulsed, a steady rhythm. He had felt this before, stepping off a ferry onto a new shore, the first week of term when the names on the register were only marks on paper. He knew how to wait. He knew how to be patient as a river finding its channel.

The ball of yarn shifted. A wake from the Heron's final settling rocked the planks, a gentle lift and fall. The ball broke free and rolled. It traveled the length of the bench, crossed the gap, and came to rest against the toe of Ansel's right boot.

He stared down at it. Grey wool, unremarkable, the strand catching the first lantern's glow. He crouched, his coats bunching at the shoulders, and picked it up. The yarn was faintly warm in his glove. Not the warmth of a pocket or a fire. Something else. Something like a coal wrapped in cloth, burning low and steady.

He stood, the ball cupped in his palm. The needles paused. The woman's head lifted. Her eyes were the color of the lake in winter, pale and deep. She said nothing. Her mouth did not move.

Ansel held the yarn out toward her, his arm extended across the space between them. "Dropped your stitch, miss. Or your ball, at least."

A shape detached itself from the shadow of the Net Sheds. An old woman, bent double over a cane of pale ash wood, advanced with the certainty of a tide. A shawl with one small hole at the shoulder hung over her shoulders, the edges frayed. Her hands, knotted as tree roots, gripped the cane handle.

She did not look at Ansel. She turned her face to the quay, to the lanterns being lit along the harbor, to the ice whispering in the harbor mouth.

"The Winter Knitter will see to him," she announced, her voice carrying over the water. "Hester will see to him. She knits the cold out. She always has."

The words rang against the planks. The woman on the bench, Hester, did not flinch. Her mouth thinned.

The words settled on the planks like chalk dust on a slate. Ansel felt them in his fingers, in the faint heat of the yarn cupped against his palm. The old woman, Gran, the quay had called her, stood planted between the lantern light and the bench, her cane planted, her shawl's hole catching the breeze.

He had been the new master before. He knew the shape of a welcome that carried weight. He knew the shape of a warning, too.

"She knits the cold out," the old woman had said. Not *for* him. *Out of* him. As if the cold were a splinter and the knitting a needle.

Ansel's throat moved. He wanted to laugh, to make a joke about splinters and needles and the perils of metaphor, to spread his arms again and tell the quay that he was fine, fine as a fresh slate, fine as a ferry on a calm crossing. The words crowded his teeth.

The yarn in his glove pulsed. Low. Steady. Like a coal wrapped in cloth.

He looked at the woman on the bench. Hester. The name fit her: two syllables, clean, no waste. Her needles had not resumed. Her hands rested in her lap, the grey scarf wound twice, the ends tucked. Her mouth remained a flat line, thinned by whatever passed behind her eyes.

A porter shouted an order down by the Heron. A lantern flared as a match caught. The flame steadied behind glass, throwing a pool of light across the planks. In that light, the yarn in Ansel's hand seemed to glow, the grey strands catching gold.

He did not know what to do with it. He did not know what to do with any of this: the cold in his bones that no coat touched, the welcome that pressed blankets and pies into his hands only to watch him give them away, the old woman's certainty, the younger woman's silence.

He had a letter in his pocket written in a child's round hand. He had a cottage on the shore with one stove and a window facing the Ice Road. He had a schoolroom waiting for him, thirty-odd names on a register, thirty-odd pairs of eyes that would watch him to see if he cracked.

He would not crack. He had resolved it on the ferry, watching the ice whisper along the hull. He would be no trouble. He would carry his own weight. He would stand in front of that class and make the lesson live, and if the cold in his marrow made his hands shake on the chalk, he would hide it behind a joke and a steady voice.

"Thank you," he said to the old woman. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "For the... the welcome. For the direction."

Gran's head turned. Her eyes, pale as the lake in winter, fixed on his face. She did not smile. She did not nod. She studied him the way a teacher studies a slate held up at arm's length, checking the sum.

"You're the schoolmaster," she said. Not a question.

"That's what the letter says." Ansel lifted his free hand, tapped his chest where the folded paper lay. "Ansel Brandt. Late of the river ferries. Newly of the shore."

"River ferries." She repeated it, tasting the words. "You know the water."

"I know the current. I know the channel. I know how to read the surface for what runs underneath." He heard his own voice go quiet, go serious. "Why do you ask?"

Gran's gaze shifted. It moved past him, to the woman on the bench. To Hester. Then back to him.

"The water remembers," she said. "The ice remembers. The wool remembers. You're new. You don't know what you're carrying. But they do."

The pronoun hung in the damp air. *They.* The water. The ice. The wool.

Ansel's fingers tightened on the ball of yarn. The warmth pressed against his palm, through the wool of his glove, into the skin. It was not unpleasant. It was not painful. It was simply *there*, a fact like the tide, like the freeze, like the names on a register.

"Carrying," he repeated. "Right. Well. I carry a lesson plan for Monday. I carry a chalk box that leaks dust onto my cuffs. I carry a tin that doesn't open." He smiled, the expression not reaching his eyes. "I carry a lot of coats, obviously. Beyond that, I travel light."

Gran made a sound. Not a laugh. Not a scoff. Something between, a grunt of acknowledgment.

"Light," she said. "You feel light to me. Hollow. The toll takes the weight first. The warmth. The sleep. It leaves you light as a reed stem."

"The toll."

"The newcomer's toll. The lake's due. You stepped off the Heron and the lake poured into your marrow. You felt it. You're feeling it now." Her cane tapped the planks, once, sharp. "Fire won't fix it. Coats won't fix it. Food won't fix it. Only one thing fixes it before the Breaking."

She looked at Hester. The lantern light caught the side of her face, the bent hands, the shawl's frayed hole.

"Her. What she knits. If you let it in."

Ansel's smile flickered. "Let it in. Right. I'll add that to the lesson plan. Between long division and the rivers of the northern district. 'Allowing warmth: a practical guide.'"

"Mock if you want." Gran's voice was flat. "Mocking doesn't warm bones. I've watched it for sixty winters. The ones who mock are the ones who shiver until the thaw. The ones who accept..." She left the sentence unfinished. The lantern behind her flared, the glass rattling in its bracket.

Hester had not moved. Her needles lay still. Her eyes, pale and deep, rested on Ansel's face. She had not spoken since he approached. She had not acknowledged the yarn he held out to her, the warmth he felt through his glove, the name Gran had spoken across the quay.

Ansel felt a strange pull in his chest. Not the cold. Something else. A tension, like a line drawn taut between two points. He stood in his stack of coats, his chalk-dusted cuffs, his dented tin pressing against his hip through the layers. He stood in the pool of light from the first lantern, the harbour opening behind him into darkness and ice.

The yarn warmed his glove. The cold ached in his ribs. The letter in his pocket weighed nothing and everything.

He held the ball out a little further. The gesture cost him something, though he could not have named the price.

"Miss, Hester." He tested the name. It sat oddly in his mouth, familiar and strange. "I believe this belongs to you. The ball. The yarn. It rolled to me. Seemed to know where it was going."

Her fingers moved. Slow. Deliberate. They rose from her lap, pale against the grey scarf, and took the ball from his palm. The contact lasted a heartbeat. Her fingertips brushed the wool of his glove. Cool skin. Cool as the lake. Cool as the ice forming at the harbour mouth.

But the yarn she took back was warm. The warmth stayed in his glove, a ghost heat sinking into his palm.

"Thank you," she said. Her voice was low, husky, unused to speaking across distance. "It rolls. Sometimes. When the tension's off."

"Tension." Ansel let his hand fall. "Right. Tension. I know tension. Thirty children, one room, one stove, and the wind coming off the ice. I know tension."

A corner of her mouth moved. Not a smile. Something closer to a crack in ice, a hairline fracture that might spread or might hold.

"Children," she said. "You have children."

"Thirty-odd. Give or take a few who vanish when the nets need mending." He shrugged, the coats rustling. "They're good material. Mostly. They test you. They test the chalk. They test the patience. But they're honest about it. They don't pretend to be fine when they're not."

The words hung. The lantern light caught her face: the closed eyes, the thinned mouth, the scarf wound twice. She looked away, toward the harbour, toward the Heron lying dark against the ice.

"Honest," she repeated. "Yes. Honest."

Gran shifted. Her cane scraped. "Walk with me, schoolmaster. The cottage is on the shore. The path is known. I'll show you the way the lanterns go."

Ansel glanced at Hester. She did not look back. Her needles clicked once, twice, picking up the rhythm. The grey yarn flowed. The ball sat in her lap, warm against the wool of her coat.

"Lead on," he said to Gran. "I follow directions well. It's in the job description."

He turned. The cold in his bones walked with him, a passenger who had paid full fare. The warmth in his glove lingered, a coal wrapped in cloth, burning low and steady. Behind him, the first lantern threw his shadow long across the planks, stretching toward the bench where a woman in a grey scarf knitted, her mouth a flat line, her eyes on the harbour where the ice whispered and the Heron slept.

The light caught the yarn between her fingers. Grey. Unremarkable. Warm.

Ansel walked on, his stride eating the distance, his jokes waiting in his throat like stones.
