The workroom held the stove's heat in the way a good block holds steam, even and patient. The long table had been cleared to a single length of cable running down the center, the Blue spread flat upon it with its sleeves folded back and its body ending clean at the underarm where the yarn waited, wound and ready. The drying racks stood empty. The loft above was quiet, Gran's cane leaning by the hatch, the pale ash wood catching the firelight.

Hester stood at the table with her tape measure hanging round her neck under the scarf, the linen warm against her throat. Her fingers were cold. They were cold the way the tips of needles are cold before the yarn takes them up. She drew a length of the Blue's own grey-blue across her thumb, testing the ply, the tension, the memory of her own hands in every stitch. The cable had been knit with intention. The rib at the hem had been knit with courtesy. She knew the difference the way she knew a dropped stitch by the sound it made in the silence.

The door from the shop opened. She did not turn. The tread on the boards was light, quicker than Gran's, heavier than Tilda's.

"Workroom," she said. "Gran is sleeping."

Ansel's laugh came before the answer. "Good. I would hate to wake her with the state of my shirtsleeves."

He stood in the doorway with his coat folded over his arm and the Grey left on the chair in the shop, folded neat as a pressed slate. His shirt was pale linen, rolled at the cuffs to the elbow. The collar stood away from his neck, stiff with starch or simply with the habit of a man who dressed for a room he expected to be colder than this one.

Hester turned. The Blue lay waiting. She picked it up by the shoulders, the cables heavy in her hands, and held it out.

"Arms up."

He lifted them. The movement pulled the shirt tight across his shoulders, across the breadth of his back. She slipped the Blue over his head. The wool caught on his chin, on the bridge of his nose, and settled. The cables fell into place around his ribs. The half-finished body hung to his waist, the raw edge of the live stitches brushing the tops of his trousers.

He stood still. His breath came slow. The firelight caught the line of his throat, the pulse there moving under the skin.

Hester moved behind him. The collar of the Blue had folded wrong at the back, a small thing, a twist of fabric that would sit crooked and chafe. She reached for it. Her fingers found the nape of his neck, the knuckle of her forefinger pressing into the hollow where the hair ended and the skin began.

His breath changed. It stopped, or it deepened, or it became a different shape entirely. She could not have named it. She only knew that the air between them had thickened, warmed, become something she could not measure with tape or thumb.

The wool at her knuckle was warm. Not the polite warmth of a garment left by a stove. Warm the way a stone is warm that has held the morning sun, heat sunk deep and steady, radiating from the center out. The Blue had been knit warm here. She had knit it warm here. Her hands had known what they were doing even when her mind had called it courtesy.

Her knuckle stayed at his nape. The cables at his back shifted with the rise of his chest. The live stitches at the hem brushed his shirt. He did not move away. He did not lean in. He stood in the half-knit jumper and let the warmth find him, and for one breath he did not brace against it.

She felt her own pulse in her fingertip. She felt the tension in her own shoulders, the held breath, the control that had carried her through nine winters of polite knitting and cold scarves and the Grey that sat in the shop like a shell. Her hands had stopped being polite without her permission. They had knit the cables with care. They had knit the shoulders with care. They had knit the neckband with care, every stitch a small surrender she had not agreed to.

The warm row she had ripped out in the schoolroom. The river-stone wrist under her fingers. The memory rose stitch by stitch, unasked.

Ansel's hand came up. His fingers closed over hers, light, callused, warm. He did not pull her hand away. He did not press it closer. He held it there, his palm against the back of her hand, his thumb brushing the tendon that ran to her forefinger.

"Hester," he said. His voice was low. It had no laugh in it.

She pulled her hand free. The warmth broke. The Blue went cold at her knuckle, or she went cold, or the air between them snapped back to its proper shape. She stepped away, her fingers finding the tape measure at her neck, drawing it out, measuring nothing.

"The fit is acceptable," she said. "The cables sit true. The tension holds."

She turned to the table. The Blue's yarn waited. The cable needle lay beside it. She did not look at him.

"Go," she said. "The shop is cold. Gran will wake."

He did not move at once. She heard the fabric of his shirt shift. Heard the long breath he took. Heard the laugh that came after it, thin and wrong.

"Right," he said. "The shop. Cold. Gran."

He walked to the door. His boots were quiet on the boards. The door clicked shut behind him.

Hester stood at the long table with the tape measure slack in her hands. The stove ticked. The loft stayed quiet. The Blue lay across the table, half-finished, never completed, warm at the shoulders and the left cuff and the neckband where her knuckle had rested, cold at the hem where the hem had been knit first, politely.

She picked up the cable needle. The yarn waited. Her fingers were cold.

She set the cable needle down. The metal was cool against the table, a small click in the quiet. Her fingers would not move to the yarn. They hovered above the live stitches, the half-finished body, the cables that had taken shape under her hands without her mind's leave.

The warmth at the neckband was not imagined. It was not the stove. It was not the firelight pooling on the wool. It was the row she had knit on the night the Heron came in, the row she had ripped out in the schoolroom and knit again in the dark, the row where her thumbs had pressed into the fabric as though they could push the cold out of him. That row was still there. It had not gone. It would not go. Warm knits last.

She had told herself the Blue was a courtesy. A Winter Knit for the schoolmaster who had arrived in the last of autumn with the lake cold in his bones. She had told herself the cables were structure, the rib was order, the seed stitch at the cuff was gauge. She had measured him at the door with her tape and her eye and her thumb on his wrist and called it work.

Her hand had known better. Her hand had knit the shoulder seam with a tension that was not polite. Her hand had turned the cable at the back neck with a twist that held. Her hand had worked the neckband stitch by stitch and left a warmth in it that her mind had not authorized.

The Grey on the chair in the shop was cold. It would always be cold. She had knit it flawless and undyed and perfect, and it held only tidiness. A knit with no flaw sits like a shell. She had known the rule before she cast on. She had knit it anyway.

The stove ticked. A log shifted. The loft stayed quiet, Gran's cane leaning by the hatch, the pale ash wood catching the light.

Hester's fingers settled on the Blue's shoulder. The cable there was warm. Not hot. Not burning. Warm the way a held cup is warm, the heat sinking into the palms and stopping there, the way heat always stopped for her. But this warmth did not stop. It ran up her fingers, into the knuckle that had rested at his nape, into the wrist she had pressed to his pulse in the schoolroom, into the arm that had reached for him without permission.

His hand had come up. His fingers had closed over hers. Callused, warm, light. He had not pulled. He had not pressed. He had held.

The memory of it sat in her palm like a live stitch waiting to be worked.

She had pulled away. She had said go. She had measured the fit and called it acceptable and sent him out into the cold shop with the Grey folded on the chair and the door clicking shut behind him.

The door. The click. The silence after.

She picked up the Blue. Not by the shoulders this time. She gathered it into her arms, the cables heavy, the live stitches at the hem brushing her wrists, the neckband folding against her throat where the scarf lay. The wool was warm at the shoulders. Warm at the left cuff. Warm at the neckband where her knuckle had rested. Cold at the hem where the hem had been knit first, politely.

She stood at the long table with the half-finished jumper in her arms. The drying racks empty. The stove ticking. The loft quiet.

The winter was not stopping. The lake was frozen from the quay to the far shore. The Ice Road lanterns would be lit at dusk. The Heron lay in the Net Sheds until the thaw. The schoolchildren would skate until the light failed. The schoolmaster would stand at the water's edge in the Grey that kept nothing out, counting heads, laughing before he answered, wearing every coat he owned.

She would knit the Blue. She would finish the body. She would shape the sleeves. She would work the second cuff in seed stitch to match the first. She would cast off the neckband she had already knit warm. She would give him a garment that held care in every cable, every rib, every stitch she had not meant to surrender.

And the Grey would stay on the chair, cold as slate, flawless, a shell.

Hester held the Blue against her chest. The warmth at the neckband seeped through the scarf, through the linen of her coat, into the skin at her throat. Her fingers were cold. The wool was warm.

The door stayed shut.
