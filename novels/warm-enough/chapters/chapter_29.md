The Green was the best thing he had ever worn, and it lasted until the thaw began to drip.

He noticed every hour of it, because he was a schoolmaster and could not help it. Whole days of walking about the schoolroom with his hands loose at his sides and his shoulders down from round his ears. Whole days of the stove being a stove instead of a thing he had to lean into like a man leaning on a gate in a gale. The children noticed. The children noticed everything. A girl in the front row had asked him over the slates whether he was ill, since he had stopped holding himself like a man waiting to be struck, and he had said that he was merely rested, and the whole class had nodded with great solemnity and agreed that this was suspicious.

He had not taken it off to sleep. He would have been ashamed to admit that, if there had been anyone to admit it to.

On the afternoon the first drip fell from the eaves, when the last of the children had gone clattering off down the shore road and he stood at the slate with the wet cloth in his fist, Mr. Ostler came up the school steps.

He came one step at a time with a hand on the rail, an old man in a hat. He wore the hat in all weathers and in all rooms, a flat brown felt that had taken the shape of his skull so long ago that the two had come to an understanding, and under it his face was the color of the underside of a fish. His nostrils were blue. His lips were not far behind. He stood in the door and said, in the courteous cracked voice of a man who has been asking for little all his life, that he was sorry to trouble the master, that his own fire had gone out and the wood by his step was wet, and that he had wondered whether he might warm his knees at the school stove until the light went.

"Sit," said Ansel. "Sit, sit, take the good chair, it's the only one with a back." He had the old man by the elbow and into it before the sentence ended. The stove was low. He fed it. When he turned round Mr. Ostler had his hands out to the iron and they were shaking, a fine fast shake that went up the wrists and into the sleeves, and the hat had begun to rattle very slightly against the chair back.

"It's the thaw," said Mr. Ostler, to the stove. "It's worse at the turn than in the dead of it. The cold lets go of the ground and gets into a man's knees. I've known it all my life."

Ansel was warm. That was the whole of the difficulty. He stood in the middle of his own schoolroom in a moss green jumper that held the heat the way a good slate holds the sun, and across from him an old man was shaking in a hat, and the one fact stood next to the other with nothing whatever between them. It did not feel like a decision. He did not think about the Green, or about whose hands had made it, or what it had cost. He was already pulling it up over his head. It came off with a small sigh of static, and the schoolroom air hit him through the Grey like a bucket of the lake, and he held the jumper out in both hands and said, "Here."

"Master. I couldn't."

"It's a loan. I am a furnace. The children say I steam." He laughed, and the laugh was ready, as it always was. "Put it on, Mr. Ostler, I beg you, I shall be quite insulted if you don't."

The old man did not say another word. He put it on over his coat and under his hat, rather crookedly, and it took him a moment to find the neck. And then he went still. He sat in the good chair with his arms inside the sleeves and his mouth a little open, and Ansel watched the thing happen to him that had happened to himself on the school step. The color came up from the collar. It came up the jaw like a tide up a slip, and the blue went out of the lips and the grey out of the skin, and the shaking stopped, all at once, the way a ferry stops shaking when it comes in under the lee of the quay.

"Well, I'll be," said Mr. Ostler.

He took it as he took the weather: gladly, and without argument. That was the trouble, and also the whole of the answer. A man who lets the warm in is warmed. Mr. Ostler had never in his life turned down a hot cup or a fire or an offered chair, and he opened himself to the jumper as a door opens to a wind, and it went through him in a rush, shoulders to waist, as it had been knit to go.

Ansel walked him home. It was not far, to the next doorstep along the shore road, the low stone house with the dead fire. He stood on the step and saw the old man in, and the old man turned in the doorway with the Green over his coat and his hat pushed back and said, "Warmest I've been since the autumn, Master," and shut the door on a good smell of newly lit kindling. Ansel stood a moment longer in the lane.

The cold had come up through the Grey and was sitting in him again.

It had come back quite quietly, like a lodger returning to his old room. He gave it no weight. He had had a long spell of grace, and a man could not expect grace to be renewable, and the thing had gone to a better home than his. He turned the collar of his coat up and put his hands in his armpits and went back along the road, whistling, and the whistle came out thin and flat.

Tilda was in the schoolroom.

She sat at her desk in the back row, where she was supposed to be doing her sums as a punishment for a matter of ink, and she had not done them. She had her slate on her knee and her tongue between her teeth and her eyes on the door, and when he came in she said, without a hello, "You gave it away."

"Lent."

"It's the Green."

"It's a jumper. A very good one. It happens to be on a gentleman who needed it."

"Hester made it. For you." She was not accusing him. She had the flat reasonable tone of a child setting out the terms of a problem on a slate. "She was up late at the end. I know because I fetched the oil. And then you went and gave it to Mr. Ostler, and she's coming up the lane right now and she went past his window and she looked in and she went white."

He turned round.

The light was low and gold in the window, the long light of a late winter afternoon with the first drip beginning from the eaves, and in it, coming along the road from the Spindle end with her scarf pulled up and her hands in her sleeves, was Hester. She was walking quickly. She was not looking at the school. She had her eyes on the ground a pace ahead of her boots, the way a person walks who has been stopped in the street by a sight and is trying to get away from it before it gets into her.

"Tilda," said Ansel.

"I'm not going."

"There are blankets in the chest at the foot of my bed. Two of them, the grey ones. The stove's low and the night's coming and I should like you to bring them. Please."

She looked at him with great suspicion and total delight. She had understood the whole thing, he saw, and she meant to go in the end because the going was an adventure, and in the meantime she meant him to know that she had seen the trick. "Fine," she said. "But I know what you're doing."

"Then you are a person of observation. Go."

She went. She went out of the side door in a flurry of boot and scarf, and across the yard to the cottage, and he heard the latch go. A thought went under him as he stood there, small and quick, like a fish passing beneath a ferry, a thought about the bed and the pillow and what lay under it. He let it go by.

Hester came in.

She shut the door behind her. She did not say a word. She came up the room between the desks with her scarf still at her chin, and stopped, and looked at the stove, and the empty hook, and then at him, at the Grey, the plain flawless Grey with the lake in it, standing there where the Green had been. He saw her take the measure of it. She did it quickly and without mercy, from the collar to the cuffs, the way she looked at every man's shoulders before she reached for the tape. And her face did not set. That was what he had braced for, the dry shut look, the order being put back into a room. It did not come. It stayed open.

"Mr. Ostler is wearing it," said Hester.

"He was cold."

"Yes."

"I could see him shaking, and I was warm, and it was the work of a moment. I should have asked you. It was a gift, and one doesn't"

"I am not angry."

"You have a look."

"I am afraid," said Hester.

She said it flat, as she said everything. It fell between them on the boards like a dropped slate and lay there, and neither of them moved to pick it up. He had his back to the master's slate now, the great black board behind him, and he found that at one point he had stepped back until the wooden ledge struck his shoulder blades, and that his hands had gone behind him and taken hold of the chalk tray on either side, and were gripping it.

She came closer.

She did not seem to know she was doing it. It was only a step, and another. The stove ticked. She stopped when her shoulder was an inch from his chest, so near that the grey wool of her scarf stood against his waistcoat and he could smell it, lanolin and cold air and a trace of the wet iron of the workroom racks. She was not tall. She had to look up. Her breath came out and went on his collar, quick and light, and went cold on the skin below his jaw, and was followed by another. His hands were on the chalk tray behind him and the ledge cut into the heels of them. He held on.

"It is not that he has it," she said. Very quietly. To the buttons of his coat. "He is an old man, and it is warm on him, and I am glad. I am not a person who grudges a coat to a person who is cold. It was how easily it went. It was in your hand and then it was in his, and you did not so much as turn your head. You gave away the best thing I have ever made as one gives a man the time of day."

"It's only wool, Hester."

"It is not only wool, and you know it, and so does the town." She looked up. Her eyes were very close. "You have given away your coats, I am told, the whole of the autumn. You gave your last pie to the first child who looked at it. You give and you give, and I think that if there were a way to hand a man the cold that lives in you, you would have posted it to him by now. And I am afraid, because I have just seen how it is done. I have seen you do it to a thing that I made. And I cannot tell, standing here, what stops it from being done to me."

He opened his mouth. The joke was ready. It was a very good one, about the post.

He did not say it.

It was true. That was the plain awful thing about it, standing in the schoolroom with her breath on his throat. It was true all through, and he had never put it into words, and she had put it into words for him in the time it took the stove to tick twice. He gave things away. He gave them quickly, before they could be taken, because a thing given was a thing chosen, and a thing taken was only a loss. He had thought of it as generosity all his life. He had been rather proud of it. It was a very neat piece of arithmetic, and it came out the same every time, and the sum was an empty pair of hands.

"Keep one thing," said Hester.

"What?"

"One. I do not mind what it is. It need not be from me. Keep one thing, Ansel, that you will not hand to the first cold man who comes to your door." Her voice had begun to shake, a fine thread of it, and she stopped and put it right. "I would like to know that there is a thing you will hold on to. That is all. I would like to see it."

He thought. He did honestly think. He went through his pockets in his mind, the satchel, the cottage, the chest at the foot of the bed with the two grey blankets, and he took the inventory of himself as he would have taken the register at the end of a long term, name by name. Chalk. A coat that did not fit. A scarf that was not his. A letter in his waistcoat that he had not opened. He held each one up and looked at it and saw that it was already half out of his hand.

The stove ticked. The light moved a little on the floor.

"There is nothing I can keep," said Ansel.

He said it lightly. He had meant to say it lightly, with the smile in front of it, and it came out of him in a flat small voice that was not his, and the smile did not come at all. The words stood in the room. And he watched her face, turned up to his own, and saw them go into it, one after another, and saw what it did.
