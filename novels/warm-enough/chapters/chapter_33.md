The children went home at the usual hour and Ansel did not. He sat at the master's desk with the register open and a page of sums he had marked twice and could not have answered for, and the stove ticked, and the window that faced the Ice Road went from grey to the blue of old ink. His hands lay on the blotter. They had been warm that morning for the length of a swatch. By noon the warmth had gone out of them, as it always went, and he had looked at them over the heads of the third bench with the plain surprise of a man who has been lent a coin and finds his pocket empty again.

He did not mind. He told the register he did not mind. He minded it exactly the width of one cream square.

The day had been a long one to get through. He had taught the whole of it with his hands going to the place on his ribs where the Green's warmth gave out, and twice he had lost the thread of a sum and found it again in the middle of the room, laughing at nothing. Tilda Brook had watched him through the whole of the afternoon's arithmetic with her tongue caught between her teeth. At the end, when the benches were emptying, she stopped at his desk with her slate under her arm.

"Mina says you'll have the coats off by the Breaking," said Tilda. "I said sooner."

"You have wagered on my coats."

"A cake." She considered him with the evenness of the very honest. "You look like a man who's had good news. You've marked the register upside down."

He looked. He had. "It is an experimental method," said Ansel, and laughed before he said it, and Tilda gave him the look of a girl who has seen the answer on the back of the page and means to say nothing until it is asked for.

At dusk he put on the brown coat and the grey-green one, and the Green under both, and Tilda's lumpy scarf over the lot, and went out and locked the door that no longer stuck. The shingle was wet. The thaw had got into the shore path in the night, and water ran in a dozen thin streams under the old snow, talking to itself the way a back row talks when it thinks the master is writing on the board. Behind him the quay lamps began to come up, one and then the next, in their old order from the Net Sheds toward the town, and he knew who was walking the quay by the order they came. He did not turn round. He went down onto the ice.

It rang under his boots, as it had the night before, but softer. He noticed that and put it away. The Ice Road lay ahead of him in its line of poles, and every lantern was lit, small and gold, with the lake gone the dark of a wiped slate between them and the stars coming out thick over the far shore. He walked past the fifth pole and the sixth. At the ninth he stopped, because it was where they had stood, and there was nothing to mark it except that he knew.

He had four things to say and he took them out and looked at them. One was about the lamps. One was a very good one about the ferry. The third was a small decent remark about the weather, which he could have delivered in his sleep, and the fourth was the truth, and he had never in his life gone first with it. He set the others down one at a time, as a man sets down a heavy bag on a quay. The ice boomed far off. He stood in the middle of the road in his three layers with his hands in his sleeves and his breath going out white, and he was cold, as he had been cold since the Heron, to the roots of every long bone, and he let himself know it. It was a very large cold. It was much larger than a man who laughed at it.

She came along the poles from the town side.

He saw the small gold of her taper first, a stub of it, still lit, held low in a hand she did not feel it in. Then the scarf, wound to the jaw. She walked without hurry. She had lit the harbor lamps, every one, and then she had walked out onto the ice to where he was, and she did not pretend that was a coincidence, which he loved her for, plainly and at once, standing there. She stopped an arm's length off. She blew out the taper, and the little smoke went up between them, and the dark came in.

"You are on the road," said Hester.

"I am."

"It is thin at the edges now. The poles will come in before the Breaking."

"I know. I've been told." He tried to laugh and it came out the wrong size. "I was waiting. I thought it was the most dignified thing to do."

She said nothing. She stood with the dead taper in her fist and her eyes on his face, the lantern on her left laying its line along her cheek, and he understood that she would wait as long as it took. She was a woman who measured. She would stand there until the ice went out.

There was a room in him with a door, and the door had a bolt, and the bolt was a good one. He had put it on at twelve. He had kept the room neat for the rest of his life, in the way a master keeps a schoolroom for the children who are coming. Nothing in it was ever for him.

He said it. "I'm cold. I've been cold since the ferry and I didn't want to be any trouble. Will you stay here a minute?"

It went out into the air and hung there like a breath, and nothing came down on it. Not a laugh. Not a joke after it to catch it. He had not known a sentence could be so bare. His heart was going as it had gone in the schoolroom the first morning with his mouth open on nothing, and he stood in front of her with his hands in his sleeves and nowhere to put his face, and he did not look away.

Her face did a thing he had not seen it do. It came apart, quietly, at the mouth, and she did not put it back.

"I am staying," said Hester.

The taper dropped. It rang once on the ice and rolled. She stepped in, one step, and her hands came up and took hold of his collar, both of them, the coats and all, in two cold fists, and she held on as one holds a rope. He felt the cold of her knuckles through the wool against his throat. He felt them shake.

"Your hands are warm," she said, in a voice that had no breath behind it.

He had not known. He took them out of his sleeves and looked at them, and they were. They glowed against the dark of the ice, in the lantern light, the long white fingers gone the color of a man's, and the heat in them climbed to the wrists and stopped there. Nothing else in him had changed. The cold stood in his feet and his back and behind his eyes as it had all winter, the whole lake's weight of it. Only his hands were warm, and he had put on no wool for them.

He put them on her face.

She made a sound when they touched her. It was very small, and he would have it for the rest of his life. Her cheeks were as cold as the iron of a stove door in the hour before it is lit, and he cupped them, the whole of her jaw in his two palms, his thumbs along the bones under her eyes, and he felt her go still and then lean, a little, her whole weight, into the heat. Her eyes closed. A lash was wet. The scarf had slid and the line of her throat was bare, and under the linen tape she wore against it he felt the pulse go, hard and fast, as a child's does after a race.

"Hester."

"Yes," she said, before he asked.

He kissed her.

It began slowly. It began the way a very careful man puts a foot on new ice, and her mouth was cold, and still, and it held. Then it was not cold. He felt it change. It warmed under his as a hand warms a slate held a long time against the chest, and her fingers let go of his collar and opened and ran flat up the back of his neck, into his hair, and she made the small sound again against his mouth, and the care went out of him all at once.

He had not known how hungry he was. He had known he was cold. He had thought cold was the whole of it, and it was the least of it, a thin covering over a hunger that had been shut in the dark for a long time without a name. It came up through him now like water through the ice. He drew her in, an arm at her back, the other hand still in the cold fall of her hair, and felt her whole length come against him, coat to coat, the buttons of one set hard against the buttons of the other. Her fist closed in the front of his coat and pulled. He bent his head. Her mouth opened. The poles went by behind his shut eyes in a gold line and the ice spoke under them, long and low, and he did not hear it. He heard her breath and his own, and the small damp catch of her mouth leaving his and coming back.

They stopped because they had to. She turned her face into his shoulder, and he held the back of her head in his warm hand, and neither of them said anything. Over her scarf he could see the Ice Road run away to the far shore in its beads of light. His own breath shook. Hers shook against his collar, and she laughed once, short and wet and amazed, a sound like a girl's, and he felt it go through the whole length of her.

"Your hands," she said into his coat.

"I know."

"You are still cold."

"Everywhere else. Yes."

"I am not good at this," said Hester. She said it against his coat, as she might have read a figure off a ledger. "I have never been shown how. I know where every hand goes on a person for the length of a measuring. I do not know where they go after."

"You were extremely good at it."

"That was not a skill."

"No," said Ansel. "I noticed. It was better." He felt her laugh again, and under it the long unsteadiness going out of her, a little at a time, as it goes out of a child who has been crying and has been picked up. On the planks of the quay, the first night, he had looked at the cold and called it weather. He did not call it anything now. It was only the cold, and it stood where it had always stood, and he was not alone in front of it.

Her fingers moved at the back of his neck, once. "I could stay here a minute more."

"I should like that very much," said Ansel, and heard his own voice, which was perfectly steady and perfectly his, and he thought, I asked her, and she said yes. He thought it again, with wonder, like a man turning a coin over in his palm. He had asked, and nothing had been taken. The sky had not come down.

They stayed. The cold stood round them in its great still room, and his hands were warm on her, and the lake turned slowly in its sleep under both their feet.

The morning came up grey and then gold over the water and found him at the cottage window in his shirtsleeves with the stove cold behind him. He had not slept. He had lain on the cot with his hands open on his chest, watching them in the dark, and they had kept their heat until the first light touched the sill and then gone out of him like a lamp turned down. He did not mind that either. He stood and looked at the Ice Road laid out white and clear across the lake, every pole a thin dark line in the morning, and the school step below, and the shore path beyond it where she would walk, and he put the flat of one hand to his mouth. Down on the shingle the first child of the day went by with a slate under her arm, and looked up at the window, and looked quickly away, and he understood that he had been standing there smiling at the glass for a long while with nobody to see him do it but a small girl and the lake. He took the smile off his face. It would not come off. He gave it up and went to find the chalk.

He had thought one kiss would settle the account. A man could carry it for the rest of his life, he had supposed, and be fed by it. He stood at the glass with the sun coming up and was hungrier than he had been at any hour since the quay, and wanted the day over, and the day had not begun.
