By the third night of the cold snap the whole of Larkmere had been tried, and the town had come up short.

Hester had walked it herself, from the quay to the last gate on the lane, with her thumb out. She had stopped at every door that would open to her. She had taken a sock from a child and a muffler from a grandmother and a pair of mittens off a man who held them out with both hands, and she had drawn each one across her thumb and known it before the wool had left his fingers. Neat. Tidy. Sound at every seam. The town knitted as it had always knitted for the cold months, carefully and from duty, so many pairs of socks by the Breaking, and carefully is cold. A town can knit for a hundred winters and be properly dressed in every one of them, and a hard night will go through it like a draught through a loose weave.

It was Mina who called it. She stood in the doorway of the Kettle Room at dusk with her flour-white apron wrung in both fists and said it to the lane in general, in the voice she used for a skein about to tangle. "Every needle in Larkmere. Here. Tonight. Bring your own wool or take mine. Nobody goes home till it's light."

They came. That was the thing Hester had not allowed for. She had thought of the Knit-In as a custom, a thing the old people told, and she had not once thought that the old people had been told it by a woman who had stood at that door in a cold like this one. They came up the lane out of the blue dark with their breath going ahead of them and their needles tucked under their arms, the whole town, the baker and his wife and the three girls from the Net Sheds and a man who had not been seen indoors since the autumn, and they pushed in through the steam and sat down wherever there was wood.

By midnight the Kettle Room had been knit into one piece. The benches stood shoulder to shoulder to the walls. The long counter was heaped with wool in every color the racks had ever dyed, and the fire in the great hearth had been fed to a roar and would be fed all night by whoever was nearest, and the windows had gone white on the inside with breath. Over all of it hung the one sound, a thousand needles at once, dry and small and quick. In the chimney corner Gran sat enthroned on the settle with the copper kettle at her elbow, supervising. Beside her, Tilda Brook knitted with her tongue clamped between her teeth and a lumpy blue length of work trailing off her lap to the floor. At the far end of the long bench by the door, sitting a little apart, as a man does who is not yet sure of the room, Piers Ellory was knitting a sock. He was knitting it atrociously. Nobody had said so. He had a very pleasant look on his face, and the ink on his fingers had gone into the wool.

Hester went down the benches with her thumb. It was what she was for.

She drew each piece across it as it came. Cold. Cold. A mitten for a husband, tidy to the last stitch and cold as slate. Then a woman at the window who was making a cap for a grandson and did not look up, and under the thumb the stitches rose against it with a small steady warmth, as a fleece holds a day's sun. Then a man with scarred hands who was working a square of garter stitch very slowly, and that was warm too. By the end of the second bench she had found nine, and by the end of the room she had found twenty, and she stood in the middle of the floor with the whole town knitting round her and understood how it was going to be done. The warm ones would carry the rest. It would not be the politeness that kept them alive. It would be twenty people in a crowded room who had remembered, for an hour, whom they were knitting for.

She went back to her bench.

Ansel was going round with the wool.

He had been at it since the doors opened. The first woman through the door had put a hank in his hands, and after that nobody had been able to stop him. He went down the room from knitter to knitter with his coats unbuttoned and his cuffs thrust back and his arms out in front of him, a long grey loop of wool slung round his two wrists, and wherever a bench ran low he stopped and held the hank, and a hand reached up and wound. He stood like a swift with its arms spread. He did it without a word of complaint, and a good many words of the other kind, and she heard him from the far side of the room coaxing a baker's wife through a tangle in a voice of such ridiculous gravity that the whole bench had begun to laugh.

"Onion," said Tilda, from the settle, with her tongue between her teeth. "You've got to knit. Everybody has to. Mina said."

"I am a guest in this house," said Ansel, "and Mina is a woman who must be obeyed." He laughed before he said it, and then said, "I am better at holding things."

He said it lightly. He said it to the child. Hester, with a mitten drawn across her thumb, heard the whole room in it and heard the lie in it too, quite plainly, small and sound and tidy at every seam. She had watched him take three blankets from her at dawn, one reluctant turn at a time, like a skein drawn off a swift. A needle, in front of the whole town, was another. He was not refusing the wool. He was refusing to be seen taking it.

She did not say so. She found the only unoccupied foot of bench in the place, which was beside the end of the settle, and sat on it. It was no distance from his reach. She noticed that.

He came round with the hank a quarter of an hour later. There was no one else left that wanted winding. He stood over her with his arms out and said, "You will want a ball. You have been staring at that grey for twenty minutes and I cannot bear it," and sat down beside her on the wood without waiting, because there was no other place to sit, and the whole bench shifted to make a seat of it and closed in.

His thigh lay against hers.

It lay from hip to knee. The bench was too full for it to lie any other way, and neither of them had room to take it back, and she felt it through her skirt and his three coats with a clarity she would have given a great deal not to have. It was not warm. The cold of the lake was in him as it was always in him, and it came through the cloth to her in a long plain chill, and under it, quite separate, like a pale second thread running in a cold fleece, was the thing she knew she would find if she did not look for it. She kept her eyes on her needles. She was working a polite grey scarf for the stock, four plain rows to the inch, no more feeling in it than in a ball band. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady.

"Hold the end," said Ansel, "and I shall do the rest. A hank is a thing I understand."

He drew the loop up over his wrists, and she took the loose end, and he began to turn his hands, and the wool began to come off him in a long steady strand, and she wound it on to her fingers into a ball. It was a thing they had done once before, in a workroom at night, with the lamp between them. It was a good deal closer now. His arms were across her lap, and his hands in front of her face, and every time he turned them, the thick grey loop slid on his wrists, and his elbow brushed her own.

She saw the tremor before he did.

It began in the left hand. It was small, a fine fast shiver in the fingers, and the loop of wool answered it in the small ridge of its turning and began to slip. It slid toward his knuckles. It would catch, and tangle, and take the whole hank down in a snarl in front of a roomful of people who had spent a lifetime learning to look away from a man shaking.

She put her hands over his.

She did it without deciding. They were just there, as a hand goes out to a stitch that is about to drop. She closed her palms over the backs of his hands and her fingers over his fingers, and held the loop on his wrists, and held his hands still. For a moment there was nothing in it but the cold, hers and his. Then the shiver went out of him, under her hands, by degrees, as a loose thread goes slack when the tension is taken up. His breath came out. It came out on her temple, soft and uneven, a man trying to breathe quietly, and a loose strand of her hair lifted and settled.

She did not turn her head. He did not lean.

"Thank you," he said, very low, to the hank.

"You are welcome," said Hester, to the same.

They wound the rest like that. She held his hands and he turned the loop and the ball grew on her fingers, and neither of them said a word that had any wool in it. She could feel his pulse in her palms. She could feel the long cold muscle of his thigh against hers and the weight of his forearm lying along her wrist, and she went on holding the end, and the grey wool ran between them, plain and sound and exactly as it had come off the rack. It was only when she let go of the last turn that she looked down at the polite scarf in her lap.

It was warm.

She had not touched it. It lay across her knee where she had set it down when he sat, and the last stretch of it, the rows she had worked since he had come to the bench, glowed under the lamp with a small unmistakable heat. She laid her thumb against it. A hand's breadth of the plainest grey in the room, knit by a woman keeping her mind politely to one side, and every stitch of it had come out as warm as a hand. She had not meant it. She had not felt it go in. It had gone in while she was looking at his wrists.

She drew her thumb away.

It was worse than she had thought, and she saw it quite coldly, as one sees a flaw in a row that has run too many rows already to be undone. She could keep the wool polite while he was across a room. She could keep it polite on the far side of a door, with the lamp turned low, in a drift against a wall. But he had sat down beside her on a bench with his breath on her hair, and her hands would not stay polite. They had not asked her. They had simply stopped taking her instructions. Whatever she had spent nine winters learning to hold in them had gone out of her fingers into a hand's breadth of plain grey, in a public room, under a lamp, and she could not rip them out, because the whole town was watching its own needles and not one of them was watching hers.

She did not look at him. She felt him not looking at her. They sat thigh to thigh on a crowded bench in the middle of a warm room, with a ball of wool between them, and the room went on knitting.

The night went on. The fire was fed twice and then twice again. The old man with the scarred hands finished his square and held it up to the lamp and then, with great solemnity, handed it to the woman beside him, who took it without a word and put it round the neck of the man on her other side. It went down the bench. By one o'clock Hester had given up counting the warm ones. By two the room had stopped being cold at all. The windows ran. The three girls from the Net Sheds had fallen asleep against one another in a heap with their needles still in their hands, and at the far end of the long bench Piers Ellory had put down the dreadful sock and was watching the fire with a look on his pleasant face that she would not read.

She did not know when the book fell.

She knew only that Tilda had shifted on the settle to reach the wool, and Gran's elbow had gone out, and a large soft square thing had slid off the settle's arm and landed with a slap on the boards at her feet. It lay on its spine. It was the pattern book. It had come open as it fell, with the broad flat sigh of a book that has been opened in one place a great many times, and it lay in the lamplight with its pages spread, and she looked down at it.

It was the ribboned page. The ribbon lay across it, faded, a thin pale line. And the page under the ribbon was pressed. She had never seen a page so pressed. It was not worn at the edge, as a page is worn that has been turned. It was flat, smooth, thin as a worn selvedge, shining where a thumb had rubbed it, as wool shines at an elbow, as if a hand had laid it open and held it down, and held it, and held it. She saw lines of ink gone brown. She saw a crowd of small stiff writing in a hand that was not Gran's. She did not see a single word of it. She leaned forward over the page with her hands on her knees, and did not know why.

A hand came down on it.

It was an old hand, bent at every knuckle, brown with age and quick as a shuttle. It closed over the open cover and the ribbon and the pressed page together and shut the book with a flat sharp clap that turned heads clear down to the girls from the Net Sheds, and Gran, who had been dozing on the settle with her chin in her shawl a moment before, held it shut under her palm and looked at nobody.
