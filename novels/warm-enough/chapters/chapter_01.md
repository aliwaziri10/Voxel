The Heron came into the harbor the way an old teacher comes into a room, slowly, with a great deal of noise and no apology. Her bow found the town quay and bumped it once. The thin ice that had gathered along the pilings whispered, broke, and closed again behind her hull.

Ansel Brandt stood at the rail in every coat he owned. The long brown one went over the grey-green one, which went over the one with the missing button, and under all three was a jacket that had been handsome once. Chalk dust showed at every cuff, no matter how far back he pushed them. He had been warm the whole way across, wind and all, and he would have liked that put on the record.

Larkmere lay ahead of him in the last of the light. Chimney smoke stood straight up from the roofs, the lake lay flat and dark and perfectly still, and along the quay the lanterns waited on their posts with their glass holding the last grey of the sky. Nobody had lit them. The dusk was still making up its mind.

He had spent most of the crossing practicing his first morning. Good morning, he would say, to a room of faces turned up to him, and he would be funny straight away, because a room that has laughed will forgive a new teacher nearly anything. The first joke was ready and so was the second. The third he was less sure of, and he was still working on it when the gangplank came down with a clatter that sent a gull off the nearest bollard.

The deckhand who had coiled line all the way across swung Ansel's bag up from the deck and stood a moment with his hand on the rail. "That's her last run, teacher," he said. "She sleeps in the Net Sheds till the thaw. Nothing crosses after tonight but the ice." He said it kindly, the way you tell a man the shop is shut, and Ansel understood that he was being given the size of the thing. There was one door behind him and it was closing.

"Then I had better be worth the fare," he said, and the deckhand laughed and did not look as though he disagreed.

Ansel hitched the bag higher on his shoulder, told the gull he had meant to arrive quietly, and stepped off the Heron onto the planks of Larkmere.

The cold came with his second step.

The air was crisp and thin and clean, with woodsmoke in it, and the cold ignored the air entirely. It came up from below and entered through his boots, and it reached his knees before he had finished the step; by the third it had gone into the bones of him, the long ones of the legs and the small ones of the hands, the cold of the whole lake at once, as if the water had been waiting under the planks and had decided he would do. He felt it in his back teeth and behind his eyes.

He had no name for it. He decided, on the spot, to call it weather.

"Well," he told the quay at large, and laughed, and the laugh went out of him white. "That's the coats justified."

A woman with her sleeves pushed up was waiting at the foot of the plank with a red blanket over her arm. "Take it, you've gone the color of the lake," she said, and put the blanket into his arms before he could begin to refuse.

He laughed first, because he always did, and then said it was the kindest thing anyone had said about his complexion in a long time, and he would take very good care of it. She looked satisfied. He carried the blanket for exactly as long as it took to find a better home for it.

A little way along, a porter was heaving crates off a handcart with his collar open and his ears gone raw. The blanket went round the man's shoulders in one movement, tucked at the throat the way you send a boy out to play in weather. "You need it more than I do," Ansel said. "I've a great many coats to fall back on."

"Here now," said the porter, to the back of him. Ansel was already walking.

The quay had begun to notice him. He felt it in the way the talk dropped as he passed and picked up again behind him, the frank interest of a town that did not get many new things off the ferry and meant to look at this one properly. A girl pointed at his coats. Her mother lowered the hand for her.

A stout woman mending a net on an upturned crate pressed a brown fringed shawl on him, one that had plainly been loved for years. "It's a cold night for a new man," she said, and tucked it in at his collar herself. He thanked her, and meant it, and the shawl lasted until the next post, where an old woman sat on a coil of rope with her hands drawn up into her sleeves, watching the harbor ice. He unwound it and settled it over her shoulders before she had properly noticed him. She looked at the shawl. She looked at him.

"Well," she said.

"Exactly," said Ansel, and went on.

He had a habit, never examined, of passing a kindness along before it could settle in his hands. It was easier on everyone. Nobody ever had to watch him hold a thing and wonder what he owed for it.

The man with the covered basket was the last of them. He lifted the cloth and the basket breathed out steam, and he put three meat pies into Ansel's gloved hands without a word, as if he were handing over live birds. The heat came through both gloves, a good honest heat, and stopped at the wrists. Nothing went past it. Ansel stood a moment with his hands full of warmth and the rest of him full of lake.

Three children sat in a row at the edge of the quay with their boots swinging over the ice-skinned water, and the smallest was watching the pies with open grief. He crouched, which took a good deal of doing in that many coats, and held them out. Hands came up. The pies were gone with the speed he associated with a back row on the day before a holiday.

"You're an onion," the smallest said, with her mouth already full.

"Layers," Ansel agreed, and laughed, and it went white again. "Don't peel me before supper."

He stood too fast. The quay tilted and came back. He waited until it had finished, hands on his knees, breathing woodsmoke, while the cold went on being there.

The lee of a stack of crates promised to be out of the wind, because there is always one place on a cold night where the air relents. It did not. The cold had come in with him. He stood among the crates with his shoulders up round his ears and found it made no difference whether he faced the water or the town, and he filed the discovery under things to be funny about later. His jokes had begun to come out shorter. He noticed that, and let it pass.

By the end of the quay his arms were light and his hands were empty. He took stock of himself the way he took roll on a first morning: every coat still on, one bag, one letter. He put a hand flat to his chest, over the place beneath the layers where the letter lay folded small, his name across the outside of it in a round, careful, child's hand. It was unopened. He touched it once, through all that cloth, and took the hand away.

Ansel would be no trouble to anyone this winter. He settled it the way he settled a noisy room, all at once, with the flat of his hand on the desk: warm enough, or quiet about it, and nobody put out on his account.

There was a winter of work ahead of him, and a room of children who would look up on the first morning to see whether he was a man who could stand in front of them. If he could not, the post would go to another man by the thaw. He did not intend to find out how that felt.

The quay ran on past the last dark lantern to the Net Sheds, long and low and shuttered, where the Heron would be put to sleep until spring. Against the nearest wall stood a bench, and on the bench a woman was knitting grey wool.

A grey wool scarf was wound up to her jaw. Her face above it was a slate wiped clean and turned to the wall. She had not looked up at the noise of the quay, or his laugh, or the children, though she must have heard all of it, and her hands went on working the needles, quick and exact, the ends of her fingers white.

He was a few paces from her when the ball of yarn slid off her knee. It dropped to the planks and rolled, paying out a long grey line behind it, and bumped against the toe of his boot and stopped.

He bent and picked it up. The yarn was faintly warm in his glove. It was a small warmth, the sort a river stone keeps at the end of a long afternoon, and on any other night he would have missed it. Tonight it stopped him where he stood.

He found the loose end and held the length of it out to her, the ball in one hand, the grey line hanging slack between them across the planks.

She looked at the yarn first. Then at his glove. Her eyes came up as far as his chin and went back to the wool. She took the end between finger and thumb, drew it once across the pad of her thumb as if checking it for harm, and gave a nod, to the yarn.

"It tried to escape," Ansel said. "I stopped it. You can tell it I'm unarmed." He laughed, since nobody else was going to.

"Thank you," she said, and that was all, and she held out her hand for the ball. He gave it to her without touching her fingers. The warmth stayed in his glove a moment after the yarn had gone from it, then drained out, and he closed his hand on nothing as if he could keep it.

Her thumb stopped on the yarn. It lasted the length of a blink, her eyes narrowing at the grey in her fingers as if it had spoken out of turn. Then the thumb moved on, and the frown went wherever she kept such things.

"What are you making?" he asked, since a silence was a room he had never once managed to leave alone.

"A scarf."

"It's very straight."

Her needles paused. "That's the idea," she said, and the needles went on.

She wound the loose end back with small exact turns. The grey piece growing on her needles was perfect. Every edge sat straight, every stitch the twin of the one beside it, and looking at it was like looking at a copybook page with no child anywhere in it. He found he did not want to look at it long, and did not want to leave either, which he put down to the bench being near a wall.

A cane struck the planks behind him. Then again, nearer.

The quay, which had been murmuring over its baskets, went quiet the way a room goes quiet when the door opens. An old woman had come down the planks on a cane of pale wood, both bent hands folded on the crook. She did not look at Ansel. She looked back along the whole length of the quay, at the blankets and baskets and the porter in his red blanket, and when she spoke she pitched it to the back row.

"You have all been very kind," she said. "The new man has thanked every one of you and handed every kindness straight on, and I watched him do it, and I'll say nothing about that." The cane tapped once. "Hester will see to him. The Winter Knitter will see to him. Mark it, the lot of you."

A murmur ran down the quay and settled. Baskets were lifted. A handcart creaked into motion. It had been agreed, apparently, by a great many people who had not consulted him. He had been in Larkmere for the length of one quay and the town had already assigned him.

He opened his mouth to say that he was fine, that he had the coats, that nobody need trouble. Nobody had turned round to hear it. The old woman did not look at him either, though for one moment her eyes passed over him the way a teacher's eyes pass over a desk she has already decided to move to the front.

He did not know what a Winter Knitter was. He knew, now, that the woman on the bench was Hester, and he backed a step, then another, out of the old woman's reach and the quay's attention, until a short stretch of wet planks lay between them.

Hester had not moved. She did not look at the old woman, or at him, or at the quay turning for home. Across the planks her mouth thinned to a single stroke of chalk. Behind her, on the post by the Net Sheds, the first lantern took its flame.
