The Heron shuddered against the quay as thin ice fractured along her hull, a sound like slates cracking under a careless foot. Lanterns were being lit along the harbor wall, their flames catching the dusk and holding it. Ansel Brandt stood on the gangplank in every coat he owned, five layers of wool and cotton and one stolen blanket stitched at the collar, and he was still shaking.

"Onion," a voice called from the quay. "You'll sink the boat before you step off it."

He laughed, the sound short and bright in the cold. "Better than sinking after. At least this way the fish get a warm coat." He clattered down the planks, chalk dust puffing from his cuffs with each step, and the quay folk closed around him like a tide.

A blanket thrust into his arms, rough and smelling of tar. "For the walk up," said the porter, a man with hands like rope knots.

"Thank you," Ansel said, and before the man could blink he had draped it over the shoulders of a boy shivering beside a crate. The boy's eyes went wide. Ansel winked. "Pass it on when you're warm."

A shawl next, soft and grey, pressed by an old woman with a face folded into lines. "The master needs looking after."

"Does he now," Ansel said, and folded it around her instead. "Looks like you need it more than me, ma'am. I've got layers like a river has silt."

A basket of meat pies steamed into his hands, crusts golden and cracking. "Eat now, walk later," the baker's wife commanded.

He distributed them to the children clustered near the Net Sheds, three pies for four children, and watched them negotiate the division with the gravity of a slate arithmetic. The smallest got the biggest piece. He liked that about children. They understood fairness better than the rules said they should.

The cold bit deeper with every step. Not the ordinary cold that a coat could turn away. This was the cold of the whole lake in his bones, a chill that had nothing to do with weather and everything to do with arriving. He had felt it the moment his boot touched the gangplank, a hand closing around his ribs from the inside. He made a joke of it because jokes were buoys. Because if he stopped to name it, the name might be true.

His inner pocket held a folded letter, addressed in a child's round hand. He touched it once, the paper sharp against his fingertips through the wool. Unopened. Unexplained. He would open it when the shaking stopped. If the shaking stopped.

The quay thinned toward the Net Sheds. The Heron's mast stood black against the last light, her sails furled until the thaw. At the far end, on a bench beneath a lantern pole that had not yet been lit, a woman sat knitting. Grey yarn. Her hands moved without looking. A closed face, the kind that had learned to keep its own counsel.

Her ball of yarn rolled free. It bounced across the planks and fetched up against his boot.

Ansel stopped. The shaking in his hands steadied, just for a moment. He picked up the ball, the yarn faintly warm against his glove, a heat that had no business existing in this air. He held the length out to her.

"Dropped your stitch," he said. "Or your ball. One of the two."

She did not look up. Her needles clicked.

A cane tapped the planks behind him, pale ash wood on wet wood. Gran Tansley appeared at his elbow, bent hands curled around the handle, a shawl with one small hole draped over her shoulders.

"The Winter Knitter will see to you," she announced to the quay at large. Her voice carried like a bell across water. "Hester will see to him. He's come for the winter and the winter's come for him."

The woman on the bench did not move. Her mouth thinned.

The words hung in the air between them, spoken to the quay but landing on him like a slate dropped on a desk. He stood with the grey yarn still extended in his gloved hand, the faint warmth of it seeping through the wool into his palm, a small rebellion against the lake cold locked in his ribs.

The woman on the bench did not move. Her mouth thinned.

A lantern flickered to life behind her, the flame catching in the glass and throwing her face into a geometry of shadow and light. He saw the line of her jaw, the angle of her shoulders hunched over the work in her lap, the needles still clicking in a rhythm that had not broken for the cane, the announcement, the name spoken across the water. Hester. The Winter Knitter. The name settled on the quay like frost on a windowpane.

Ansel lowered his hand slowly. The yarn rested in his glove, a small warm weight. He could feel the pulse in his fingers, steady now, the shaking gone quiet the moment the wool touched him. A buoy, he thought. A joke made real.

Gran Tansley leaned on her cane, the pale ash wood tapping once more as she shifted her weight. She did not look at him. Her eyes, the color of river water under ice, stayed on the woman on the bench.

"Walk with me, master," she said. Not a question. "The house is cold. The stove needs feeding. Hester will send what you need."

He wanted to refuse. The words formed behind his teeth, I'm fine, I've managed worse, I don't want to be a burden, but the letter in his pocket pressed against his chest through five layers of coat, and the lake cold bit deeper with every breath, and the warm yarn in his glove was the only honest heat he had felt since the Heron nosed into harbor.

"Lead the way," he said.

He followed her along the quay, past the Net Sheds where the ferry's rigging slept under tarpaulins, past the harbor wall where the lanterns bloomed one by one in the gathering dark. The quay folk melted back into the shadows, their welcome given and received and redistributed. He heard a child's voice behind him, bright and sharp: "Did you see? He gave away the blanket. And the shawl. And the pies."

" He's the new master," another voice answered. "They're all like that."

He smiled into the wind. Let them talk. Let them think he was generous. The truth was simpler and colder: he could not carry what was not his to hold. The blanket belonged to the boy. The shawl belonged to the old woman. The pies belonged to the children who had negotiated their division with the gravity of slate arithmetic. He was only the current that moved them along.

The house. Stone walls, one stove, a window facing the Ice Road where lantern poles stood waiting for the freeze to deepen. He had seen it from the water, a dark shape against the shore. Now he would live in it. Teach in the classroom beside it. Stand in front of a class of children who would watch him for signs of cracking, the way they watched the ice in early winter, testing, waiting, knowing before the adults did when the surface would hold and when it would give.

He had nine winters of wages to send home. He had a folded letter in a child's round hand waiting in his pocket. He had a winter of work ahead and a cold in his bones that no coat could reach, and if he could not stand up in front of that class on the first morning and write his name on the slate without his hand betraying him, he would lose the post before the snow fell deep.

He would not be a burden. He would not be trouble. He would feed the stove and plan the lessons and learn the names of every child before the week was out. He would joke and he would listen and he would give away every warmth that came to him, because warmth was a current and he knew how to swim in it.

The house door opened into a room that smelled of cold ash and damp stone. Gran moved to the stove without invitation, her bent hands finding kindling, arranging it with the economy of someone who had fed fires for eighty winters. Flames caught. Heat began to push back the dark.

"Sit," she said, not unkindly. "The shaking will pass. Or it won't. Either way, you're here now."

Ansel sat on the wooden chair beside the hearth. He held the grey yarn in his lap, the faint warmth spreading through the wool of his glove into his fingers, up his wrist, a thread of heat stitching itself into the cold places. He watched Gran work, the shawl with its one small hole catching the firelight, the cane leaned against the wall within reach.

The door opened again. Cold air swept in, carrying the scent of the lake and the harbor and something else, wool, lanolin, the sharp clean smell of frost on fibre.

He looked up.

Hester stood in the doorway. The grey wool scarf was wound around her throat, the ends tucked into her coat. A linen tape measure hung around her neck under the scarf, the metal end catching the firelight. Her hands were bare, pale against the dark wool of her cuffs, and he saw her draw a length of yarn across her thumb as she stood there, testing it, measuring something in the air between them.

She did not speak. Her mouth, still thin from the quay, did not soften. Her eyes, the color of storm water, moved from Gran to the stove to the chair where he sat with the warm yarn in his glove.

"Hester," Gran said. "The master needs a knit. A true one. The toll is on him hard."

Hester's gaze stayed on him. She said nothing. Her fingers moved at her side, a knitter's motion without needles, counting stitches that existed only in the air.

Ansel felt the laugh rise in his throat, the old reflex, the buoy. He swallowed it. The lake cold pressed against his ribs. The warm yarn in his glove pulsed like a second heartbeat.

"I'm fine," he said. The words tasted like chalk dust. "Truly. You don't need to..."

"Stop," she said. One syllable. Flat as a slate wiped clean. "Being fine at me."

The words hung between them, heavier than the cane, heavier than the shawl with its unmended hole, heavier than the lantern light pooling on the floorboards. He had heard them before, or words like them, from children who saw through the jokes to the shake underneath. But never from an adult. Never from a woman with storm-water eyes and a grey wool scarf and a tape measure at her throat.

The laugh died unborn. His hand tightened on the warm yarn.

Outside, the first lantern on the Ice Road flared to life, a single point of fire stretching across the frozen lake toward the far shore. The light reached through the house window and fell across the floorboards, across the stove, across the space between his chair and the doorway where she stood.

She did not move. Her mouth did not soften. The tape measure at her throat caught the light and threw it back, a small bright line in the dark.
