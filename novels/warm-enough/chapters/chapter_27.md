The voices came up the hill ahead of the light.

They were thin at first, a long way below, hardly more than the wind had been all night, and Ansel took them for the wind. He lay in the grey dark of the hut with his eyes shut and his face against a warmth so good that he refused to look at what it was. Then the thin sound came again, and it had a shape in it, the shape of a name called up a slope. Hester. Then the other name, which was his. Then both together, and a long drawn shout like a ferryman hailing the far bank, and under it the crunch of a great many boots.

He opened his eyes.

Light stood in the cracks of the shutter in hairlines, ruled across the stones. The stove was a black box with its door ajar and a little white ash at the bottom. The roof was low above him. His breath went up and hung. His cheek lay against the shoulder of a grey coat, and under the brown blanket his hand lay in a hand that was not his own, fingers laced through his fingers, palm to palm, warm.

He did not move. He knew the stillness of a child who has just understood that the master is in the room, and he did it now with his whole body. She was awake. He knew it from her breathing, which was level and careful, the breathing of a person taking pains not to disturb a sleeper.

"They are on the path," said Hester, very low. She did not turn her head. "You have perhaps a minute."

"For what?"

"To be a man who fell asleep sitting up against a wall, for the warmth. It is a good account. It is nearly true."

He laughed, which ruined the stillness, and in the laugh he lifted his head off her shoulder and the cold came in.

It did not come in everywhere. That was the first thing he noticed, and it held him there with his mouth half open on the end of the laugh. The whole right side of him, from the shoulder to the knee, where she had lain along him all the night through, was warm. It was warm the way the bench under the window is warm after the sun has gone round, a warmth left behind and slowly spent. The left side of him was the hut. The left side of him was the whole lake. And down the middle ran a line as sharp as a ruled margin, with the heat on one side of it and the old cold on the other, and the Grey lay against his ribs under every coat he owned and was cold as slate on both.

Then she drew her hand out of his.

She did it one finger at a time, without hurry, as a woman lifts a sleeping child's hand off a coverlet, and when she was done his own hand lay open on the blanket with nothing in it. The air found it at once. It was only air. It felt like a slate dropped on a stone floor, the clean flat shock of it, and he closed the hand and opened it again and the shock was still there.

She had a small roll of dark cloth in her other fist, and she pushed it into the front of her coat with a movement so quick and practiced that he would have missed it had he not been watching her hands. He was always watching her hands. He did not ask.

"Hester," he said.

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You were about to say it was a fine night, or that you had never slept better, or that the stove was a credit to the man who stacked the peat." She had got herself upright against the door, and her scarf was being settled, and her face had come back to its ordinary shape, the dry set of it, like a room being put in order before visitors. Only the color was wrong. There was a high red along each cheekbone that had nothing to do with the cold. "Whatever it was, do not."

The first fist struck the door.

It was not a knock. It was the flat of a glove and then another, and a voice outside cried, "Here, it's lit, there's smoke, here!" and the planks shook, and the drift that had banked itself against the sill in the night gave with a long scraping sigh. Hester was on her feet. Ansel got up after her in a tangle of sheepskin and blanket and found, standing, that he had not the least idea what to do with his arms. The blanket was still round his shoulders. It was still round hers, one end of it, because they had been sitting in it together, and she plucked it off as though it had caught fire and flung it on the bench. He caught the other end and held it. It hung between them like a rope between two boats.

The door came in.

Daylight came with it, the thin blue daylight of a morning after weather, so clean and so cold that it hurt. Behind it stood the town. Not the whole of it. It only looked like the whole of it, a crowd of coats and mufflers and breathing in the white doorway, lanterns still lit and paling in their hands, men with snow to the thigh and a woman with a shovel across her shoulders and a boy up on a drift to see over the heads. They had all come to a stop on the threshold at once, the way a class comes to a stop when the master walks in, and there was a silence in which a lantern could be heard to tick.

He knew what they saw. He could have drawn it on a slate: a hut with a cold stove; two people on their feet, one of them in a sheepskin that stood up by itself; and between them, trailing from his fist to the bench, a brown blanket that had been wrapped round both of them to the chin, for a very long time, with the print of it still pressed into their coats.

"Well," said a man at the front, in the voice of a man who has been out all night and is not going to be any stupider than he has to be. "They're not dead."

"Not in the least," said Ansel brightly, and heard it go out into the cold and sit there.

Nobody laughed. Hester said, in her flat clear voice, "Thank you for coming up," and walked out of the hut between the lanterns with her head high and her hands in her sleeves, and the crowd opened for her and shut behind her, and Ansel stood in the door with the blanket in his fist and understood that he was now expected to follow.

He put the blanket on the bench. He folded it first, which was foolish. He set the sheepskin by the stove and looked once at the cold iron and the dent in the floor where two pairs of boots had stood, and then he went out into the light.

The path down was a trench the search party had cut coming up, one boot wide, with the snow standing in walls on either side as high as his hip. They went down it in a line. The party had put him in the middle, in the way a child is put in the middle of a crocodile of children crossing the road, with a fellow ahead and a fellow behind and a good deal of kindly, careful nothing said. Hester was three people in front. He could see the back of her scarf. Every few steps, between the shoulders of the men, the grey of it showed and was gone, and each time it showed he found that he had stopped breathing and started again.

He was happy. That was the dreadful part. He kept looking for the shame that ought to come with a morning like this and it came, it came in plenty, in a long hot tide up the back of his neck, because he was a schoolmaster in a small town and the whole of that town had just watched him come out of a hut with a blanket in his fist and the Winter Knitter ahead of him on the path. But under the shame, quite steady, like the floor under a flooded schoolroom, lay a thing that had no business being there at all. It was glad. It was glad so plainly that he put his chin down into Tilda's scarf to hide it.

The warm side of him kept the road. The cold side kept the weather. At every turn of the trench the wind found his left ear and his left hand and the Grey over his heart, and at every turn he felt the other half of him, the half that had been leaned on, walking in a small private summer. He did not ask it to stop. A river in the thaw does the same, running at the bank and standing in the middle, and he would have marked a pupil down for that comparison and been wrong.

At the foot of the hill the trench opened into the lane, and the lane was full.

The others had waited there. They had waited all night, he understood, round a brazier the neighbors had dragged out onto the trodden snow, with an iron pot slung over it on a pole and a trestle with loaves on it and a row of bowls. The pot steamed. The smell of it came up the lane to meet him, oats and salt, and his whole body turned toward it like a plant on a sill. At the edge of the crowd, set a little apart, stood a very small old woman on a cane of pale ash wood with a shawl over her head. She had a copper kettle at her feet. She did not wave and she did not call out. She stood and watched Hester come down the lane alone, and her eyes went from the top of Hester's scarf to the bottom of her boots and back again, and then, slowly, they lifted and came down the line of men to him.

Gran said nothing. From Gran this was a speech.

A small body hit him at the belt.

It was Tilda. She had come out of the crowd like a thrown stone and wrapped both arms round the middle of him, over the sheepskin and the coats and the cold Grey, and had her face pressed into the buttons, and she was saying into them, very fast, "I said the hut, I said it and I said it and they wouldn't, they said the road, I said you'd have gone to the hut, Gran said so, Gran said you'd be warm enough," and then she stopped and drew a huge wet breath and said, in a quite different voice, "Were you?"

"Was I what?"

"Warm."

He had a hand on her hat. He did not remember putting it there. Over her head he could see Hester at the brazier with her back to him, taking a bowl from a woman in an apron, saying a civil word, and he could see the set of her shoulders, which said that every word he did not say was being listened for.

"I was very comfortable," said Ansel, and heard it, and shut his mouth.

Tilda looked up at him. She had the face of a child who has been given an answer she intends to use later. Her tongue had gone between her teeth. She did not say another word, and he knew she would say several, to several people, before noon.

"Master," said a voice at his elbow, and there was a bowl in his hands.

It had been put there by a neighbor, a broad man with oatmeal on his sleeves who had not taken his eyes off the middle distance since the hill. The bowl was thick and hot and heavy as a loaf. It was full to the rim. A spoon stood upright in it. "Get it down you," said the man to the middle distance, and moved off, and Ansel stood in the lane with the steam coming up into his face and the whole town round him, very quietly eating.

He could not.

It was the old thing and it came up at once, as fast as the laugh. They had been out all night. They had cut a road up a mountain with shovels. There were men in this lane with ice in their beards who had not had a hot mouthful since the dark, and the oats ought to go to them, to the boy on the drift, to Tilda, to the woman with the shovel. He had been warm. He had been, by any reckoning, the best-provided man on the hill. He opened his mouth to say so, brightly, and he knew precisely how it would sound: that he was fine, that he had slept like a king, that he could not possibly, that the others must first. He could feel the shape of it in his teeth. He held the bowl out a little from himself, at arm's length, the way a boy holds out a dead mouse he has been told to carry to the door.

"I couldn't," he said, and laughed. "Really. I had a perfectly good night. The others have been out in it, give it to"

"Eat."

She had not raised her voice. She had not turned round. She stood at the brazier with her own bowl untouched in her hands and her back to him, and she said it the way she gave a measurement, one word flat on the cold air, and it went down the lane and every head in it turned a very little, as heads turn to a bell they have been told to expect.

He stopped.

The laugh went out of him. Nothing replaced it. He stood in the lane with the oats steaming against his chin and the whole of Larkmere pretending to look at the sky, and he felt his face do what he had never in his life permitted it to do in public: it simply showed. It showed the glad and it showed the shame. It showed the night, the whole of it, the warm hand and the cold air after, and he could do nothing about any of it, and he saw the neighbor with the oatmeal sleeves look at him once, sidelong, and look quickly away, kindly, like a man who has seen a boy cry over a dropped slate and means to say nothing for as long as he lives.

Hester turned round.

She did not come nearer. The width of the lane lay between them, with a crowd in it and a brazier and a steaming pot, and she stood on the far side of it with her bowl in her bare hands and her scarf pulled up and the red still high along her cheeks. She looked at him. Her eyes were very grey and very steady, and there was no teasing in them, and no triumph. There was only the plain level attention with which she had watched his throat in the Kettle Room when the first spoon went down, as if the whole business of the morning, the town and the hill and the long night, came down to one small thing she needed to see him do.

His hands closed round the bowl. The heat of it came up through the china into his palms, and it was only heat, and he let it come.

She did not look away. She would not, until he lifted the spoon.

He lifted it.
