The lake froze in the night, the whole of it, from the quay to the far shore.

Ansel knew before he was properly awake. The cottage had gone silent in a way he felt at the back of his ears, as a schoolroom does when the last slate stops scraping, and then out beyond the shore path a long low note started up like a ferry horn heard through a blanket. It climbed. At the top it cracked, sharp as a plank splitting over a knee, and the quiet fell after it. He lay in the Grey with the tin pressed against his ear through the pillow and waited to see whether there would be more. There was not.

At the window he rubbed a clear patch in the frost with the heel of his hand. The skin of ice that had lain over the water for a good while, wrinkled and dull and not to be trusted, was gone. In its place one white floor ran from the harbor to the end of the world, level as a table, with the lantern poles of the Ice Road set along the middle of it like a row of chalk strokes on a board.

He was cold. That was nothing new. The cold had been in him since the Heron, in the long bones of his arms and the hollow behind his knees, and no stove had reached it yet. But he stood at the glass and looked at all that water gone solid from the top to the mud, and it came to him with the flat clarity of a wrong answer found at last that the lake and he had been done the same way. Ice to the marrow, the pair of them. He wore the Grey and three layers besides, and the Grey sat against his ribs, level and exact, and kept its cold in perfect order. It kept nothing out.

"Wonderful," he told the window, and laughed, and the laugh fogged the patch he had cleared.

It was skating day. The children had been informing him of it for a long while, one at a time, each with a small air of menace, and the master's part in it was understood to be standing at the water's edge and counting heads. He put the tin into the inside pocket of the long brown coat, flat against his ribs, and went out.

The path rang under his boots. The school was gathered at the water already in the low pale light, a shoving heap of mufflers and noise, and out beyond them a girl had got out alone and was crossing it in one long straight glide with her arms flung wide, shrieking at the top of her lungs. The shriek struck the far bank and came back thinner.

"Onion!" a boy yelled, and the word ran through them as a joke runs through a back row.

Ansel bowed. There was chalk dust on his cuffs and he made sure it showed. "Good morning. I see the water has made itself useful."

"We're skating, sir," said the largest boy, whose nose had gone the color of a brick.

"So I observe."

"You don't skate, sir."

"What a thing to say to a man before breakfast." He laughed, a good bright one, before the question had finished arriving. "I am told it is mostly a matter of standing up."

The children received this in silence. Then they took it in all at once, and the silence broke into an uproar of pure joy, and a dozen hands had hold of his sleeves. Mr. Brandt was coming out. Mr. Brandt, who knew everything, was going to be bad at anything in front of them.

The largest boy knelt in the snow and strapped a pair of skates over Ansel's boots himself, with the grave attention of a master checking a slate for errors, his tongue clamped in the corner of his mouth. The skates had been handed through a good many feet. Ansel looked at the top of the boy's head, at the cowlick standing up on it, and thought that he must not be moved to tears by a boot buckle in front of the school. He wasn't, quite. He said it was splendid, in a voice that came out a half tone high, and every child on that beach politely did not hear.

A small girl with a red muffler up to her eyes planted herself in front of him. "You bend your knees," she said. "And you don't look at your feet. If you look at your feet you go where your feet go."

"That is excellent advice. It applies to a great deal besides."

"It applies to skating," said the girl, with contempt, and pushed him.

He crossed the shingle in a short flailing run and stepped out with one boot and then the other, and the lake took him, and he stood. For the length of three breaths he did stand, upright and entirely astonished, all his coats hanging off him, and he had time to think that it was easy.

Then the blades went in two directions, which blades should not be able to do, and the floor rose to meet him.

He landed on his back with a noise like a sack of slates dropped from a cart. The shriek that rose from the beach was the happiest sound he had heard since he stepped off the Heron.

He lay and looked at the sky. It was high and thin and scoured, a clean blue, and the cold of the lake rose through every coat and through the Grey and found his own cold waiting, and for a moment he could not have told which of them was the ice and which was the master. They ran together like a stream into a river and made one thing. The sky wheeled. A ring of faces crowded into it, upside down and delighted.

"Are you hurt, sir?"

He laughed before he answered. He always did. The laugh got out ahead of him like a child let out early. "Only my dignity, and that was thin when I arrived."

They hauled him up by the arms, eight of them at once, and stood him on his blades like a coat on a hook, and he fell again. That time he pitched forward, slowly, with great deliberation, the way a tall bookcase goes. The time after he slid sideways into the largest boy, who sat on top of the nearest girl, and the heap of them skated off across the white together, screaming. He fell with his arms out and he fell with his arms folded. He fell once while explaining to a pupil, with a good deal of dignity, why a fall is only a lesson in the direction of the floor.

Every landing let the lake up into him, and every time the children shrieked, and he found he minded the cold less when there were that many people making noise around it. Their laughter was warm. He could stand in it as at a stove. It asked nothing of him except that he go on being absurd, and that he could do all morning.

He had worked back toward the beach in a series of lurches, and was in the act of telling the small girl in the red muffler a clever remark about gravity, when he saw Hester.

She stood at the top of the shingle where the shore path met the beach, in her long coat with the scarf wound to the jaw and both hands pushed deep into her pockets. She was quite still. He did not know how long she had been there. Tilda Brook was out in the middle behind him, going in a wobbling circle with her tongue between her teeth, and Hester was not looking at Tilda.

He raised a hand to her. It was the wrong thing to raise, because his feet left him at once.

He fell backward and flat, and the children shrieked, and he lay with the sky turning slowly overhead and laughed until his ribs hurt, because it was funny. A man in all his coats trying to turn round on a frozen floor in front of the Winter Knitter had nothing left to protect. Then he rolled his head sideways and looked at her.

She had not laughed.

It took him a moment to find out what was wrong, because he had been braced for it as you brace to catch a falling chair. A short dry breath through the nose, one corner of the mouth, anything. It would have been right, and no more than the thing deserved: a grown man in too many coats on his back in front of a roomful of children. He had a joke ready to meet it. But her face did not move. It was closed the way the school door was closed on a holiday, locked and entirely neat, and her mouth, thin by habit, held its line. She was watching him. Not the children and not the lake. She watched him with the steady attention of a person reading a hard line upside down, and her eyes moved once to his mouth, still stretched open on the end of the laugh, and stayed there a second longer than there was any need for.

His laugh ran out. His mouth stayed a little open after it, and he shut it.

He had been laughed at before and he liked it well enough. There was a warmth in it, a plain fire a man could hold his hands out to, and it let him be a fool in company and none the worse. This was the other thing. A crowd looks at a man who falls. One person looks at the place where he has fallen. He lay on the cold of the lake and felt it along the length of his back, and he knew that of everyone on that beach, only one had seen him.

She did not come to the shingle. She did not take a hand out of her pocket. A man lay flat in front of her, and she stood exactly where she was, and he could not have said whether that was the worst manners he had met in Larkmere or the best.

Tilda arrived at speed, failed to stop, and stopped against Ansel's shin.

"Mr. Brandt, Hester's come."

"So I see."

"She's come for me." Tilda sounded like a person reporting a flood. "I haven't even got the hang of it."

"Neither has anyone, I think," said Ansel, "but I am doing it very thoroughly."

"Tilda." Hester's voice crossed the white, level and low, and carried without being raised. "You are wanted at the house."

"I've only just started!"

"Now, please."

Tilda breathed out through her nose and began, with the injured movements of a martyr, to flop into the snow and unbuckle her skates. Ansel got a knee under him. The children had gone a little quiet round him, the way they do when a voice has come into a room that is not the master's. He put his right hand flat on the white to push himself up, and the blade went out from under him, and his weight landed on the heel of that hand with the wrist bent back.

The pain was white and quick. It ran up the bone like a hot wire. He sat hard and his breath left him in a noise he turned into a cough.

His left hand reached inside his coat before his wrist had finished telling him about itself. It found the tin there, flat and whole, the dented corner pressing his ribs. He held it through the cloth for the space of a breath. Only after that did he think of the wrist.

It was the right one. The same wrist that two cold fingers had lain across, steady, in the schoolroom, with his pulse jumping under them like a boy caught out at a window. He flexed them. They closed. It hurt in a way that was not going to go away by dinner, and he thought of the board, and the chalk, and the whole room of small faces that watched him write. A master who could not hold chalk was a master with no use in a school of one master. It would keep until the swelling eased. It would have to.

He pulled the cuff over it, chalk dust and all, and the sleeve of the Grey under that, and tucked it under his other arm as though it were only cold, which it was. The children were crowding round Tilda and her skates. Nobody had seen. He was sure of it.

When he looked up Hester was looking at the cuff.

She had not moved. The scarf stood at her jaw and her hands were in her pockets. But her eyes had gone to the place where the sleeve lay over his wrist, and now they lifted from it, slowly, to his face, and rested there. Nothing in her expression changed. He had the sense of a page being turned and a finger laid on a line.

"I'm fine," he said. He said it a good way before anyone had asked. He laughed after it, which was the wrong order, and it came out thin.

Hester said nothing.

He had half hoped she would put out her hand, and half dreaded it, and he found now that he was glad she had not. He would have taken it. Then she would have had hold of the wrist.

Tilda climbed the shingle in her boots with her skates slung round her neck by their straps, trailing a wake of protest, and fell in at Hester's elbow. "He's fallen over a lot," she reported. "He keeps saying it's wonderful."

"Does he," said Hester.

She turned. She climbed the shore path between the white banks with Tilda trotting at her side and talking, and she did not look round. He watched her all the way, from where he sat, with his wrist folded in his sleeve. The children had begun to shriek again behind him and he did not hear them. Against all that white, her scarf was the only grey there was.
