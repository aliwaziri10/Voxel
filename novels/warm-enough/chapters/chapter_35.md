He took the Green off at dusk, to see what the cold would do without it.

It was a schoolmaster's experiment, small and a little foolish, the sort that goes up on the board in the morning and is wiped before the children arrive. He folded the jumper over the back of the one chair by the stove, with the left cuff hanging, and stood in his shirt in the middle of the cottage and waited to be punished. The stove ticked. The window that faced the lake had gone the blue of old ink, and his own face hung in it over the Ice Road like a lamp left burning in a classroom after the school has gone home.

The punishment did not come, or not all of it. The cold was where it had always been, down in the long bones and behind the eyes. But it had drawn in. He put his hands out in front of him in the dark, and the tips were the plain pink of a man who has been indoors a quarter of an hour. They had been white since the ferry. He turned them over, the way a master tilts a slate to the light to see whether yesterday's sum still shows through, and it did not.

It was lifting. He had nobody to tell but the stove, and he told it. Out on the lake the ice spoke, long and low, a sound he had learned to take for weather and could no longer. Water ran off the eaves in a dozen small voices. The poles would be taken in before the Breaking, he had been told, and after the Breaking the ferry would run, and once the ferry ran there would be nothing left in Larkmere to hold a man against his will. The cold would let go of him. He would be as free as a boy let out at the end of the day, and what he felt about that was nothing like a boy's.

Two knocks sounded at the door, flat and evenly spaced, the knock of a woman who had measured it first.

He knew before he got the latch up, and he laughed before he opened it, because there was no other way to carry that much at once.

Hester stood on the step with her scarf wound to the jaw and a pie carried before her in both hands, as if it were a lamp she had been given to carry through a crowd. Behind her the shore lay black and loud with water. She had brought no lantern. She had taken the shingle, then, and not the lane.

"This was left at the workroom for you," said Hester. "I am to see that you eat it."

"By whom?"

"The woman who brought it. She leaned in my door and explained it at length." Her eyes passed his shoulder to the chair and the jumper hanging over it, and returned to him. "You have taken it off."

"Only to see. It's on the chair. It has been extremely well behaved." He took the pie from her because her fingers were icy through the dish, and set it on the sill, where it sat in its cloth looking like the whole town's good intentions. "You came the long way."

"I walked the way with no doorways."

"Ah," said Ansel. "A sensible route."

She stepped in. She shut the door and stood with her shoulders to it a moment, as though the room might need persuading, and then her hand slid inside the scarf and drew out the linen tape, its loose end trailing to the floor.

His heart did a sum he had not set it.

"I have come to measure you," said Hester. "For a jumper."

He had a reply ready, and it was a good one, about the town's opinion of a man measured twice in a winter. It stayed where it was. He had been measured once before, on a stool in front of the whole school, through every coat he owned, and she had kept a strip of air between her knuckles and his sleeve all the way round. He had held that pose, thinking of the chill in her hands. He had called it concern.

"A whole one," he said.

"A whole one." She looked at the tape and not at him. "I do not know yet whether I can keep it up. I will know when I have the measure. Arms."

They went out easily this time, there being so little on them.

"I have fewer layers than at our last appointment," said Ansel.

"You have one." She looked at the shirt. "It will have to go."

"I was told the coats were the difficulty."

"Linen over linen gives me a shirt's worth of error. I will not knit your shirt."

She said it so flatly, in her workroom voice, that he nearly missed the color that had risen along her jaw above the scarf. He did not miss it. He undid the buttons with fingers that had stopped being pink and become clumsy, and got the shirt over his head, and the stove warmed his back and the draught from the door cut across his front, and he waited in the dark of his own cottage with his skin gone tight all over.

She moved in close. The stove flap threw one red slit of light across the floor and up the front of her coat, and she lifted the tape to it to read. Her fingers had the chill of a pump handle. They set its end at the knob of his shoulder and ran it across the top of his back, over the nape, and she followed it with her free hand spread, so that for the length of the reading her palm lay flat at the base of his neck and each of her fingers made a separate cold.

He did not breathe.

"Breathe," said Hester.

"I'm told I'm very in already."

"Ansel."

He breathed. The tape moved with it. She read it against the red slit with her lips shut and told him nothing, and she never would tell him a number, he saw that now, because the number was not what she was taking. She lowered it. She bent and set her lips, without lifting her eyes, to the point of his shoulder where the reading had ended, and he felt it in the backs of his knees.

"That is the shoulder," said Hester, to the floor.

"I should be delighted to hear about the others."

"You will not."

The sleeve was next. She ran it from the shoulder down the outside of his arm to the wrist, and her thumb found the bare bone there, at the pulse, and pressed. It was the place where she tested every yarn that entered her house. He had stood in a doorway once, in a Kettle Room gone white with steam, and watched that same thumb lie on another man's cuff, and had smiled as though he were being handed a present. He did not smile now. The thumb lay on his own pulse, and his pulse rose under it like a class when the master drops the chalk.

She felt it. He watched her feel it, and watched her not look up.

The chest was done with her arms round him. It passed under his arms and across the breadth of his ribs and the two ends met behind him, and his arms stayed out the whole while because he had been given an instruction and was very good at instructions. Her scarf brushed his breastbone. Her coat hung open, and under it the wool of her jumper was warm from her own walk and held her shape, and when her fingers met at his back they stayed there a breath longer than the reading needed, flat against the skin over his spine.

She moved round to the front again. She laid her thumb on the tape, over the bone of his breast, and kept it there, and he watched her face while she felt what lay under it. His heart knocked against her thumb like a boy at a locked door. She did not read it. She kept her thumb over the pulse and her eyes on nothing, and the red light moved on her mouth.

"Hester."

"Put them down."

He put his arms down. It was the first instruction of the evening that he could not carry out gracefully; they hung at his sides and did not know where they lived. She looked up. Her lips were a very little parted. She rose on her toes and put her lips on his, and they were cold for the length of a breath and then they were not.

It began slowly and did not stay slow. His hands found her without leave from him, under the open coat, under the hem of her jumper behind, and met her skin, bare and cold as a slate carried in from the yard, and she made a small sound against his mouth and arched into the heat of his palms. They were warm. They had been warm since the ice. He spread them flat on the long hollow of her back and she stilled in them like a child being carried. He slid one hand up the spine and into the hair at her nape, where the scarf stopped. The hair was heavy and smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke. He wound it once round his knuckles and tilted her head, and she let him.

"The waist," said Hester, against his mouth, with great dignity, "is not done."

He laughed before he answered. It rose from his heels, helpless, and she felt it through her palms and her mouth. "By all means."

She knelt. He had not expected that, and he kept very still above her with his arms hanging in the air, uncertain what was permitted. She took the tape round his waist, low, over the plain skin above the band of his trousers, and her knuckles pressed the hollow of his hip as she drew it tight. He looked down at the crown of her head, at the grey scarf and the parting in the hair where his fingers had been, and he thought that he would carry the sight of it to his grave and that he would be very glad to. She rested her forehead against his stomach for the space of a breath. He felt her breathe out. Then she rose in one movement, and her mouth found the side of his throat under the jaw and stayed on the pulse there, open and unhurried.

His head went back. He heard himself say her name in a voice a master does not use to a class. His hands were under her jumper again, spread on her skin, and hers lay flat on his chest, both of them, fingers apart on the bare skin. He waited for them to take hold.

They pushed.

It was not much. She put the whole of her weight into it and it amounted to an inch. His palms left her coat at once, slowly, so that it should not look like being sent away, and hung in the air apart from her, open. She had not taken hers off him. They lay flat on his chest where they had pushed. Her mouth was still wet from his, and her breath was hard and plain, and she looked down at her own hands as if a stranger had handed them to her.

"Hester?"

"Everything I make warm, I lose," said Hester.

She said it to his breastbone, in the voice she used to read off a measure, and the stove ticked after it.

"Piers," she said.

He knew. He had known the shape of it since the quay, since a hand held up palm outward over the rail of a sledge, since a canvas bag knocking at his hip with nine winters of other people's rivers in it. He looked past her at the chair. The left cuff of the Green hung over its arm in the red slit of light, warm, he knew, to the very edge, and would be warm when the pair of them were dust. It had never gone cold. The wool was not what she was afraid of.

"I'm not a map," said Ansel.

It sounded low and rather rough. He had not planned it. There was no joke in front of it and none behind. A map was a thing a man drew once, and finished, and carried off down the ice to draw again. He was not that. He did not know how to say what he was instead.

"No." Her thumbs moved against his ribs and stopped. "You haven't been asked to stay," said Hester.

It lay between them in the red light. He heard both of the things in it, as a master hears a wrong answer with the right one folded inside. That she had never asked him. And that, being unasked, he was free to go when the lake let go of him, and nobody would say a word.

He had the word. It was small, one syllable, and he held it behind his teeth. He had asked her for a minute on the ice and been given it, and the sky had stayed where it was. A minute was a thing a man could ask for. The other was a winter, and a spring behind it, and every thaw there would ever be, and it had her name on it, and he could not say her name as a request. He had said it against her throat and into her hair a quarter of an hour ago and it had cost him nothing, because it asked for nothing. Said the other way it asked for her whole life. A thing asked for could be refused. A thing asked of her might turn into a debt, and a kind man on a sledge had put a key in his hand and told him to let her be owed, and he stood in the dark with the key and still did not know the lock.

"Hester," said Ansel, and stopped, because that was all of it that would come.

Her mouth opened, and he watched whatever her own word was get as far as her teeth and go back down. Her palms stayed flat where they were, rising and falling on his breath, and under them his heart went on, quick and entirely honest, telling her all of it.

Out on the lake the ice spoke, long and low, and went on speaking. The stove ticked. The tape hung from her fist to the floor between their feet, and neither of them moved to pick it up.
