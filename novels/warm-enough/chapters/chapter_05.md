The Grey lay folded on the step of the master's cottage with a skin of frost across the top of it, and Ansel stood over it in the first light with the door open behind him and did not understand for a moment that it was his.

There was no note and no string. It lay square on the flagstone the way a good page lies square in a book, one fold, the sleeves tucked under, undyed grey wool with nothing worked into it but the work. He went down on one knee, which in four coats wanted planning, and lifted it in both hands.

It weighed what a jumper ought to weigh. It was very cold.

He turned it over. The seams were closed so well that he hunted for them with his thumbnail and had to be told by the shape of the thing that they existed. There was no loose end and no snag, no place where the work had been hurried or had faltered. He thought of the first rows he had watched her set down at the long table, the clean slate he had called them, and he did the sum on the doorstep without wanting to. She had cast on in the afternoon. A jumper did not happen between supper and morning. He stayed on one knee in the frost and looked at an answer with no working beside it, and it troubled him as much as a child's right answer with no working troubles a master.

"Well," he said to the step. "I did promise to take very good care of it."

He took off the long brown coat and the grey-green one where he stood, so that the frost found him through the three layers that were left, and he drew the Grey down over his head and put the other two on over it.

It fitted. The sleeves stopped at the wrist bone and no farther. The hem sat at the hip and the collar lay flat against his neck, and across the shoulders it was exactly as wide as he was. She had measured him over four coats in front of a roomful of children, with her knuckles held off the cloth, and had taken from that, apparently, everything there was to take.

He pushed the cuff back from his right wrist without quite meaning to. The skin was pale and ordinary. Two fingers had lain across it the afternoon before, cold and entirely steady, and he looked for a mark anyway, the way you look for the place a drop of ink fell. There was none.

The cold of the jumper was level and even, a slate left out on a roof all night, and it went through the three layers beneath it as though they were paper. He stood in his own doorway moved nearly to tears by a jumper and ashamed to the soles of his boots that he was cold in it. The two feelings were of a size, and neither would give way to the other.

"Wonderful," he told the empty cottage, trying it out. It came out a shade too bright. He said it again, lower, the way you drill a word until the whole room has it right.

The shore path was white and hard underfoot, and the lake beside it lay shut under its skin of ice with the lantern poles of the Ice Road marching off across it into the grey. He said the word to all of it as he went, to the poles and the far shore and the low pale sun, practicing the lift in his voice. The ice, which had heard larger lies from larger men, gave nothing back.

The school stove had held its banked coals through the night. He opened the flap and the fire took the new air with a soft sound and a sudden light, and the long window began, slowly, to run. He stood in front of it with his hands out. The heat touched his palms and his face and stopped there, as it had since he stepped off the Heron. He held his hands out all the same.

On the row of pegs by the door the small mittens hung where they had hung. He did not touch them. He knew without touching that they were warm, the way you know a lamp is lit in the next room, and he put his back to them and set the first sum on the board.

The children came up the path in ones and twos with their breath going ahead of them. One of them looked at the board, looked at him, and said "Onion" with deep satisfaction, and the word went down the benches like a coin. He took a bow. It was a good bow, with chalk in it.

The jumper drew them as a fire draws a room. By the second sum they had worked up the nerve to cluster at his elbow while he handed out the slates, and a boy reached out one finger and touched the Grey at the cuff, and snatched the finger back as though the sleeve had bitten it.

"It's cold," he said, in the whisper of the very shocked.

"That's the frost," said Ansel. "I carried it in on my back. Go and stand by the stove, you haven't my coats to keep you."

The boy went. A girl said, with finality, "Hester made it," as if that closed the matter, and it did. Nobody in the room would have weighed the Winter Knitter's work against the word of a single finger. But the boy went on looking back at the cuff from the stove with a frown he was too young to know how to name.

Tilda Brook came last and sat down in the second row without being told, which she did every day and would, he suspected, do on the day of her own funeral. She waited until he had turned from the board. She had her tongue between her teeth.

"Mr. Brandt. What's your favorite color?"

"Is this a test?"

"It's a question."

"Blue," said Ansel, and heard it come out before the laugh did, which was unusual enough that he noticed. "A deep one. The blue of a good ink."

Tilda nodded once, a clerk entering a figure, and her hands went down below the desk. For the length of a breath he saw what they held, a lump of blue wool the size of a fist with two needles stuck through it at angles that did not suggest a plan. Then it was gone into her lap, and the needles began to tick, and her elbows jerked in time to them as she wrestled with it.

He looked at the ceiling. A master who sees everything under the desks is a master who never sleeps. He found a crack in the plaster and gave it his full attention.

"That's Hester's," said Tilda, from below the desk, without looking up. She meant the collar. She had eyes that missed nothing. "Is it warm?"

The room had gone quiet in the way a room does when a question has a trap in it. He felt them all, a whole roomful of small attentive faces that would carry whatever he said down the shore path at dinnertime and up Spindle Lane by supper. If he said the Winter Knitter's work had left him cold, he had insulted her before the whole town and nobody would forgive him. If he admitted he was cold, in a voice that shook, he was a master who was struggling, and the town would begin to watch him as it watched the weather.

"Wonderful," said Ansel.

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the best-made garment I have ever owned. I'd stake the stove on it."

Tilda considered this for as long as it took her to finish a stitch, or to ruin one. "That's still not what I asked," she said. But she let it go, and the needles ticked on beneath her desk with a ferocity that wanted no help from anyone, and the room breathed out. He went back to the board and wrote the second sum with a hand that did not shake. It was a hard sum and they took it like a hill.

Through the afternoon he kept the Grey on under all of it. He wrote with it on and walked the benches in it, and at an hour he could not afterward name he found himself standing at the window with his arms wrapped round his own ribs, looking down the white length of the Ice Road. The jumper sat against his chest, level and cold. It asked for nothing. It had no flaw he could find to be angry at, and he went on looking for one.

Tilda was the last out and the slowest. She stopped in the doorway with one arm behind her back, where the lump of blue was bundled out of sight, and her face had the set of a girl about to ask for a loan.

"Is blue a good color for a scarf?"

"It is the best color for a scarf," said Ansel gravely. "I would not trust a scarf of any other."

"I didn't say it was for anyone."

"I didn't ask."

She looked at him a moment longer, the tongue just showing between her teeth, as if checking the answer for a trick. Then she went out into the white afternoon with her elbow still crooked behind her, and the mended latch closed the door behind her without a sound.

When she had gone he stood in the emptied room with the stove ticking down and the long window gone dark, and went, without deciding to, to the pegs. The nearest mitten hung by its cuff with the thumb worn thin. He slid two fingers in. It was far too small, and it was warm, the plain unhurried warmth of a thing that had been made to be, and he stood with his hand in a child's mitten in an empty schoolroom while the Grey lay across his chest and gave him nothing back.

Then he took his hand out and hung the mitten back exactly as he had found it, cuff over the peg, the thumb turned the way it had been. It seemed important that nobody should know.

The cottage was dark by the time he got back to it, and the stove was cold. He lit it and sat at the table with the lamp and a sheet of paper and a pen from the drawer that had not minded the delay.

He wrote at the top of the page, and then he stopped and looked at what he had written, and drew a line through it, and left the head of the letter bare. The name could go on the outside.

"All is well here. The school is warm and the children are good. I have been given a jumper that fits as though it had been cut round me, and the stove draws like a dream. I am very well. Do not worry on my account."

He read it over. Every line of it was true, which was the beauty of the thing. He had weighed each one as he wrote it, as you check a sum for slips, and not one of them said how he was. He held the pen over the page for a while, wanting to add one more line and finding that he had no true one that would fit in the space.

He folded it in three and wrote the name on the outside, and set it on the table to dry. His other hand had gone, without being told, to the left side of his chest, to the place beneath the Grey and the three layers where the first letter lay folded small, the one in the round child's hand, unopened, where it had lain since the Heron. He pressed it once through all that cloth and took the hand away.

The tin lived in the bottom of his bag, under the spare chalk. He took it out and set it on the table in the lamplight. It was flat and a hand wide, dented at one corner where a heavy thing had once come down on it, the lid scratched pale along the edge where a thumbnail had worried it open and shut a great many times. He did not open it. He turned it once in his hands and weighed it against his palm, and put it back down.

He banked the stove, because he did not know how to leave a stove otherwise, and blew out the lamp. He took off the long brown coat and the grey-green one and laid them over the end of the bed, and he did not take off the Grey. Taking it off would have been an admission, and there was nobody in the room to admit it to, and still he lay down in it with the covers drawn up to his chin.

The cold lay on his chest and did not shift. The window was a pale square, and the stove was a red eye.

He thought of the workroom at the hour the jumper must have been made, the lamp burning, the needles going with nobody there to watch them. He wondered whether she had slept at all, and then wondered why it should matter to him, and he found the answer was in the sleeves, which were exactly the right length.

He reached for the tin on the table, and slid it under the pillow, flat against the ticking, where his ear would find it in the dark. In the corner the stove ticked as it cooled.
