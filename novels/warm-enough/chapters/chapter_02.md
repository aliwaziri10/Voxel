The workroom held the day's heat in its rafters. Drying racks stood in rows along the walls, skeins draped over them in shades of undyed cream and charcoal and the deep blue of lake water at midnight. The stove breathed a steady orange glow. Gran's copper kettle sat on the hob, its lid dancing with steam.

Hester hung her coat on the first hook by the door. The second hook held nothing. It had held nothing for as long as she could remember. She did not look at it twice.

Gran leaned her cane against the worktable. The pale ash wood clicked against the leg. "He's yours."

Hester's fingers found the tape measure at her throat beneath the scarf. The metal end was cold against her collarbone. "He is not mine."

"The toll chose him. The winter chose him. The Knitter takes what comes."

"I take what comes when the wool tells me true. The wool has not spoken."

Gran turned to the stove. She fed a split log into the fire with the economy of motion that came from eighty winters. Sparks rose. "The wool spoke at the quay. You felt it. I saw your hands."

Hester's jaw tightened. "My hands felt a warm ball of yarn. Yarn holds the heat of the hands that made it. Rule one. That is all it means."

"Rule one," Gran repeated. She did not turn. "And rule three. The warmth is set when knit. You cut a length from that ball. You put it in your pocket. You have been testing it across your thumb since you walked through the door."

Hester's hand drifted to her coat pocket. The cut length of grey yarn lay against her fingers, warm as a live coal. She had cut it on the quay while Gran announced him to the harbor. The ball had rolled to his boot. She had taken her shears and severed a strand where it lay against the leather, quick as a dropped stitch.

"It was a sample," she said. "For gauge."

"Keep telling yourself that."

The kettle sighed. Steam curled toward the rafters. Hester watched it rise, straight and true as a selvedge edge.

"I make correct wool," she said. The words fell flat in the warm air. "Correct wool is tidy. Even. True to gauge. Nobody can fault it. A flawless charcoal jumper is a flawless charcoal jumper. It does not lie. It does not promise what it cannot deliver. It is polite, and polite is safe."

"Polite is cold."

"Polite survives."

Gran faced her then. The shawl with its one small hole caught the firelight. The hole had been there since before Hester could remember. Gran had never mended it. "You have survived nine winters on polite. The town has survived nine winters on polite. The newcomers have survived nine winters on polite. But the toll does not care about polite. The toll cares about care."

"The toll is not my concern. The knit is my concern. I will knit him the best jumper Larkmere has ever seen. Undyed grey. Seed stitch at the cuffs, cable at the yoke, gauge so even you could measure it with a breath. It will be perfect. It will be correct. And it will be enough."

Gran's cane tapped the floorboards. Once. Twice. "You said that about the blue one. Half-finished. Never completed. You said that about the green one. Warm at the shoulders, warm at the waist, warm at the left cuff. Cold at the hem because you knit the hem first, politely. You know what warm feels like. You refuse to name it."

Hester's fingers closed on the tape measure. The metal bit into her palm. "I name what is measurable. Tension. Ply. Stitch count. Gauge. Those are real. The rest is story."

"The rest is the only thing that keeps the cold out of a newcomer's bones. The toll is on him hard. You saw him shake. You felt the yarn warm in your glove when you handed it back. You felt your own hands warm without your permission."

That was the truth she would not speak aloud. At the quay, watching him stand in his five coats and his stolen blanket, watching the lake cold bite deeper with every breath, her hands had heated in her cuffs. A flush of warmth up the wrists, into the palms, spreading through the fingers like dye through water. She had not given them permission. They had simply responded, as if the cold in him had called to the skill in her and the skill had answered.

She had stood there with her mouth thin and her needles clicking and her hands burning, and she had hated the betrayal.

"I will knit the grey jumper," she said. "It will be flawless. It will pass every test. The town will have its Winter Knit. The newcomer will have his garment. The toll will be paid."

Gran studied her. The fire caught the lines in her face, the bend of her hands on the cane. "You will knit the grey jumper. And when it is cold, because you knit it polite, you will tell yourself you did your duty. And the boy will wear it like armor. And the winter will take its due."

"The winter takes what it takes. My duty is the knit."

"Your duty is the care. You know the difference. You have always known the difference."

Gran moved to the loft ladder. She climbed slowly, each rung a deliberate choice. At the top she paused, one hand on the trapdoor. "The stove is fed. The kettle is hot. The yarn is in your pocket. The needles are on the table. You have everything you need for a perfect polite jumper. And you have everything you need for a warm one. Only one of them will keep him alive until the Breaking."

The trapdoor closed. The workroom settled into silence broken only by the stove's breath and the kettle's low song.

Hester stood at the long table. Her coat still hung on the first hook. The second hook remained empty. She pulled the cut length of yarn from her pocket and drew it across her thumb.

Warm. Unmistakably warm. The heat of her own hands, captured in the fibre at the moment she cut it, held fast by rule three. The warmth was set when knit. She had not knit this. She had only severed it from the ball where it lay against his boot. But the ball had been in her lap. Her hands had been on it. Her warmth had gone into every ply, and the strand she cut carried it still.

She wanted to throw it into the stove. Watch the grey fibre curl and blacken. Smell the lanolin burn. Be rid of the evidence that her hands had spoken without her consent.

Her fist closed on the warm length. The stove door gaped open, waiting. The firelight threw her shadow long across the drying racks, across the rows of perfect polite skeins, across the empty second hook.

She did not throw it into the fire. The impulse burned clean and left only the ash of decision. Her fingers uncurled. The yarn lay across her palm, a single grey strand glowing in the firelight. She could feel the heat of it seeping into the lines of her hand, a small insistent warmth that had no business surviving the cut.

She carried it to the long table. The surface was scarred by generations of shears and needles, marked by the dark rings of countless kettles. She laid the strand down beside the ball of undyed grey that waited in its basket, the one she had wound the morning before the Heron arrived. The basket held enough for a jumper. The pattern was already fixed in her mind: seed stitch at the cuffs, cable at the yoke, a gauge so even it would hold water.

She sat. The stool was hard. The stove breathed behind her. The kettle sang its low song. She took up her needles, the long ones of polished birch, and cast on.

The first row slid onto the left needle smooth as breath. The second followed. The third. Her hands knew the rhythm. They had known it since before she could read. The stitches formed themselves, even and true, each one locking into the next with the quiet satisfaction of a selvedge drawn tight. No hesitation. No variation. The tension held perfect from the first cast on to the twentieth stitch. She could have measured it with her eyes closed.

This was what she did. This was what she had always done. Correct wool. Polite wool. Wool that answered to gauge and gauge alone.

The grey grew under her fingers. Row after row. The back panel first, then the front. The cable at the yoke would come later, a braid of stitches crossing and recrossing, mathematical and pure. She worked without looking at the pattern book on the shelf. The pattern book had a ribbon marker she never turned to. She did not need it. She had knit the blue one from that page. Half-finished. Never completed. She had knit the green one from memory. Warm at the shoulders. Warm at the waist. Warm at the left cuff. Cold at the hem because the hem had been knit first, politely, before she understood what she was doing.

She understood now. She had understood for nine winters.

The fire popped. A spark climbed the chimney. The kettle lid rattled.

Her hands moved. The yarn flowed. The grey fabric grew heavy in her lap, a rectangle of perfect stitches. She could feel the warmth of the cut strand still ghosting against her thumb where she had tested it. A phantom heat. A memory of fibre.

She did not think about the boy in the classroom. She did not think about the letter in his pocket or the five coats or the blanket he had given away. She did not think about the way the lake cold had bitten him or the way her own hands had heated in their cuffs without her permission. She thought about tension. She thought about ply. She thought about the way a stitch could be twisted or untwisted, the way a row could lean left or right, the way gauge could drift by a fraction over a hundred rows and nobody would know but her.

She knew. She always knew.

The grey jumper would be flawless. The cables would cross at exact intervals. The seed stitch at the cuffs would be crisp as frost on a pane. The cast off would be elastic and even, not too tight, not too loose. The seams would be invisible. The garment would pass every test the town could devise. The newcomer would wear it. The toll would be paid. The winter would take its due.

And the jumper would be cold.

She knew this as she knew the stitch count. Polite knitting held only tidiness. Rule one. Care knit in stayed as warmth. She was not knitting care. She was knitting correctness. The warmth would not be there because she would not put it there. She had decided.

The decision sat in her chest like a dropped stitch she refused to pick up.

Her fingers flew. The birch needles clicked. The grey fabric lengthened. The back panel reached the armhole depth. She bound off the shoulders with a crochet hook, each loop precise. She began the front panel. The cable pattern emerged at the yoke, eight stitches crossing over eight, a rhythm of over and under that she could have worked in her sleep.

The stove door yawned. The fire painted the walls in orange and black. The drying racks held their silent rows of cream and charcoal and deep blue. The second hook by the door remained empty.

She worked until the light through the window faded to the color of bruised plum. She worked until the kettle fell silent and the fire dropped to embers. She worked until her fingers ached and her shoulders held the stiffness of a seam pulled too tight.

Then she stopped. She laid the needles down. The front panel waited, half-finished, the cable frozen mid-cross.

She picked up the cut length of yarn from the table. It was still warm. The heat had not faded. Rule three: the warmth was set when knit. But this had not been knit. This had only been cut from a ball that had rested in her lap while she watched a stranger shake on the quay. Her warmth had gone into the fibre. The strand had kept it.

She drew it across her thumb again. Warm. Unmistakably warm.

The stove door gaped open, waiting. The firelight threw her shadow long across the drying racks, across the rows of perfect polite skeins, across the empty second hook.

Her fist closed on the warm length. ***
