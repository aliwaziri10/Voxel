The door took both of them to shut.

The wind had it by the edge and meant to keep it. Ansel put his shoulder to the planks beside hers and shoved, and for a moment the whole hut leaned on the argument. Then the latch dropped, and the roar went to the far side of the boards, where it stood like a crowd kept out of a schoolroom, enormous and in no hurry to go home.

Inside there was only the dark, and the sound of two people trying to breathe quietly and failing.

"Dunstan Rudd's," said Hester. Her voice came from a little below his elbow. "He will not mind."

"I should mind a great deal if he did," said Ansel, and laughed, because the laugh was ready before he was. It went up in a white cloud and hung there. "I mean to write him a letter of thanks. A long one. With a margin."

She did not answer. He heard her go past him, a short sure scuffle of boots on the packed floor, then the small hard noise of iron on iron, then a spark, and a second, and a thin orange flame stood up out of the black like the first line ruled on a clean slate. By the time he had finished knocking the snow from his sleeves she had the stove going. It was a squat black thing with a door that would not close, and the light it threw was the color of weak tea and reached about as far as the walls, which was not far. The hut was three benches wide and four long. The roof came down so low that he stooped without being told.

He lifted the pack frame off his back and stood it against the door, where it creaked and settled with the cream fleece still strapped to it, a great pale hump that smelled of grease and sheep. The sheepskin Dunstan had pressed on him was stiff as a board with ice and cracked when he bent. He propped it by the stove to thaw.

There was one blanket. It lay folded in the corner where the wall met the floor, brown and scratchy, and it smelled of peat, and he picked it up and held it out to her.

"You first."

"No."

"Hester. You are wearing one coat."

"I am wearing the right amount of coat."

"You are wearing a coat and a scarf and a very bad temper, and the temper is not rated for this weather." He heard it land badly and went on over it, brightly, the way a master goes on over a dropped slate. "I have all of mine. I have the sheepskin, which is only damp, and Tilda's scarf, and I am the best-dressed man on this hill. You take it."

She looked at the blanket. Then she looked at him, a long flat look from the collar to the cuffs, the look she gave a customer's shoulders before she reached for the tape, and whatever the measure came to she kept it to herself. "Put it down," she said. "We will see."

He put it down.

She went and sat against the door with her back to the planks and the pack frame at her shoulder, as far from the stove as the hut allowed, which was the whole of one wall. She drew her knees up and put her arms round them. He had seen her sit like that once before, on the floor of his cottage with the dawn coming, and he knew better than to say so. He took the other end, where the stove could reach him, and set his back against the stones and his hands in his armpits, and told himself he was being sensible.

"I want it noted," he said, "that I am sitting down. I am out of the weather. A child could not fault me."

"A child could fault you for the rope."

"A child has not seen the rope."

She almost answered. He saw the corner of her mouth try it and think better.

The wind leaned on the hut. The stovepipe took the push and rang a thin note, and the note went up and up and broke off. Ansel listened, and found that the cold outside was a stranger to him, and that he was nearly glad to meet it. It was loud. It threw itself at the walls and hunted for the cracks, and where it found one it came in along the floor as a line of hard air that bit at his ankles and the wet hem of his trousers with a plain honest malice. He knew what to do about that. A man could answer it. He could put a coat on it, and a stove, and a wall at his back.

The other cold had no malice in it at all. He had carried it up the hill under the Grey since the end of the autumn, and it did not bite. It sat low in the marrow like the slow reach of a river below a weir, deep and patient, and not in the least interested in the weather overhead, and no stove he had ever knelt at had made it so much as ripple. He could tell the two apart as a ferryman tells a squall from a current. The squall was in the air and the current was in him. Tonight he had both, and the stove was only answering one.

Across the hut Hester had gone very still. It was the stillness of a child holding a sneeze through a recitation. Her jaw was set and white in the little light, and her breath went out in a plume and flattened in the draft from the door, and she kept her hands shoved up the opposite sleeves. He remembered how she had said warm in the shed, with the grease of the fleece shining on her fingers. They had gone back to what they always were.

"How long do these last?" he said.

"Until they stop."

"You are a great comfort to a man."

"I did not say I was."

He laughed. It was a fine small laugh, and it did not warm anything, and he let it go.

The stove ate. He watched it do it. The box beside it held what Dunstan Rudd had stacked there, dark bricks of peat, and each time the iron door ticked he glanced at the stack as a master glances at the last of the chalk, with a face that gave nothing away. The dark outside did not look as though it ran out. It looked as though it had been poured, and was settling.

After a while Hester moved. She did it slowly, so that he would not notice, and he noticed. She took one hand out of her sleeve and reached sideways without looking and laid it flat on the fleece where it hung from the frame, and held it there. She held it the way one holds a hand to a stove. It was nothing but the grease and the body of an old ewe, and it was all there was in the hut that had any give in it, and she did not take her hand away.

He looked at the fire.

He had no thought of offering her anything. She would refuse, and the refusal would have a courtesy in it that he could not have borne. So he fed the stove with a brick and watched it catch, and the light came up for a moment on her face, and went down.

"How much is left?" he said.

She leaned and looked into the box. "Enough to be sorry about."

He fed it another. The wind had found a new voice, lower than before, and it moved along the wall in a long even drag like a keel on gravel. He thought of the lake out there under all that white, and the lantern poles, and the whole Ice Road gone, and a river under it that did not know what weather was. His teeth had begun to go. He pressed his tongue against them to make them stop, and they did not stop.

"Ansel."

"I am fine."

"I did not ask," said Hester. She had not turned her head. She said it to the stove, flatly, like a figure read from a ledger, and then she said the rest in the same voice. "Come here."

He laughed before he answered. The laugh came and he heard that it had nothing behind it, and he sat with his mouth still open on the end of it.

"The stove is nearly done," said Hester. "The wall by the door is colder than the floor and the floor is colder than a blanket. It is arithmetic."

"I see," said Ansel, and did not move.

"Ansel," said Hester, and that was all she said.

It was four steps. He crossed them in the dark with his hand out for the low roof, and she did not look at him as he came, and he sat down against the planks beside her. The wind went through the wood and into his spine, a long shuddering roar with a cold in it that had edges. She lifted the edge of the blanket without a word. He took it, and she spread the rest across her knees and then his, and tucked it under his thigh with the brisk hands of a woman making a bed, and drew her own side in.

There was no room. That was the plain fact of the door and the blanket. His shoulder lay along hers from the point to the elbow, and his arm lay along her arm, and her hip was a warm line at the side of his.

He had sat like this once before, on the ice, with the whole town asleep around them and two tin cups going cold. He had thought then, with the foolish certainty of a man who has just been warmed for the first time in a season, that it was as close as he would ever come. He had been wrong by a blanket.

She was warm.

He was not ready for it. She had sat all night against a wall with her hands up her sleeves, and still where she lay against him there was a long clear line of heat, from the shoulder down to the knee, as steady as the iron of a stove. It came through two coats. Where it touched him the Grey, cold as slate against his skin, seemed to forget what it was for. The lake did not leave. It lay where it had always lain, deep in him and patient. But down the whole right side of him it had a neighbor now, as the bench nearest the window has the sun a minute before the rest of the room.

He put his hand on his knee. He put it down flat under the blanket, as a pupil is told to put a hand on a desk, and he left it there. It was the only thing in the hut he was sure of.

Her head had come to rest near his shoulder. Not on it. An inch of air stood between her temple and the wool, kept there by what he supposed was her pride. Her breath came and went at his collar, where the lumpy blue scarf left a hand's width of bare skin under his jaw, a small warm damp pressure each time, and then a cool one, and then the warm. It was no louder than the stove. He felt it on his throat and it went down the middle of him like a lamp carried the length of a cold passage.

He held still. It was harder than anything he had done on the hill.

"You are not sleeping," said Hester, very low.

"I am keeping watch."

"Against what?"

"The night."

"It is winning."

"I should hate to leave the post," said Ansel, and she breathed once in a way that might have been a laugh. It stirred the collar. He shut his eyes.

The stove ticked and sank. In the long middle of it he noticed that they were breathing together. He had not arranged it. She had not either, he thought, she was too proud. But the cloud from her mouth and the cloud from his went up and fell on the same stroke, as two oars find the same water, and he held his own slow and even for fear of breaking it. The effort of that was nearly more than the cold. He did not know how long he could do it. He did it anyway.

The wind went on. The roof ticked. Once the whole hut shook to its stones and a fine dry dust came down out of the roof, and she flinched against him, one short tightening of her whole side, and then forced herself loose again by degrees, like a fist being talked open. His hand did not move from his knee. It cost him.

The box held one brick, and then it held none.

He saw the last go in. It lay on the iron in its small red nest and caught at the edges, and the light rose up the walls and showed him the stones and the roof, and then it began, quite politely, to come down again. He knew the sound of a day ending. It was the same sound the school stove made at the end of the afternoon, a settling, a stove that had done what it could. The night had a great deal of itself left. The stove had not.

He wanted to ask her for one small thing.

It was very small. It was what any child asks of whoever is nearest on a long road in the dark, and it stood up in his mouth whole, in a good round hand, the way a sentence stands on a board before the chalk has dried. He did not say it. He sat with it there. He turned it over and read it through once, carefully, from the first word to the last, and he did not rub it out.

It was strange, to keep it. He had given away everything he had ever been handed, every coat and every warm hour, so quickly that he had never once found out what the wanting felt like before it was spent. Now it lay in him, whole, and nothing was owed on it. He had not asked, and so she had not been asked, and so no one could refuse. It was his own. He held it as one holds a warm cup in a doorway, a thing that had been given to him by nobody, and for a moment it was enough that it was warm.

Her breath came on his collar. Even. Slow. He knew from the shape of it that she was awake.

The last brick sank. The red went from orange to a darker red and drew in on itself until it was a single coal the size of an eye, and the dark came in around it from every side and stood close. Under the blanket her hand lay on her own knee, an inch from his, and neither of them looked down. He kept his where it was. What he wanted stayed in his mouth, whole and unasked, and the coal went on dimming in the iron, and out in the white the wind leaned on the door as though it too were waiting for him to speak.
