The snow began before noon and went on all afternoon. By the time the light failed it lay along the workroom sill in a smooth heap, and the lantern poles of the Ice Road stood up out of the white a long way off, small and black, going on across the lake to a shore nobody could see.

Hester had banked the stove high, swept the long table bare and spread a clean sheet over it. On the sheet lay the Blue.

It was not a jumper yet. It was a body and no more, worked in one piece from the hem to the underarms, with the front and back joined at the shoulders by a basting thread of pale cotton that was meant to come out again. No sleeves. No collar. The neck stood open, its live stitches held on a length of waste yarn, and down the middle of the front the cable rose out of the ribbing in one thick rope with a twist on either side of it, every crossing sharp, every strand lying where she had put it. She had gone over it that afternoon with the flat of her palm, as she went over the skeins at first frost, and she knew where it was warm. The cable was. The shoulders were, right across, where she had set the warmth in on purpose and held it there row after row like a thumb kept steady on a tension. Only the ribbing at the hem ran cool. She had knit that first, in the hard hours after she tore the other out, with her teeth shut.

She knew where it was warm and she had meant every inch of it. She told herself so twice. It was a matter of gauge.

What she had not managed was the rest. She had come to the armholes and put the needles down, and taken them up, and each time the row came off her hands warm and she put them down again. The sleeves and the collar waited in a ball of blue wool at her elbow. The Grey was finished and did exactly what it had been made to do. The Blue was warm and she could not finish it. Beyond the glass the snow came down on a lake frozen to the mud, and the winter showed no sign of knowing it had an end.

Overhead a board creaked, and a snore came down through the ceiling, slow and even as a long row knit in the dark. Her grandmother had gone up after supper without a remark, which was not like her. Hester had stood at the foot of the stair until the snoring settled. The Brook child had carried word to the school that morning, with orders to say that the Winter Knitter wanted a fitting once the children were let out and to say nothing else. She had gone off with her tongue between her teeth. Hester did not like to think what she had said when she got there.

She took up the ball and drew the loose end across the pad of her thumb, as she drew every yarn she touched, whether she meant to use it or not. It ran smooth and dry. It told her nothing.

He knocked twice, the knock of a man who expected to be told no, and the door opened before she could oblige him, and he came in sideways with snow on his shoulders and chalk on his cuffs. "Good evening. I was sent for by a child, who then told me a great many other things, and I have come to be measured against my will."

"You have been measured."

"Then I have come to be measured twice." He laughed, short and bright, and stamped on the mat. "It is snowing. I thought you might like to know, in case you had been too busy."

"I had noticed."

"A woman of attention." He crossed the floor with his hands out to the stove and his coats beginning to steam, and stopped under the racks. The hanks hung along the poles in their rows, dyed and undyed, quite still. "They look like a class that has been told to hang up its coats and wait quietly."

"They are wool."

"I never said they were not." He turned, and his eye fell on the table. "And that?"

"The front and the back."

"It is blue."

"I had blue."

"A great deal of it, I see." He came closer and looked down at the cable with his hands tucked into his armpits, the way a man looks at a thing in a shop he cannot afford and has decided to admire anyway. "Is it a second jumper? I had thought I was already clothed."

"The Grey is correct."

"It is perfectly correct," Ansel agreed. "It is the most correct thing I own." He laughed again, and the laugh had nowhere to go in the quiet room and came back to him.

"Coats," said Hester.

He stripped them off one at a time, the brown and the grey-green and the one with the missing button and the rest, and hung each on the second hook, the empty one, until the hook sagged and the last few went over the back of the stool. Under them was the Grey. He drew it off over his head with a small gasp when the air got at his shirt, and folded it square on his knee, sleeve over sleeve, the way a swatch is folded for keeping, and laid it on the chair by the stove. Neither of them looked at it again. It sat there flat and exact and gave its cold off into that corner of the room without any fuss.

He stood in his shirtsleeves in the lamplight with his arms folded across his chest. The shirt had gone soft and grey with washing, and chalk had worked into both cuffs. He was thinner than the coats had let her believe. His breath did not show in the warm room, but she watched the long muscles of his forearms draw up against the air all the same, as a hung skein draws up in a draught.

"Arms up," she said.

He put them up. The right cuff slid down toward the elbow and the wrist came out into the light, swollen from the heel of the hand halfway to the bone, mottled purple and a sick yellow at the edges, the skin gone tight and shining. He saw her see it. The arm came down.

"That wants binding," said Hester.

"It wants nothing. I fell over a great many times in front of the school, and it was a triumph." He laughed before he had finished the sentence, and the end of it came out lighter than the start. "Arms up, you said."

She had seen him fall. She had stood at the top of the shingle with her hands in her pockets and watched the blade go and the heel of the hand take it, and she had said nothing. She said nothing now. He put both arms up again, the right a little slower, and she lifted the Blue off the sheet.

It was heavier than it looked. The wool had a body to it, close and springy, and the cable gave it a thickness no plain knit has. She gathered it to the neck opening and brought it down over his hands and his head, and for a moment he was a man in a blue sack with a sound coming out of it that might have been a remark. His head came through. His hair stood up in every direction. The laugh was already waiting in his face.

She pulled the hem down over his ribs. She smoothed the front, and the cable lay up the middle of his chest where she had meant it to lie. She set both palms flat on his shoulders, over the basting thread, to settle the seams.

The wool was warm under her hands.

It was warm the way a stone is that has lain all morning in the sun, a depth of heat that came up out of the knitting and into her palms and did not stop at the skin. She felt it go into her wrists. She saw it reach him. His shoulders went down an inch under her hands, the whole line of him slackening like a skein when the twist is let out, and his eyes closed, and his mouth fell open a little round the start of a word. For one breath he stood in the Blue and took it.

Then his shoulders came up.

She felt it happen. They rose and set, a tension hauled tight on the needle, and his jaw shut and his eyes opened and he laughed, loud and good, at nothing at all. The warmth had not gone out of the wool. It lay under her palms exactly where she had knit it and it would lie there until the wool wore through, but he had gone out of reach of it, as a seam draws shut when the thread is pulled. She stood with her hands on his shoulders and the heat running up her arms, and nobody to give it to.

"It is wonderful," he said.

"Is it warm?"

"Warm as toast. It fits like a, well. It fits." He turned his head to look at the shoulder, and his chin went down against the neck opening and the stitches caught. "Ah. The collar has gone in. I appear to have eaten my own collar."

It had. The collar of his shirt had folded under the raw edge of the neck and stood up crooked on one side. She said "Hold still," in the voice she used on a skein that would not lie, and put up her hand and took the collar between finger and thumb to draw it out.

The cloth came free. She laid it flat. She ran her fingers along the fold to smooth the crease, and the backs of her knuckles went against the nape of his neck, under the edge of the hair, on bare skin.

It was cold. She had known it would be. It was the cold she had read at his wrist, close and hard and all the way through. And then it was not. The cold lay against her knuckles for the length of a breath and gave, the way a stiff fleece gives when it is worked between warm hands, and under it ran a pulse, steady and low, and the skin of her fingers went from white to pink as she watched.

His breath changed. She heard it. He had been breathing in the long loose draws of a man who talks, and now he took a short one and did not let it out. The whole of him went still. She could feel the back of his neck under her knuckles and the faint down on it, and the heat of her own hand on that cold, and she could feel him not moving, with effort, exactly as she kept a tension through a hard row.

It was a collar. It had been smooth a good while ago.

She did not know how long her fingers had rested there after it lay flat. She did not know when they had left off minding her. They had not been polite for longer than she cared to count, she saw, not at his wrist, not on the handle of the scuttle, and they had been going their own way ever since while she went after them with her face composed and called it gauge. Now they lay on the back of a man's neck in her own workroom with her grandmother asleep overhead, and her heart, which she had not consulted, had told the whole room.

She took her hand away.

She took it too fast. The knuckles caught a lock of his hair, and she felt him feel it.

"Well?" he said, to the lamp, and his voice came out a half tone high.

"It sits," said Hester.

"I thought it did. I thought it sat very well." He laughed. There was nobody in it. "I was going to say it fits like a glove, and then I remembered who I was talking to."

"Take it off."

"Now? I had hoped to stand in it a while longer, in case the sleeves arrive."

"There are no sleeves."

"Then I should like to see how I get on without them."

"You will get on without them in the lane." Her voice had gone flat and hard. She heard it and went on. "Arms up. Your coats are on the hook. This stove is not here to warm the shore path, and you are letting my heat out."

"The door is shut."

"Then you are letting it out of the walls."

She had him by the hem already. She dragged the Blue up over his ribs and his chest, and his arms went up because they had been told to, and the wool caught his chin and then his ear, and she hauled it clear of his head, and his hair rose in a crest and settled. He was laughing from under it, helplessly. "I am being evicted from a jumper."

"Yes."

"I was only going to say," he began, and stopped. Her face had come out from behind the wool and he was looking at it. The laugh went out of his mouth and left it a little open. She did not know what he saw there, and she did not wait to learn.

She pushed his coats into his arms. The brown, the grey-green, the one with the missing button, each shoved at his chest before he had hold of the one before, until he stood loaded to the chin and blinking over the pile. The right cuff slid back under the load and the wrist showed, mottled and shining, and she looked at the door.

"The Grey," he said.

"It will keep."

"It is on the chair."

"It is a jumper. It does not wander." She opened the door. The cold came in off the lane, and the snow with it, and the dark standing close behind. "Good night."

"Good night," said Ansel, slowly. He went out into it with every coat he owned on his arm and not one on his back. At the end of the step he turned his head, as though he might say it, whatever it was, and she shut the door on him.

The latch dropped. She stood with her forehead an inch from the wood and the Blue in both hands, and it was still warm.
