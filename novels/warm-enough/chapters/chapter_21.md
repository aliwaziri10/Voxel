The stove went out in the dead hour of the night, and Ansel heard it go, as one hears a clock stop in a quiet room. It was not a sound. It was the end of one.

He had been feeding it by the spoonful since dusk. The cold had come down off the north shore at nightfall, harder than any since the Heron, the kind that makes the lake speak under its ice in long low groans, and he had stood at the school window a good deal longer than there was any reason to, and then gone home and sat on the bed in every coat he owned with the lumpy blue scarf at his throat. The scuttle held dust. He had made the coal last to the thaw by sums, and the sums had not allowed for a night that ate a week of it between dusk and midnight.

He knelt at the stove with the poker anyway, as a master goes on writing on a board that has been rubbed out, and the iron under his hand was as cold as the lid of a well.

That was the least of it. The other cold was the real trouble, the old one, the lake's, that had gone into him on the quay at the end of the autumn and lain ever since in his marrow like a stone at the bottom of a river. On an ordinary night it lay quiet under the coats. On this night it woke. It came up through him in long slow shudders from the bone outward, and it did not matter how many coats lay over it, because it was not coming from outside. He got back onto the bed and pulled the one thin blanket round the Grey and the three coats over that, and his teeth began to go, small and dry and fast, like chalk skittering on a slate.

"I'm fine," he said to the dark.

It did not carry. It was a small sentence in a large cold, and the stone walls gave nothing back. He sat with his knees drawn up and his hands pushed into his armpits and the two letters lying against his ribs, and he understood, quite calmly, that a man alone in this room until morning might not be a man in the morning.

Then a knock came at the door. It was two knocks, short and flat, the knock of a person who had never in her life stopped to ask whether she might.

The latch was stiff with frost and his fingers would not close on it, and he stood working it with the heel of his hand, and when the door came open the night came in with a gust of white, and she stood in it. She had snow on her shoulders and her grey scarf pulled up to her eyes. She had a black iron pot clasped against her chest with its lid tied down by a cloth, and a heavy bundle of blankets on her other arm, and her breath went up past her face in a long plume.

"My grandmother says your chimney has stopped," said Hester. "She can see it from the loft. She has never been wrong about a chimney."

"I'm fine," said Ansel.

"Yes," said Hester. "I can hear your teeth."

He laughed. It came out of him before he had decided anything, high and cracked, and it turned halfway into the shudder and shook the rest out of him, and he stood holding the frame, rattling like a loose shutter. She did not wait to be asked in. She came past him with her shoulder an inch from his chest and the pot knocking against her coat, and he smelled soup and snow and the faint clean smell of oiled wool, and he shut the door behind her because there was nothing else to do with a door.

She put the pot on the dead stove. "The iron will hold it," she said, and he saw that she had thought of that the whole length of the lane, with her arms full. She laid the blankets on the table. They were grey, folded square, heavy as a sleeping child, and they smelled of cedar and of a loft where things are kept for years. Then she walked back to the door and sat down on the floor with her back against it.

"What are you doing?" said Ansel.

"I am working." She drew a roll of cloth from her coat, and out of it a long tube of moss green on four needles, and he knew it at once, because he had stood in a lane with his arm held out while it was pinned to his sleeve. "A sleeve wants a quiet room. Mine is full of my grandmother's opinions."

"You cannot sit on the floor."

"I have sat on worse."

"Take the bed. I insist. I shall be perfectly happy against the wall, I am a great hand with a wall."

"No," said Hester.

It was not the first time he had heard her say it, and it was a different word every time. She did not look up. The needles had begun to move. "The soup is for you. The blankets are for you. I shall be here until you have taken both. Sit where you like."

He sat against the wall beside the stove, the length of the room from the door, with the whole cold floor lying between them like a river. He looked at the pot, as he had once looked at a bowl in the Kettle Room, and thought: it is only soup. It was a thing he had learned, and he did it. He got up on his stiff legs and took the cloth off the iron, and the steam went up into the dark in a slow white column, and he ate from the pot with the spoon she had tied to the handle, standing in his coats at the dead stove with his eyes shut. It went down into him like a coin into a well, and he waited to hear it land, and it did not land.

He did not touch the blankets. He sat down again with his back to the stone.

There was no lamp. He had not lit one, and she did not ask for one, and the only light was the grey seep of the window through the frost ferns, which showed him the shape of her against the door and no more. She did not need it. Her hands went on in the dark with a small dry whisper, the needles ticking together, even and slow, and it was a sound he knew from the schoolroom in the last hour of the day, when a child far down the benches is writing a long word on a slate and thinks nobody can hear. He listened to it. It was a long time before he noticed that the shudders had grown further apart.

The cold had not gone. He wished to be exact about that. It was in him to the marrow, where it had always been, and the coats lay over it to no purpose. But it had company now, like a long dark crossing with a lantern set on a post ahead. It did not make the water any warmer. It made it a thing one could cross.

He thought of a ladder, and a lantern shaking on a hook, and a woman standing in the snow below with her hands in her pockets, saying what she had come to say. He had gone up to her roof in the dark and been found shaking. He saw now, without any comfort in it, that it had been a way of knocking.

"Why was your scuttle empty?" said Hester, to her needles.

"I ate it."

"Ansel."

"Coal is dear," he said, "and I have never been good at the arithmetic of a scuttle." Then he stopped, because she had stopped. The needles were quite still. The shape against the door did not turn its head. It only waited, with the stern patience of a person who has all night.

He had not meant to say it. He began, in a voice low and not at all bright, like a man going down a ladder in the dark with his foot feeling for each rung.

"I am the eldest," he said. "I am from a house in a river town a long way down the water, and there is a great deal of house and not much that comes in. The one who is oldest is the one who carries. I have carried it since before I was a man. It goes down the water at every quarter-day in a folded paper, nearly the whole of it, and I keep enough for coal and bread and chalk." He gave a small laugh. "I allowed for the coal, you see. I merely did not allow for this."

"And what comes back?"

"A letter. In my sister's hand." His palm had gone flat to his ribs without his knowing it. "It says that everyone is well. It has said so every time. She has never once said that anything is wanted, and so I have never found it possible to send less."

"You have not opened the last two."

He turned his head. "How do you know that?"

"You touch them when you think no one is looking. You did it on the quay. A man who has read a letter puts it away. You are afraid of what is in it."

"I am afraid," said Ansel carefully, "that it will say everyone is well."

It lay on the dark between them, the whole absurd truth of it, and he waited for the laugh that would come after, and the laugh did not come. He sat with his hands in his armpits and was exposed to the root.

Hester did not say that she was sorry. She was silent a long while, and her hands went on, and then she said, in the flat dry voice she kept for plain fact, "I knit from the first frost to the Breaking. In between I am asked nothing I wish to answer. I have liked it that way for a long time."

It was not an answer to his. It was another thing laid down beside it, the same size, and he understood that it was all she had, and that she had given it.

"It is a good way to live," he said.

"It is a way to be warm in," said Hester. "That is another matter."

After that neither of them spoke, and the grey in the window grew by degrees too slow to see. The frost ferns went from blue to pearl. He did not ask what she had come from, and she did not say, and the thing that stood between them in the dark was exactly the size of the room and was not mentioned.

In the small hours the air by the door began to be warmer by the width of a finger, and then by the width of a hand, and the warmth lay along the floor toward him as the first light lies along a table, and stopped at his feet. It did not reach his bones. It waited in front of them, patient, like a ferry at a landing for a passenger who has not decided to step down.

He looked at the blankets.

They were only blankets. She had carried them the length of the lane in the snow, and sat all night on stone with her back to the door so that no wind should come under it. He knew exactly what taking them was. It could not be passed to the next child or laughed off. It was a thing he would owe, and she would know he held it, and it would sit in him afterward like a stone in a pocket.

He got up on stiff legs and crossed the floor. He took the top blanket in both hands, and it was heavier than it looked, and he stood holding it with his head down. It cost him. He felt it go, a small hard coin out of a purse he had never in his life opened, and nothing came back in its place but the weight of the wool.

He put it round his shoulders over the three coats. He sat down against the wall.

"Thank you," said Ansel, with no joke in it. It was the first time he had said it to her that way, and the room seemed to hold still to hear.

The needles stopped.

"The other two as well," said Hester.

He took them. He laid the second over his knees and the third over his feet and sat bundled to the chin in grey, like a parcel at the foot of a stair, and she did not look round, and he said nothing clever. The cold in his marrow shuddered once, long, from the base of the spine to the nape. It did not leave. But it moved over a little to make room.

The window had gone white. A thin gold line lay along the top of the ferns, and then the light came through them all at once and laid a long pale square across the floor, and the dawn stood in the cottage with them like a third party.

She put her work down.

She did it slowly. She rolled the green tube into its cloth and laid it on the boards beside her, and got up from the floor in stages, one hand on the door, like a woman who has sat a night on stone and does not mean to say so. Her face was grey with it. For a moment she stood in the pale square of light looking at him, with her bare hands hanging at her sides and the knuckles white.

Then she walked the whole width of the room.

He did not breathe. He watched her come past the cold stove and the pot and stop in front of him and go down on her knees on the stone. She did not speak. She reached out and took his hands out of his armpits, one and then the other, as one lifts a child's hands from a slate, and held them a moment in the air between them. They were shaking. She saw it, and he saw her see it.

Then she covered them with hers.

Her hands were cold. They were as cold as they had always been, as cold as a slate left out all night, and the shock of it went up his arms and he almost drew back. He did not. They lay over his, palm to knuckle, her thumbs laid across the insides of his wrists where the pulse lay, and for a long breath nothing happened but the cold, hers and his, one on the other like two stones of the same river.

Then it was not cold.

It came up in her palms slowly, out of nowhere that he could see, a small dry heat like the first warm patch of floor in a morning room. It spread across the backs of his hands and into the fingers and down to the wrists, where her thumbs lay, and under them his pulse jumped once, hard, and then went on beside hers. He felt her breath catch and let go. She was looking at their hands. She was not looking at his face, and she did not look up, and he understood that she could not.

He did not turn his hands over. He did not close his fingers. He held as still as a man holds who has been handed a vessel too full, and must not spill it.

The light stood on the floor. Her hands lay over his, and neither of them moved.
