The frost came down on the harbor in the night, and by afternoon it had climbed the workroom windows in white ferns. Hester lit the lamp early. The skeins at the far end of the racks had stiffened in their rows, and she went down the line with her palm flat against each one, as she did at every first frost, reading which would take the cold and which would stand it. Nothing was wrong with any of them. She went down the line a second time.

Gran sat on her stool by the stove with the cane across her knees and the copper kettle ticking behind her, and the lamp found the hole at the edge of her shawl. She had said nothing about the second stool set out in the middle of the floor, or about the needles laid ready on the long table beside a hank of undyed grey, and the nothing she had said filled a good part of the room.

"He's late," Hester said.

"He isn't late. You're early."

"I am exactly on time."

"You have been exactly on time since noon."

Hester went back to the racks. The coat hung on the first hook by the door with its right pocket still low, and she kept her back to it.

The knock came as two raps, the knock of a man who expected to be told no. Then the door opened and Ansel Brandt came in sideways, because of the coats, and brought the frost in on the shoulders of the long brown one. Chalk lay on him to the elbows. His breath went white in a room with a stove in it, and he laughed at that before anyone could remark on it.

"Good afternoon. I was told there would be a stool."

"There's a stool," said Hester.

"I have had a great deal of practice with stools lately. I am becoming a connoisseur." He crossed the floor and sat, and his knee knocked the leg of the long table as he did. "Madam. A pleasure."

"Gran will do," said Gran. "The whole town uses it. Tea."

He opened his mouth to say he couldn't, and Gran had a cup in both his hands before the word was out. He took it, because it was held out and there was no way not to, and he held it the way you hold a hank you have been asked to mind for a neighbor. He did not drink. After a moment he set it on the table beside the lamp, a little apart from himself, where it steamed and was ignored.

"Wrist," said Hester.

She sat at the corner of the long table, so that her arm and his lay along the same scrubbed boards with an elbow's length between them. He gave her his right hand, palm up, like a skein held out to be wound. She took the cuff of the long brown coat between finger and thumb and found cloth and nothing else. She had measured him over every coat he owned in front of a roomful of children and read nothing off him then either. The cloth had been in the way.

"Cuffs," she said.

"All of them?"

"All of them."

He pushed up the brown cuff, and under it the grey-green, and under that the cuff of the coat with the missing button, and last a shirt gone soft and grey with washing. It took a while. At each layer he found a remark to fill the time: that the grey-green had been sold to him as lucky, that the brown was the only coat that had ever loved him back.

"Lucky for the seller," said Gran from her corner, and that was the whole of her remark.

Hester watched his hands. Chalk had worked into the creases of the knuckles and the skin there was cracked. The cuffs rode up and bunched in a ring of cloth below his elbow, and the wrist came out of the last of them bare.

It was pale and fine, narrower than his size had led her to expect, and a vein ran blue under the skin like a thread of indigo in an undyed ground.

She laid two fingers across the inside of it.

The cold came up through her fingertips at once. She had known it would. It entered the way wool takes up damp from a wet floor, steadily and from below, and she felt it reach the first joint of each finger and go on. A man who was cold on the surface, as most newcomers were, gave up a little warmth under a firm hand. He gave up nothing. The skin was smooth and hard, a worsted spun tight and left out overnight, cold all through with no give in the twist.

That much she had expected. The pulse was another matter.

It was slow. She waited for it to hurry, because a man who laughed before he answered, who talked through every cuff, who made a roomful of children laugh, ought to have a wrist that raced. It did not race. It went on level and low, a plain row knit at leisure, one stroke laid down after another with no flourish and no catch. She held her fingers where they were and listened to the quietest wrist she had ever read, and could not square it with the rest of him.

"Is it true you can tell a man's fortune from his wrist?" Ansel said. "I would like to outlive my coats."

"Hold still."

"I am holding still. This is how still looks on me."

"Then hold stiller."

He stopped talking. The workroom took the silence in and the stove filled it with small ticks. His forearm drew tight under her wrist, and he looked at the lamp rather than at her, and Hester found that she was looking at the lamp too.

Under her fingers the pulse changed. One stroke came early. The rest followed it up, a little faster, a row picked up at the wrong tension and carried on before anyone could stop it, and she counted and lost the count and began again. Her own fingertips had stopped being cold. She noticed it the way a tight row eases when the tension goes out of it. The white had gone out of the ends of them where they lay on him, and the blood had come out to meet the cold, pink first and then nearer red, and it went on while she watched, and she gave it no permission and could not find the place to refuse it.

She did not move her thumb. She did not let her hold go soft, or tighten either. She kept it exactly as it was, as she would have kept a tension through a hard row.

"Well?" said Ansel, to the lamp.

"You're colder than the lake."

"I'm fine," he said, and laughed, and the pulse stumbled under her two fingers when he did.

She did not say anything to that. She let it lie. A laugh that made the wrist stumble was a row knit to look well and nothing more, and she knew a good deal about those.

She turned that over as she would a flaw in a hank. The wrist had told her nothing while he talked. It had spoken only when she told him to be quiet, and then it had spoken at once.

She lifted her hand. The print of her two fingers stayed white on his skin and filled in slowly from the edges, pink and then the color of the rest of him, and she looked at it for exactly as long as it took to be sure it was only skin. Then she reached for the needles.

"Is it for me?" he asked.

"It is."

"I'll take very good care of it."

She heard him say it. She had told Gran he would give the jumper away by the first frost, and here was the frost, and here he was promising to take care of it in the voice of a man who had already decided whose shoulders it would go on.

"It will be close," she said. "Dense, a firm gauge, and heavy. You run cold to the core and the wool has to go the whole way in. Wool doesn't hurry."

"How long is not hurrying?"

"Until it's done."

"That is the most reassuring thing anyone has told me since I got off the boat," said Ansel, and she could not tell whether he was joking, and neither, from his face, could he.

She drew the grey across the pad of her thumb. Cool, dry, plain two-ply, a little hard in the twist, her own stock, with nothing in it to fault. It was not the length that lay in the pocket of the coat behind her. She did not think of that. She cast on.

The stitches went onto the needle one after another, each the twin of the last, and she knit the first row without looking at her hands, because her hands knew. Then the second. The wool came off the needle and lay down in a flat, close fabric, every stitch seated, every edge true, and Ansel leaned over the table to look with the whole of his attention.

"That's the neatest thing I have ever seen," he said. "It's a clean slate."

"It's the beginning of a jumper."

"I don't doubt it. I only meant that if I wrote on it, I'd be ashamed of my hand."

She knit another row. The fabric lay flat as a held breath, and it was cold to her fingers, cold with the right sort of cold, the cold of a thing that wants nothing. It held tidiness and held nothing else. She had known it would, and she had decided it, and she knit the next row to the same gauge exactly, and watched him watch it, and felt the lack in it for the first time in many winters as a thing with a shape.

She had never been slow. In all her winters she had cast on for a newcomer in the first hour after the Heron came in, and gone on casting, and finished before the harbor had finished freezing. This one had stood on the quay and walked into the school and been measured and been read, and only now was she casting on, with the frost already at the glass. A man with the whole lake in his bones and no true jumper over it would be a man who could not stand up before his pupils by deep winter, and then there would be no master and no school to speak of. She knew what it came to. She knit the next row.

"Do you ever look?" Ansel said.

"At what?"

"Your hands."

"I don't have to."

"That is the saddest and most impressive thing I've heard today," said Ansel, and then, because the kettle ticked and Gran did not move and nobody laughed, he stood up too quickly and the stool scraped. "I'll be keeping you from supper. The school, the stove, I have a great deal to put right before the morning." He was already pulling the cuffs down, one over another, the shirt, the coat with the missing button, the grey-green, the brown. He did it fast, the way a row is ripped back when it has gone wrong.

"Your tea," said Gran.

"It was perfect," said Ansel, who had not touched it, and he was already at the door.

The coal scuttle stood beside it where it had been set down that morning, full to the lip. Hester saw him see it. She saw him decide, in the time it took to cross the floor, that Gran should not have to carry it to the stove, and that he was the person to do it, and she half rose and he was already there. He closed his bare hand on the handle and heaved.

The scuttle came up off the boards. His arm shook under the weight, and then the shaking ran through his shoulder and into the coats, and coal ticked against the iron as the whole thing swung a hand's breadth off true. He laughed. It came out thin.

She crossed the floor without a word. Her hand closed over the handle beside his, her knuckles against his knuckles for the length of one breath, and she took the weight. The cold of his hand reached her through the iron. His fingers opened, because the weight was gone from them and there was nothing to hold. She carried the scuttle to the stove and set it down by the hob and straightened up with her back to him. She told herself it was the floor. Coal on boards she had scrubbed that morning, and a man bent over it with his hands shaking: anyone would have taken the scuttle. Her own hand was still warm from the handle. It had no business being, with the iron as cold as it was.

"I was only trying," said Ansel.

She did not turn round.

"Right," he said. "Fine." The door opened on the dark and the frost, and the latch took it behind him, and the cold he had brought in thinned and drew out through the gap.

The cup stood full beside the lamp, gone cold without a mouthful out of it.

Hester went back to the table. She sat. The first rows of the Grey lay flat on the scrubbed boards in the light, as even as a selvedge, and she looked at them and did not pick up the needles. On the far side of the stove Gran bent over the copper kettle with her face turned down. Her mouth had moved, a very little, at one corner. It was too small to be seen by anyone who was not looking.
