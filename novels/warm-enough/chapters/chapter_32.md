The thaw began in the gutters. Hester heard it from the workroom a long while before the light, a thin steady ticking off the eaves, and under it, from far out, the lake, booming once and going quiet like a cable left to settle. The far racks had gone soft. Skeins that had stiffened at their tips all winter hung limp on the poles and smelled of wet wool, and the window ran with damp along its sill.

She had not slept. She had come in off the shore with the ice still in her coat and a print of his forehead still on her own, and she had sat down at the long table and cast on, because her fingers wanted work and she had nothing else to give them.

She had knit two swatches. The first she had knit by habit, in the cream she took down from the top pole in the dark, a hand's width of plain stocking stitch with every edge ruled straight, and when it was done she had looked at it a long time and then cast on again. The second was the same wool, the same needles, the same hands. At its left edge, high up, she had let one stitch go. She had done it with the point of the needle, on purpose, and watched it run down through every row to the cast-on, one open ladder no wider than a thread. In all her winters at this table she had never dropped a stitch she did not mean to be rid of at once. She had not picked this one up.

She laid the two side by side under the lamp, a finger's width apart, as she had once laid a hank beside a cut length of yarn. The first lay there being correct. The second lay there being warm. She drew it across her thumb and it was warm the whole way across, as the cuff of a good jumper is warm, and along the ladder the heat did what it did nowhere else in the piece. It opened. It lay along the gap like a palm held out flat.

The hob was bare. Gran had carried the copper kettle up the loft stair at first light, which she had never once done, and had given no reason for it, and the loft door stood open the width of a hand.

He came when it was light, as she had told him. She had said it at the school step with the lamp gone out, to the lane and not to him, in a voice she had not authorized. One knock. It was a quiet knock, and she knew it for his by the quiet.

"It's open."

He stood in the doorway in the Green with Tilda's lumpy blue scarf wound twice round his neck. His hair lay flat on one side. He had not slept either; she could see it in the skin under his eyes, and there was chalk at both cuffs, as there always was. The Green sat on him as she had made it, holding its heat from the shoulders to the waist, and she knew the row where the warmth stopped, and found she was looking at it.

"Good morning," said Ansel, and drew breath to laugh, and did not. "I had four jokes on the way over. I've dropped them in the lane."

"Shut the door. The warm is going."

He shut it. "I was told first light."

"You were."

"It is first light," he said, a little anxiously, as if she might dispute it.

"Sit."

She had set the second stool at the corner of the table before he came. She did not ask herself why it was the near corner and not the far. He sat, and his knee came to rest an inch from hers, and he looked at the two squares of cream and at the needles laid beside them, straight, and then straight again.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"No."

"Nor did I. I marked a whole page of sums last night and got every one of them wrong. The children will be delighted." He touched the Green at his ribs, and found with two fingers the line where the heat gave out, and she watched him find it. "Here. It's very exact of you."

"I knit the hem first. I was careful. I could not keep it up past the ribbing." She heard what she had said and shut her mouth on the rest.

"I like the hem," said Ansel. "It's the only part of the Green with no opinions."

She put the warm swatch into his hands before she could find another use for her own.

"Hold that."

His fingers closed on it. The ends of them were white, as they always were, and she watched the white and waited for it to last. It went slowly, from the tips inward, to the color of an ordinary hand, and he looked down at what he held.

"It's warm," he said.

"Yes."

"I'm told I'm not to say wonderful."

"You may, if it is."

He turned it over. His fingertip found the left edge and stopped on the ladder, and he said, very gently, the way one tells a friend about a loose button, "You've dropped a stitch."

"I dropped it."

"On purpose."

"On purpose. Now that one."

She gave him the first swatch. He took it in the other hand, and she saw the difference arrive in his face before he spoke, a small flinch, quickly put away.

"That's a slate," said Ansel. "That's a slate left out all night."

"It is correct."

"It's beautiful. It's perfectly beautiful. It's the Grey." He laughed, short and surprised, and the laugh had nothing behind it, and he only looked at her. "It's the same wool."

"Same wool. Same hands. Same night." She kept her eyes on the cloth, because the cloth was easier than his face. "A knit with no flaw has no way in. I have made them for nine winters, and there is not a seam in one that anyone could find. I am very good at them. Nobody has ever been warm in one, and I knew that, and I made them anyway, because they cannot be faulted." Her thumb found the ladder on the warm swatch where it lay in his palm, and she laid it in the open loop, and the heat came up round it. "My grandmother has a saying. I told her it was a rule for knitters who wanted to be wasted on a stranger."

He waited. He had learned that, she saw, in the last of the winter. He let a silence sit and did not fill it.

"Leave one door open," said Hester.

The open loop lay under her, plain as a thread. She felt him look at it, and then at her. He bent. He did it to see, as anyone bends to a dropped stitch, and she bent too, because her thumb was in the gap and a person cannot show a gap from a yard away. His own thumb came down beside hers. The warmth went up through both of them, and the flinch he had made over the cold one went out of his shoulders by degrees.

It had come to an inch the way a ladder comes, one row and then the next, with no single moment she could have caught and stopped, and now she was an inch from his mouth with her thumb in the open loop and his breath on her lips, uneven, carrying the cold of the lane, and every instruction she had given her fingers in all her winters went out of them together like a cast-off row let go. Her thumb did not move. His stayed beside it. Neither of them took what was offered, and the whole room leaned.

Overhead the kettle began to scream.

It went up through the ceiling in one thin tearing note, as a needle goes through a loose weave, and the lamp flame shivered with it. "Hester!" Gran's voice came down, level as a ruled edge, from the loft. "This kettle will have the roof off!"

"Take it off the heat," said Hester, to the ceiling. She drew her thumb out as one draws a thread, and sat up, and the inch became a yard.

"My hands are bad this morning."

Those hands had lifted that kettle off a hob in every weather for as long as Hester had owned a memory.

"Then leave it."

The scream stopped. It stopped all at once, as if a palm had been laid over it, and the quiet that came after hung in the workroom like a dropped stitch.

Ansel had gone back on his stool by the width of the world. He was looking at the ceiling and laughing, low and helplessly, with the back of his wrist against his mouth. "That kettle," he said, "has the best timing in Larkmere."

"It has had practice."

"I should be at the school." He did not move. Then he did, all at once, standing and holding the two swatches out to her, one in each hand, with care, as one hands over a thing that is lit. She took them. His fingers did not touch hers and she felt exactly where they did not. "The stove will have gone out. The children will find me a very poor example."

He said nothing more about the gap. By the door, beside the stove, stood Gran's coal scuttle, filled from the shed, its iron handle bent from years of the same grip. He looked at it. He took it up, and the coals slid, and he looked at her over the top of it, and she did not take it from him.

She had taken it from him once, without a word. She stood with her arms full of cream wool and watched the Green go out into the wet light with the scuttle swinging, warm to the waist and cool at the hem where she had knit it politely first, and the latch took the door behind him.

She could not have said which of the two she would burn. The correct one was safe. It could be hung on a rack and shown to anyone, and nobody would ask it a question. The other had a thread-wide hole in it and was warm to the wrist, and she had made it with a man's breath on her mouth in her mind and nothing else in her fingers. To knit him a whole jumper like that she would have to do it again, row after row, for as long as a jumper took. She did not know whether a pair of hands could keep it up that long and still be hers.

The loft stair spoke under Gran one tread at a time. She came down slowly, with the cane hooked over one wrist and the pattern book hugged to her ribs. She did not look at the stove or the swatches or her granddaughter. She crossed to the long table, moved the lamp with the back of her knuckles, and laid the book on the scrubbed boards in front of the stool where Ansel had sat.

"Sit," said Gran.

Hester sat. It was the first time in her life she had done it at the first asking, and Gran noted it and let it go by.

"I shut this on you at the Knit-In," said Gran. "I have been shutting it a long while."

She opened it at the ribbon. The lamp found the hole at the edge of her shawl as she leaned, dark against the grey. The page lay under it pressed flat and thin as a worn selvedge, shining along the margin where thumbs had held it down, and the writing on it was small and stiff and brown, in a hand that was not Gran's. It had been read so often that the ink had gone glossy. There was no name at the foot.

"Read it."

Hester read.

It was not long. Whoever holds the winter for Larkmere holds the lake's cold in her own hands. She cannot knit it out of herself. The toll is paid back only when the knitter is knit for. Not by the fire. Not by the town. One hand must make her a thing and mean it, and until then she is cold.

She read it twice, the way you count back a row you do not believe. The second time she read it with her thumb. It lay on the line about the knitter, and the paper was warm there, from Gran's palm or from the stove.

Nine winters. She had called it her hands. She had told herself it was how she was made, a fault set into the first row that every row after had to carry. She had made one warm jumper in her life and watched it go down the shore road on a man's back at the thaw, and she had not made another. Her scarf was at her throat now, wound to the jaw, and she put two fingers to it. It was cold. Every stitch she had ever worn had come off her own needles, and every one of them was correct. The town brought soup to the door. Mina put tea into her hands. Nobody had once sat down and made her anything, and it had not occurred to the town, and it had not occurred to her to want it.

At the Breaking the lake would let the schoolmaster go. It had made no such promise to her.

"Nobody knits for a Tansley," said Gran. Her voice was very quiet. "They bring us soup and call it kindness."

She did not say another word. She put one bent finger on the last line of the page, and held it there.
