The sky over the lake went the color of a bruised fleece an hour before the squall, and Hester, who knew weather the way she knew yarn, by the feel of it, had known what it was.

She was on Spindle Lane with the last of the undyed fleece on her shoulder, a bundle tied with cord, which she had gone down to the Net Sheds to fetch before the damp could reach it. The lane lay empty. The shutters were barred along both sides, the shop fronts closed early against the weather, and the stone wall that ran down the east side of the lane stood grey and bare against a sky gone yellow at the edges. The snow had been falling lazily since noon. Now it stopped, all at once, as though drawn in, and in the silence she heard the lake groan far off under its ice. Then she saw the white come up the lane toward her like a wall walking.

It hit her at the knees. She had time to turn her shoulder. The wind came off the water with the whole width of the lake behind it and took the lane and filled it, snow driven flat and fine, and the world went to a white roar with no up in it and no down. She could not see the gate of her own house. She could not see her hand. She put her left palm on the wall and held on to it and took one step, and the lane under her boot was glass.

The noon thaw had run down the cobbles and the frost had taken it again under the new snow, a hard clear skin laid over the whole lane, and she knew at once what it meant. A person who went down on that went down hard and did not rise easily. She thought, with great clarity, of her hands. They were the work. A wrist turned, a finger cracked on the stone, and there was no winter's knitting, and the town would stand on the shore at the thaw wanting its jumpers.

She went along the wall with her shoulder to the stone and the bundle clutched to her chest, a step and a slide and a step. The noise filled her ears. She heard nothing else until a voice came out of the white, thin and cheerful, torn by the wind into pieces.

"Hello! Is that a person? I believe I am addressing a person!"

She stopped. He came out of the snow a few paces off, bent double into the wind, in every coat he owned, with his collar up to his ears and one hand out toward the shuttered fronts on the far side of the lane, where there was no wall to hold, only the black slots of doorways. He was grinning. Snow crusted his hair and the shoulders of the brown coat. He looked at her through it like a man who has found a friend in a flood.

"I was on my way to see about the tiles," he shouted. "I thought I had better look, with the weather. It seemed a very good idea at the time."

"Stay where you are."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Stay where you are!" The wind took half of it. She shouted again. "It's glass!"

He heard that. She saw him hear it, and saw him nod, and saw him do the one thing a man will do when he has been told to stay still and sees a woman bent double with a bundle on the far side of a lane. He started across.

He got two steps. On the third his boot went from under him as clean as a pulled thread, and he came forward with both arms flung out and the laugh already on his face, skating straight at her down the glaze with his coats flying, and she did not think. She dropped the bundle. She let the whole fleece go into the wind and took two fistfuls of his coat front in both hands, the cloth where the lapels met, and set her heels and was dragged.

It was no use. He was too heavy and the ice was too slick. The two of them went down the lane together, locked, the wall coming up on her left, and she felt his hands come round her, both of them, hard, at the small of her back and then at the waist, to hold her up. He took her as one takes a hank off a peg, quick and entire, and threw his weight against the slide to turn it. The right hand slipped. She felt it slip, and felt it close again with a hitch in his breath, on the cloth over her hip. Then the wind got under both of them and heaved the lane's whole load of snow against the wall in one great push, and they went into it.

It was a drift, and it had not been there a moment before. It lay banked along the foot of the wall where the stonework made a pocket, and it took them to the shoulders and held them: her back against the stone, cold through her coat, and his whole body pressed to the front of her, bent over her with his shoulder to the wind. His hands were at her waist. Hers were in his coat. The squall poured over the top of them with a noise like a great bolt of cloth being torn, and nothing fell, because there was nowhere left to fall.

For a moment neither of them said anything.

"Hello," said Ansel.

His mouth was beside her ear. She felt the shape of the word there. His breath went past her throat, warm in the stinging cold, a small close movement of heat against the skin above her scarf.

"You have fallen on me," said Hester.

"I have. I am so sorry. It was not at all my intention." He laughed, a breath of a laugh, and it shook through the whole length of him and into her, and she understood that it was not the laugh. He was shaking, as he had shaken on her roof. "I thought I had better see about the tiles."

"In this."

"It was perfectly fine when I set out."

She said nothing. Over their heads the squall screamed up the lane.

"How long do they last?" he said, after a time, in the voice of a man making conversation at a bad dinner.

"Not long. Less than an hour, as a rule."

"Less than an hour." He seemed to weigh it. "I find I do not mind it as much as I ought."

She did not answer that either. A gust shouldered the drift and a sheet of snow went down the back of her collar, and she felt his hand tighten at her waist to keep her from flinching on the glass. The tightening was careful and very exact, the grip of a man who has been told a thing is brittle and means to carry it home whole.

"Don't move," he said, into her hair, quite gravely. "The ice goes the whole length of it. If either of us shifts a foot we shall go down, and I have not the wrist for it."

She did not move. She stood in the drift with her back to the wall and her fists in his coat, and she thought of the cold. She had felt it as soon as she took hold of him: through the long brown coat, and the grey-green, and the third, and under them the Grey, the whole winter's worth of it, level and exact, passing through the cloth into her knuckles as plainly as a fact. It was the cold she had made. It was being exactly what she had made it to be. She held it in her hands and it did not lessen, a plain flat cold that went up her wrists, and it was right, and she could not bear it. On the table at home the Blue lay under its cloth with its shoulders warm and its sleeves unknit, and she had pulled it off this same man with her own two hands and put him out into the snow. One cold in her fists, one warm on the table, and between them the man.

But her fingers were warm.

She had noticed it on the roof and had opened her hand and not thought of it again. She noticed it now and did not open her hand. They were warm where they gripped the cloth. They were warm to the second joint, to the knuckle, round the whole fist, and the cold of the Grey came up at them through everything and was met, and went no farther. Her hands, which had been white to the nail since the Heron, which she could not remember ever having been otherwise, were warm. She held him, and her fingers were warm.

She did not look at it. She looked at the white wall of the snow beside his shoulder.

She thought of the Grey, which was a perfect piece of work, and of every row of it laid down polite and flat under her careful thumb. She thought of the Blue, which she could not finish. Her hands had been two different things that winter. They had been a pair of tools she kept sharp and a pair of traitors she kept in her pockets. Now they were neither. They were holding a man up on the ice, which was all they had been asked to do, and they were doing it without a single instruction from her, and they were warm.

Between them, between her chin and the notch of his collar, the end of her grey scarf had been caught and crushed flat by the press of their bodies. It lay against his collarbone where the coats stood open. It was warm. It was not her breath that warmed it, nor her throat. It lay against him with the Grey cold on one side of it and his skin on the other, and it was warm, and she did not say why. She did not ask it why.

"Your scarf is warm," said Ansel. He said it slowly, and quite low, with his mouth against the top of her head. "It is lying on my neck. It is very warm."

"It is wool."

"So is the Grey." He laughed again, and there was no laugh in it at all.

She did not answer that. There was no answer she could give that would have held her weight.

Beyond the shutters the town sat close to its stoves with the bars down and did not know. She could hear it, in the gaps of the wind: a bucket knocked over a way down the lane, a door slammed and slammed again on a loose catch, the thin ring of the lantern glass on its post. Nobody would come out in this. Nobody would find them until it passed, and when it passed there would be a drift, and two people in it, and a bundle of wool lying in the lane with the snow filling its folds.

The wind dropped.

It dropped for the length of a breath, as it will in a squall, and the roar fell to a hiss and the white thinned, and she saw the lane for an instant, grey and scoured and shining, and the shutters, and her own gate a few doors off. The ice lay under their boots. She could have taken her hands out of his coat. He could have taken his from her waist. She felt the thought go through the pair of them like a thread drawn through cloth, quick and sure, and arrive at the other end.

Neither of them moved.

She counted what it would cost. The glaze lay under their boots the whole length of the lane, and the wall at her back was the only thing in Larkmere that was not sliding. A step from the drift, one step, and they would go down together on stone, he on the wrist that would not bear it and she on the hands that were the winter. It would be the end of the school's master and the end of the town's knitting, in the space of a breath, in an empty lane, for the want of one of them taking the first step. It was a very good reason to stand still. She held on to it. She knew perfectly well that it was not the reason.

His hands stayed where they were. She felt the right one at her hip, the bound wrist hard against her through the cloth, and she felt the fingers tighten, slightly, once, as if he were asking a question of the stuff of her coat. She felt his breath at her throat. She felt the shaking in him, steady, fine, running through every layer of his coats and up into her fists, and she held it, and held it, and it did not stop. It had not stopped on the roof either. She did not think it was going to stop for her. But her hands stayed round the cloth, and they stayed warm, and she found that she was not going to be the one to let go.

"It's easing," said Ansel, hoarse.

"It is not."

"No," he agreed. "I don't believe it is."

The squall came down again. It came out of the white as it had come before, a wall, and took the lane, and the roar closed over their heads. The snow drove against the wall and against his back and heaped itself in the pocket of the stones. It settled on the shoulders of the brown coat and on her hair and on the cuff of her sleeve where it lay across his chest. It began to fill the shape they made against the wall: the edge of his shoulder, the line of her arm, the hollow of the dark between them where their faces were. It filled it softly, from the edges inward, as a seam fills when wool is fulled, until the drift held the print of the two of them whole, and neither of them moved.
