The snap broke in the night, as a fever breaks, all at once and without a word, and by morning the whole lake lay under a sky so hard and blue that it seemed to ring.

Ansel had not slept to see it. He had come out of the cottage at first light with the lumpy blue scarf wound twice round his throat and every coat buttoned, and the air had gone through the lot like a bright blade through paper, a cold of a new and cleaner kind, with no bite in it, only an enormous clarity. The shore path squeaked underfoot. The lantern poles stood out along the Ice Road each with its small cap of snow, and the frost on the rail of the quay had grown into feathers a finger long, every one of them lit gold on the side the sun could reach. It was the sort of morning on which a master lets the school out and nobody argues. He was not letting anyone out. He was walking down the lane toward the harbor with his hands in his armpits because he had seen, from the cottage window, a sledge at the head of the Ice Road with its team stamping and its breath going up in lines.

Out on the white the north shore stood up small and sharp, every bare tree on it drawn in black, as clean as a thing done in ink. The driver was stacking the post. The blankets lay in a heap on the seat beside him, and he had not got down, and the bell hung on its post at the front of the runners with a small icicle at its lip.

And at the door of the Kettle Room, with the shutters still closed behind him and a canvas bag at his feet, Piers Ellory was doing up his coat.

He did it slowly, a button at a time, with the ink-dark fingers that had once drawn the whole shore of a lake on a single sheet. He had on the old grey-blue jumper, worn to a shine at the elbows. He looked rested. He looked like a man who has finished a piece of work and been fond of it, and he looked up at the sound of boots on the lane and his face did what it always did, which was to open.

"The schoolmaster," said Piers. "I had thought I should get away without a witness."

"You are leaving," said Ansel.

"On the post. I had meant the thaw." He smiled, ruefully, at the sledge. "But the map is done as far as the north shore, and I find I have run out of reasons to stand about being warm in a cold town. It is a very bad habit. Ask anyone."

"Is it today?"

"It is this quarter of an hour, I am told. The ice is good now and will not be good long."

Ansel looked at the bag.

It stood on the frozen stones, patched at both corners, a thing of canvas with a leather strap and a brass buckle gone green. It was bigger than a man might want to carry down a lane at that hour, and Piers had made no move toward it. He had done up the last button and put his bare hands into his pockets and was looking across the ice, and Ansel understood that he had been standing there a good while, working out how it was to be got to the sledge.

He did not decide. There was no deciding in it. His hands were bare and already stiff, and he knew perfectly well what the strap would do to them. He stepped forward and took the strap in both hands and swung the bag up onto his shoulder, and the weight of it went through the three coats and settled into his collarbone like a hand pressing down.

"Oh, now, you mustn't," said Piers.

"I am perfectly strong."

"I did not say you were not. I said you mustn't."

"It is a thing I cannot not do," said Ansel. "I have tried. It's a failing. You must let me."

Piers looked at him. It was the long look of the quay, the one that had gone across his open face and left it the same, and he did not argue. He only nodded, as a man nods who has met a weather before, and fell into step.

They went down the stones together, past the capped heads of the mooring posts, each with its hat of snow. The bag was heavier than it ought to have been. It was a good deal heavier than clothes. It had a weight in it that shifted when he walked, flat and dense, and it swung against his hip with each step and knocked, once, on a corner of brass, and he found himself matching his pace to it without thinking, as one does with a child carried on the shoulders. His hands had gone white inside the coat cuffs. The strap bit the bare skin of his wrist. He did not shift it.

"Paper," said Piers, watching him. "Nearly all of it. A lake, copied small, and nine winters of other people's rivers. Do not let the buckle go, it is the only thing keeping it from being a very expensive pillow."

"What do you do with it?"

"I draw it again. And again. The first time you draw a thing it is yours, and the second time it is the thing." He laughed. "I am quite capable of talking nonsense about a map for a very long while. I should stop."

"Do not stop," said Ansel. "I like it. It is a good deal better than my sums."

They came out onto the end of the quay, where the stones gave way to the packed brown ribbon of the road, and the sledge stood a dozen paces off with its runners ticking in the cold. The driver glanced round at them from inside his heap of blankets and said, "Ten minutes," in the voice of a man who had said it to a great many people, and went back to his post.

Ansel set the bag down at the foot of the sledge. He straightened. His shoulder ached where the strap had been, and his hands, now that they were empty, had begun to shake a little in their sleeves, and he thrust them back into his armpits and said brightly, "I have a coat to spare. The brown, if you wish it, it is the best of the three, and I should never miss it. The road is cold, you know. It is a long road."

"I run hot," said Piers.

"No one runs hot in a lake. It is not a thing that is done."

"Look," said Piers.

He drew one hand from his pocket and laid it flat on the rail of the sledge, where the frost stood up in white feathers a finger long. They covered the rail from end to end. They had grown on the wood and the iron and the leather of the seat, thick and fine, glittering. And under the cuff of his old grey-blue sleeve, where his wrist rested on the rail, there was a bare dry place on the wood the size of a hand, and no frost came near it. It lay there dark and clean in the white like a spot where the sun had been.

Ansel put his own hand down beside it, without quite deciding to, and the cuff of the grey-blue sleeve lay against the back of his wrist. It was warm. Not warm as a man is warm, but warm as a loaf is on the board, from the inside and without remark, a heat that had no business in old wool on a morning like this. His skin had been numb since the lane. Under that sleeve it prickled awake along the whole wrist, as if it had remembered what it was for. He could not have said whose hands had made it. Only that they had meant every stitch.

"Nine winters," said Piers. "In a snowstorm, once, on a hillside, I sat out four hours with my coat off to get the lie of a valley. I did not once shiver. I was very much ashamed of it afterwards. A man would pay a fortune for it, and I have never been able to tell a single soul how I came by it. It was given me, you see, in a winter I was too stupid to take it as given." He took the hand away, and the dry place stayed where it was for a moment and then, very slowly, began to fill in at the edge. "I told her I should say the reason before I went. She did not want it. She has never in her life wanted what was owed her, which is the whole of what I mean to tell you."

Ansel stood quite still.

"I have it still, you see," said Piers, "the thing I was going to say. And I find I cannot say it to her. She would forgive it, and I could not bear that either." He looked up. His eyes were very kind and perfectly tired, and for the first time since the quay Ansel saw the whole of what the easy smile had been put on to hold. "You are carrying my bag, so you may carry this too. I could not stand to be that warm and owe nothing."

The driver's boots shifted on the footboard. A runner creaked.

"Don't be me," said Piers. "Let her be owed."

He said it quite gently. He said it as a man hands over a key, without asking whether the other knows what it opens, and then he turned and put a foot on the runner and swung himself up into the blankets, and the sledge dipped under him, and the team began to stamp.

"I do not," said Ansel. He took a step. "I am not sure I understand."

"No," said Piers. He had sat down among the blankets and drawn the canvas bag onto his knees, and he looked back at Ansel across the rail with the long cold sun in his face and the grey-blue jumper bright as a wing. "I did not either. It took me nine winters, and I am a slow man, and a very great fool, and I should not have had you carry that bag. You are the only person in this town who would have been quite so good about it."

"I should like very much to be of use," said Ansel.

"I know," said Piers. "That is the trouble. I should have liked your cot, you know. I think I should have slept. Good-bye, Ansel Brandt."

He lifted his hand. It was a plain open hand, ink to the second joint, and Ansel saw that it was not waving. It was only held up, palm out, as a man holds a hand up to a window to show that he means no harm.

The driver clicked his tongue.

The bell had a flat brass voice, Ansel noticed, with a crack in it, and one note of it a little sharp. He found he was listening for that note before it came, the way you brace for a stair you know is loose. His hands, shaken free of the bag, closed and opened in his armpits and would not close all the way.

The sledge jerked and began to slide. The runners hissed on the packed snow, and the bell on its post began to ring, small and flat and brass, and the whole long procession of lantern poles went by one after another in the bright cold, a grey-blue shape in the stern of the sledge growing smaller between them. Ansel stood at the end of the road with his hands in his armpits. He had stood on these same stones with a letter against his ribs and watched this same sledge put a stranger down on them, and he had liked the stranger at once.

It was simpler. He told himself so. Nothing was in the way now, and he found that he would have given a good deal for one thing to be. There was the whole bright morning in front of him, and the school to open, and a rival gone down the ice with a bag of paper, and a woman in a house at the end of a lane who would be there in the afternoon, and there was nothing left in the way. He did not need to be owed. He had never needed it. He had given and given and given, all his life, and asked for nothing, and it had been the only comfort he had. He said it to himself, in the voice he used on the benches, quite firmly, and the words went out across the white and did not fit. They were the wrong size. They lay on top of what he meant like a lid that has been made for another jar.

He turned the last of it over once, and it was a stone with no face, and he could not tell which way up it went.

The bell went on across the lake. He stood and listened to it grow thin and high and small. It went down the road from pole to pole, ringing, until it was no more than a single bright speck of sound at the very end of the ice, and then a thread, and then a note he could not tell from the sky.

It rang once more, very far away, and was gone.
