She cast off the cuff at the back table of the Kettle Room, with the whole of the afternoon's light lying across it in a long yellow bar, and the last stitch went off the needle like a held breath let go.

It was done. She did not feel any triumph in it. She felt what she felt at the end of any piece, a flatness, the small dry ache in the forearm of a thing finished that could not be unfinished. She drew the tail through the final loop and snugged it down and took the bodkin and wove the end into the seam, in and back, in and back, until the wool closed over it and kept the secret. Then she bit the strand off at the root, which her grandmother had told her a thousand times not to do, and laid the right sleeve down on the board beside the left.

They were a pair. She would have sworn to it on the ball band. The seed stitch lay in the same close knots on each, the same brown-green, a moss color that looked nearly black in shadow and showed its gold when the light caught it. She smoothed them flat and ran the linen tape down one and up the other. The length matched to the width of a thread.

Then she lifted the whole jumper out of its cloth and laid it on the table and looked at it.

It was a plain thing. People who had not worked wool believed that a good jumper announced itself, and it did not. It lay there and was a jumper: a body, two sleeves, a neck she had picked up and worked three times to get right. It had no cable, no lace, no color change, nothing to make a person turn their head. It was seed stitch, which was the stitch of a woman who has no wish to be admired. What it had was a weight. She picked it up by the shoulders and felt the weight of it hang, and her fingers, which were never warm, took a long slow warmth from the shoulders as a hand takes it from a cup, and she put it down at once.

"You'll want tea."

Mina stood at her elbow in the flour-white apron with the chipped blue teapot in her fist. Hester had not heard her come. She never did. Mina Cray moved through her own room like a woman who has walked it blind a thousand times and holds no opinion about the furniture, and she put the pot on the table with a clunk and a cup beside it and poured. The tea came out of the broken spout in two thin threads and joined in the cup. It was strong enough to stand a spoon in.

"I did not order tea."

"No. You ordered nothing. You sat down and knitted at my back table for the whole of a morning and let the oven go cold on a customer's pies because I was looking at your hands." Mina wiped her own on the apron. "Drink it."

Hester drank. It was scalding, and a good deal more welcome than she would have said.

"That's the one," said Mina.

"It is a jumper."

"It is his."

"It is a jumper in his measure. Anyone of that frame could wear it." She heard how that sounded and went on regardless, in the voice she used for gauge. "The shoulder is a hand's breadth wider than a man of his height should need, because he carries them up round his ears. I allowed for it. It is a matter of fit."

"Hester."

"The hem is cool." She had not meant to say that. It came out in the same flat tone, the tone of a woman reading from a ledger, and it went across the table and lay down between the cups. "It is cool the whole way round. I knit it first, before I knew what I was making, and I knew nothing then, and it holds nothing. It will never be anything else. The rest is warm. I have put my thumb to it twice and it is warm from the shoulder to the waist and in the left cuff, and it will stay so. Wool is not revised. Whatever went into it stays in it. If I give it to him, that is the garment he gets. I cannot take back a row."

"I know what wool does," said Mina. "You've told me enough times."

"Then you know what I am saying."

"I know what you are saying, I just don't see why it's an argument." Mina set the teapot down and put both fists on the board and leaned on them, and her broad flat blunt face came down into the yellow light with no mercy in it whatever. "I've known you a long time. I've watched you knit for every soul in this town who ever came through the door, and I've never once heard one of them say they went away cold. You're good, and you know it, and so does everyone. Do you know what's wrong with the good ones?"

"I have the impression you are going to tell me."

"You have been polite for nine winters."

It was not loud. Mina did not raise her voice for anything. It went into the room as a needle goes into a seam, and for a moment Hester did not feel it at all. Then the place where it had gone in began to ache, an inch below the breastbone, the way a mended place aches in a change of weather.

"That's unjust."

"Is it?"

"I have been exact. I have made every garment that was asked of me, to the thread, and I have never once sent a customer away with a thing that was less than sound. A flawless piece is the best I can give. It is the most that can be asked."

"Exact," said Mina, "is polite with a ruler."

Hester looked at her. She wished to say a number of sharp things and found, with a clear and unpleasant surprise, that all of them were the sort of thing one says to hold a door shut. She put the cup down. Her hand had begun to shake, a small fine tremor in the wrist like a stitch gone slack on a needle, and she set it flat on the Green to stop it and felt the warmth come up under the palm. It was a very fine warmth. She had not known that she could make a thing like that.

"I did not mean to," she said.

"I know you didn't."

"It happened at the roof. And then it kept happening."

"I know that too," said Mina, and for the first time that afternoon the broad face unclenched a little. "The whole lane knows it. I wasn't on the hill, I was up to my elbows in meat pies. I didn't need to be." She picked up the teapot and tucked it against her apron. "Give it to him. Go on. Fold it in the cloth and walk it up the shore road while there's light. It's late, and the thaw's coming, and I'd as soon you did it with your own hands as left it for the child to carry."

"And if he goes?"

"Then he goes in a warm jumper. Which is more than most of us can say."

Hester folded the Green in its cloth. She did it exactly, sleeves crossed over the body, the way she would have laid out a pattern for a stranger, and when the cloth was tied she carried it against her chest with both arms round it. She was at the door when Mina said, without turning round from the stove, "The hem's the only honest bit, you know," and she did not answer that. She had no answer that was not a reply to the whole of a winter.

She walked.

The shore road was white and then grey and then a dull pink where the sun lay along the ice. The thaw had not come, but it had begun to think of it. At the roof edges the icicles had thinned to a dripping fringe, and the snow along the verge had a crust on it that broke under her boot with a small wet crack. The cloth bundle lay against her chest. The warmth came through it in a slow even push, the way heat comes through the paper band of a warm skein, and she carried it the length of the road with her scarf pulled up to her nose and the hem of her coat soaking up the slush, and every step she told herself that she was only delivering a thing in a customer's measure.

The school stood where it always stood, low and square against the lake, with the window facing the road. A lamp was lit in it. The door was shut. She went up the three worn steps and stood on the stoop in the cold with the bundle in her arms and the sky going out behind her, and she raised her hand, and the hand did not do what it was told.

The door opened before she had knocked.

He had been on his way out. She saw that at once, in the lamplight: the coat half on, the chalk to the wrist, one hand holding the door and the other holding the satchel he never went anywhere without, and the whole bright ragged scarecrow of him, in his three coats and the lumpy blue scarf, stopped dead in the frame with his mouth open on the beginning of a joke.

"Hester."

"Master Brandt."

"It is finished," she said.

She held the bundle out. It was the whole of her speech. She had prepared a better one on the road and it had gone out of her head at the first stair.

He looked at the cloth. He did not take it. For a long moment he stood and looked at it as if it were a live thing, and the lamplight showed her his face quite plainly, the high color the cold had put there, the grey under the eyes that had not left, and his mouth working at the laugh he could not find. Then he put the satchel down on the boards. He put it down carefully, as one sets down a thing one is afraid of dropping, and he reached out with both hands and took the Green from her.

"May I?"

"It is yours. It was cut to you."

He undid the cloth. It fell open in his hands, and the lamp from the doorway found the moss color and made it gold, and he made a small sound in his throat, a breath with no word in it. He did not say anything clever. He did not laugh. He shed his coats on the school step one after another, the great shabby sheepskin first, then the brown one, then the blue, and dropped them on the boards in a heap without looking where they fell, and stood in the doorway in his shirt and the plain grey jumper she had made him in the autumn, with the lake wind going through the whole thin length of him. Then he drew the Green on over his head.

It went down over him and settled on the first try.

She watched it. She had decided in the road that she would not, and she watched every inch. She saw his arms go into the sleeves and the cuffs close at his wrists. She saw the shoulders settle, and a long shudder go through him from the nape of his neck to the small of his back, the shudder of wool letting go its tension when the steam first reaches it. Then she saw his face change. The color came up into it. His mouth came open. The grey went out of it from the top as a stain goes out of a cloth, quite slowly, and under it was a boy who had just been told that he might stay.

He put his hand on his chest and spread it flat.

"Oh," said Ansel.

She did not breathe. His palm went slowly down. It went over the ribs and the belly, as a hand runs down a length of ribbing to find the fault, and it found where the warmth ended. She saw him find it. It was a little above the hem, a line round the body as plain as a selvedge, where the polite rows began, and under his hand the wool was only wool. He pressed on it. He moved his hand up an inch and it was warm. He moved it down and it was cool. He did it again, slowly, with his head bent and his lip caught between his teeth, like a man reading a line in the dark by touch, and she understood that he was looking for the exact row where she had begun to mean it.

"Here," he said. His voice cracked. "This is where it starts."

"Yes," said Hester. She heard her own voice, and it was dry and clear and perfectly steady, and she could not think where it had come from. She knew which night it had been. He could not know that, and he had found the row anyway.

He looked up.

He took her hand. She had not seen it offered. His own two hands closed round it, warm, the left over the right and the right under it, a full clasp, as two strands are held together to be plied, and the warmth went into her fingers at once. It went in slowly and entirely, from the knuckles to the tips, and the fingers she had thought were a permanent feature of her body, thin and white and cold to the second joint, came up in his palms like wool going into warm water. She had not been warm to the fingertips in front of another living person since she was a girl.

"Thank you," said Ansel.

It was only two words and it was a small ordinary thing to say. He said it as if each word had a weight he meant her to feel in her palm. She stood on his step in the last of the light with her hand held in both of his and the lake wind at her back, and the Green lay over his chest, glowing in the lamp, warm to the waist and cool at the hem, and the thaw dripped from the eaves above them in small slow drops. She ought to take her hand back. She knew exactly the moment for it, as one knows the row for the decrease, and she let it go by.

His thumbs moved once across her knuckles. His breath came out white between them and joined hers, and the two clouds hung together in the cold doorway, and did not part. She did not draw her hand away. He did not let it go.

It went on a breath too long.
