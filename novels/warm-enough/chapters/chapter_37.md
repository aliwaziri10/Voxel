By the second sum the children had stopped looking at the board and begun looking at his mouth.

He knew what they saw, because he could feel it. The color had gone out of his lips first, as it had in the autumn, and then out of the ends of his fingers, which were white again to the second joint and would not hold the chalk steady. The line he drew under the sum wandered off its ruling like a ferry with no one at the tiller. He had worn the charcoal jumper to school as he had promised he would, at once, at the first opportunity, and it hung on him now cold as a slate fetched in from the yard. The lake had come back into him through every stitch of it, as though a door had been propped open and nobody told.

The Green was folded over the chair at the cottage with its left cuff hanging. He had stood and looked at it that morning for a good while. It was warm, and he knew it would be, and he had put on the other one anyway, because a man who has said he will wear a thing should wear it. It was the only promise he had made all winter that he could keep with his own two hands.

"You're blue, sir," said a boy on the back bench.

"It is the light," said Ansel. He laughed before he said it, and it came out in two pieces. "It is a very blue light, this time of year. Eyes front. The ferry has put off half her passengers at the first landing."

They bent to their slates. He heard the small wary scratch of a room that has decided to be kind, which is worse than being laughed at, and he turned to the window and kept his back to them. Out on the lake the poles of the Ice Road stood in their long line, and the ice between them had gone dull and wet. The ferry would run again.

When the last of the children had gone he stood a while in the emptied room with the chalk in his fist and shook. It was a plain, ordinary shaking now, and it did not trouble to hide itself. The stove ticked down to its coals, and on the row of pegs by the door the small mittens hung in their pale line. He did not go to them. If he went to them now he would not be able to take his hand out.

"Mr. Brandt."

Tilda Brook had not gone. She sat in the second row with her slate on her knee and the lumpy blue scarf wound twice round her neck and trailing to the boards, and her tongue caught between her teeth. She had the look of a girl who has been working up to a sum for most of the afternoon.

"Tilda. Home. Your supper will be a wall of reproach."

"You're blue."

"I have heard."

"Where's the Green?"

"On a chair. It has been extremely well behaved."

"You're wearing the other one. The cold one." She said it flatly, as a clerk reads off a column. "Everybody on the shore has put a finger to it. They all say it's perfect, and that's what's wrong with it."

"It is a magnificent piece of work."

"That's not what I asked," said Tilda, and for the length of a breath he felt very tired and rather fond of her. She stood up. The scarf swung, and under the lamp he saw the lumps in it, the places where the stitches had gone wrong and been carried on regardless, and he saw it was the warmest thing in the room. It had been warm the first hour she put it on. It sat on her as though it had grown there.

"She never made me one," said Tilda. "Hester. I'm her apprentice and I made this myself and it's rubbish."

"It is a very good scarf."

"It's rubbish and it's warm." She frowned at the floor. "Hester knits for everyone. The whole shore, every winter, and everybody that ever came off the Heron. So I asked Gran who's knit for Hester. That scarf of hers she made herself, I watched her cast off. And Gran said nobody has. Not once. She said nobody's ever got round to it."

The stove ticked. Outside the window a long drop of water fell from the eave and was followed by another.

"Not once," said Ansel.

"Not in her whole life. I thought you should know." Tilda hitched the scarf up and gave him one of her clerk's nods. "Because you keep giving everything away, and I thought, he has to be given it by a person. And I thought, well, so does she."

She went out. The latch he had mended took the door behind her without a sound.

He stood in the middle of the room with the chalk gone to powder in his hand.

He had known it. He saw that he had known it for a long time, as a master knows which child at the back has never once been asked a question. A woman who put warmth into a hem for a stranger and never took her own scarf off. The Green had come from her, and she had said nothing about it, and he had thanked her and carried its warmth round the town in his face. It had not crossed his mind that he was the first person she had ever done it for.

And she was afraid, and had told him so, in the dark, with her palms flat on his chest. Everything she made warm, she lost. He had stood in front of that and said he was not a map, and had not said what he was instead.

The Heron would run. After the Breaking there would be nothing in Larkmere to keep a man who wished to leave, and he did not wish to leave, and he had not said so. He had decided on the quay, in every coat he owned, that he would be no trouble to anyone this winter. He had kept to it. He had been no trouble at all, and it had very nearly cost them both the thaw.

He took the charcoal jumper by the hem and drew it over his head, and the air of the schoolroom met his shirt. He stood and shook with his arms round himself and did not make a joke. Then he put on the long brown coat, and the grey-green one over it, and went to the Spindle House in the dark.

At the shop door he stopped with his hand lifted to knock, and the door opened before he could.

"You have taken your time," said Gran.

She stood on the sill with her cane in one hand and the copper kettle in the other, as though she had come to the door in the middle of a task and meant to go back to it. The shawl hung about her with its one small hole. She looked at him, and at the coats, and at his mouth.

"I was told you had been waiting."

"Since noon. The Brook child has a mouth on her." Gran stepped back. "Hester has gone down to the Net Sheds to sit with the hull. She goes at lamplighting and comes back when she is frozen, which is late. Take your boots off. The loft stair is behind the workroom and it creaks."

He took them off. The workroom was empty and the stove stood open, and the long table was bare. He crossed the boards in his stockinged feet, and at the foot of the loft stair he stopped.

"Mrs. Tansley." He had never called her that. The whole town called her Gran, and it came out stiff and strange. "I have never asked anyone for anything. I have been in Larkmere the whole of a winter and asked for nothing at all, and I find I am very bad at it. I may do it clumsily."

"Then do it clumsily."

"I want to learn to knit." His voice cracked on the last word like a bad piece of chalk. He did not laugh. "I do not know how to do anything with my hands but write on a slate. I would like you to teach me. Tonight. Will you help me?"

Gran looked at him for a long moment, in the red of the stove, with eyes that had seen a great many winters and not been fooled by any of them.

"That will do," she said. "Up."

The loft ran under the slope of the roof, low and long and warm from the stove beneath, with a narrow bed at one end and a lamp on a stool at the other. Gran lowered herself onto the stool beside the lamp. She set the kettle on the boards between them, where it steamed a little, and from a basket by her foot she drew two needles and a hank of wool the color of old brick.

"There is brown," said Gran, "and there is this, and there is a blue the Brook child has used up. Pick."

"The red."

"It is not red. It is rust. It will be the worst color in the county." She put the hank in his hands. "Sit, and hold out your arms."

He sat on the floor with his back against the bed. She wound the wool off the hank onto his wrists, as a child holds out his arms to be buttoned into a coat, and then it was a ball in her lap, and she put the needle into his right hand. He held it in his fist as he held chalk.

"No," said Gran.

"It is how I hold things."

"It is how you hold a thing you are going to throw." She leaned from the stool and took his hand in both her own. They were old hands, bent and dry, the knuckles swollen to the size of walnuts. They closed over the back of his and unclosed his fist a finger at a time, and set the needle into the crook of his thumb as lightly as a bird into a nest. "Like that. It is not a sword. It does not have to be won."

She cast on for him the first time, her fingers moving over his, slow and plain, and he watched with his whole attention, as he had watched a hundred children learn their letters, and understood for the first time what he had asked of them. It was nothing to look at. It was a great deal to do. The second time she made him cast on himself, and he got four loops onto the needle, and the fifth fell off the end and lay on the boards like a dropped coin.

"Again."

He did it again, and the loops came out fat and lean by turns, a row of letters from a child who cannot yet keep to the line. Gran said nothing about them. She let him find that it could not be done by wanting.

He learned the first stitch as a sum. In, round, through, off. He said it aloud and Gran let him. His hands did it in the wrong order and then in the right one, and then a stitch came off the needle seated and neat, and he looked up like a boy who has got the answer on the board. Gran gave a small, dry nod and said, "Now do the rest."

He did a great many more, and every one of them was a little different from the one before.

Below them the stove ticked and the water ran from the eaves. Down by the Net Sheds a woman was sitting in the dark with her hands in her sleeves, looking at a ferry. He thought of her hands, the cold of them and the scrubbed knuckles, the quick exact turn of them over a needle. He wanted them. It was a plain want with no shame in it, and it sat in his chest and made his own hands worse. A stitch came loose. He caught it. He did not know how he had done that, and Gran did not say.

The row ran out. He turned it, and Gran's hands came over his again to show the turning, and he felt every bent finger of hers lie along every one of his. He did not laugh. He had been given a thing to do with his hands that he could not do well, and a room in which no one asked him to be good at it.

"You are making it too tight."

"I am afraid it will come undone."

"Wool does not come undone because you are gentle with it." The lamp moved on the wet gleam of her eyes. "Loosen."

He loosened. The row came off the needle a hair wider than the one beneath, and the next wider still, and the strip began to lean. He saw it leaning. The left edge went in and the right went out, so that the whole piece bellied like a sail, and he did not stop, because stopping meant starting again and he did not have the hours.

It was a long way past midnight when he first noticed the heat in his hands.

It was not much. It was the warmth of a slate that has lain in a child's lap, or of a mitten left on a peg, small and steady, coming from the length of rust in his fists. It had not been there at the beginning. It grew a row at a time. He sat and knit, in, round, through, off, and it went up through his wrists and into the cold place behind his ribs where the lake had lived since the Heron, and did not drive it out, but made room beside it. He did not say anything. He did not look up. He went on, in case it should stop.

Gran opened one eye and shut it again.

By the time the window at the end of the loft went from black to the color of old ink, the strip was as long as a man's arm and wider at one end than the other. The edge was a coastline. In the middle there was a hole the size of a thumbnail, where a stitch had gone and could not be coaxed back, and near the bottom the whole piece swung sharply to the left, as if it had remembered a grievance. It was the ugliest object he had ever made, and he had once drawn a ferry on the board that three children took for a pig.

"Off," said Gran. She had woken, or had never slept. "Cast it off. Two together, pass the first over."

He did it with her bent hands lying over his, and the last stitch came away from the needle, and the strip came loose in his lap.

He held it up to the lamp.

It was lopsided. It was rust red, the worst color in the county, and it was full of mistakes, and it was warm. It lay across his palms as the mitten had, a plain quiet warmth he had not been given and had not passed along, because he had made it with his own hands in one night and had not yet decided to give it to anyone. His throat closed. He held it and did not put it down.

"Well," said Gran. She had her face turned to the dark window. "That is a terrible scarf."

"Yes," said Ansel.

"It is the best one I have seen in all my winters."

He looked at her. She did not look back. The dawn came grey and wet at the end of the loft, and the water went on running from the eaves, and in his hands the scarf lay across his fingers, crooked, and warm.
