The stove drew so cleanly that the flap sang in the draught, one thin high note like a wet finger run round the rim of a glass, and the latch on the door swung home with the soft click of a pupil who has finally got the answer right.

Ansel stood with his back to the stove and was cold.

He had been at the school since before the light. The latch had stuck so long that the paint had grown over the fault and made a feature of it, and the stove flap had been jammed half shut with soot as old as the roof. He had lain on the floorboards in the dark with a lamp on the sill and his cuffs gone black, working at the one and then the other until his fingers quit answering him. At the time he had blamed the hour. Now it was morning and the room was warm, truly warm, the long window running with damp along its lower edge, and he stood in the middle of it in every coat he owned with his shoulders up round his ears and the lake still sitting in his bones like a ferry that has forgotten how to dock.

Through the window the Ice Road ran out across the frozen water, a line of lantern poles going away into the grey, small and regular as tally marks down the edge of a slate. Nothing moved on it. He looked at it for a while because it was easier than looking at his hands, which had gone white at the tips, and then he took up the chalk and set out the first sum on the board, since a sum was a thing he could still do.

The children came up the shore path in ones and twos with their breath going ahead of them, and every one of them stopped in the doorway to look at him. The first through was a boy who put a hand to the latch, found it gave, closed it, opened it again to be sure, and looked up with real alarm.

"It doesn't stick."

"It never has," said Ansel. He laughed first, which bought him the length of a laugh to find the lie. "Marvelous door. Always been like that. Hang up your things."

The boy hung up his things and watched the latch for the rest of the morning, the way you watch a dog that has been unaccountably good.

When the benches were full Ansel came out from the stove and stood before them, and the room turned its faces up to him, rows of them, scrubbed and wary and frankly curious, exactly as he had rehearsed it on the ferry. "Good morning," he said. "I am Mr. Brandt. The coats are not a comment on your stove."

They gave him a thin laugh, the laugh of a room not yet sure it was allowed. He had a second joke ready and he used it, and it did a little better. He reached for the third and it was gone, the way an answer goes the moment you call on the one child you were certain of, and he stood in front of all those faces with his mouth open on nothing.

"You're still an onion," said the smallest girl, from the front bench. She was the one from the quay, the one who had eaten his pies, and she said it kindly, as a fact she had been saving.

"Layers," said Ansel, and the room had him then.

"The Onion," said a boy at the back, trying it out. A girl by the window said it again, and then the whole bench had it, the Onion, the Onion, passed along the rows like a note under the desks.

He did not argue. He took up the chalk and wrote THE ONION across the top of the board in letters a hand high, and underlined it twice, and turned round to find them laughing properly at last, a real noise, the kind that rattles a bench. It was the first thing anyone had handed him since the Heron that he had no wish to pass along. He kept it. He stood in front of it with chalk to his wrists and let it be his.

A girl in the second row put up her hand without waiting to be called on, which he approved of, and put it down again and spoke anyway, which he approved of more. She was eleven or so, with a scrubbed hard face and her tongue caught between her teeth as she copied the sum off the board, and she took the tongue back in to speak.

"Tilda Brook. I'm the one Hester doesn't like."

"I'm sure that can't be."

"She says I knit like I'm angry at the wool." Tilda considered this with the evenness of the very honest. "I'm not angry at it. It just won't do what I say."

"I know the feeling," said Ansel. "I've been losing arguments with chalk for years."

"I'm her apprentice," said Tilda. "She hasn't agreed to it yet." She put the tongue back between her teeth and returned to her slate, and that, apparently, was the whole of her introduction.

He taught the first sum as a ferry. A ferry leaves the quay full, and puts off half her load at the first landing and half of what is left at the second, and how many are still aboard when she makes the third? It was an old sum and he told it well, and the children leaned into it the way children lean into anything with water in it. He walked the length of the front bench as he talked. A boy got it wrong in a way that was nearly right, and Ansel took the wrong answer and found the true thing inside it and handed it back to him whole, and the boy sat up straighter than he had all morning.

A hand went up at the back. "Does the Heron come again?" The boy asked it the way a boy asks whether the dog is better. "Not until the thaw," said Ansel, and heard how flat it came out. The deckhand was in it, the shop-is-shut kindness, and he watched the children hear it too: one door behind them, the other not yet open. Tilda did not lift her head from her slate. "It freezes over," she said. "That's why." He put a smile over the silence and told them they were stuck with him, and he with them, and that the third landing was still waiting. The girl by the window looked out at the lake, then back at the board, and copied the sum down with her whole arm, as if pressing it into the slate to keep.

The chalk broke in his fingers on the fourth line. He had not felt it going. He bent for the two halves, and the board swam slightly and came back, and he stood with them in his white hand and laughed at the ceiling. "The chalk and I have agreed to part."

The class laughed. Tilda did not. She was looking at his hand.

"Are you cold?" she said, in the voice of a girl who asked questions for the answers.

"I'm fine," said Ansel. "It's the dust. Very destabilizing, chalk dust. Ask any master."

"You're shaking."

"Rhythm," he said gravely. "It helps with the sums." And he turned back to the board and wrote the fourth line again with a hand that had no business being so slow, because the whole room was still watching to see whether the new master was any good, and a man who shook at his first sum would not be given the benefit of the doubt by the town by the end of the week. There was a winter of work in front of him and one stove, and the post, once lost, did not come back.

The door opened without a sound.

He had spent a good part of the dark persuading those hinges, and he felt the small private pride of it before he saw who stood on the sill. Hester had the grey scarf wound to her jaw and a linen tape in her fist, drawn out from under it, the loose end trailing to the floor. She did not knock. She did not look at the children, who had gone silent all at once, every head turning together. She looked at him.

"Stool," she said.

"Good morning to you as well," said Ansel.

"Stool."

Tilda drew a breath through her nose and sat on her hands.

He fetched the stool from beside the stove. It took a moment, because a roomful of children was watching him do it and he had to carry it as though it were nothing, and he set it before the board, under THE ONION. Hester's eyes went to the board. Her mouth did not move. He climbed up.

"Arms out."

He put them out. They went out stiff and wide with all that cloth on them, a man being weighed for the ferry, and from the back bench came a small clear voice that said, "He's got too many for the tape," and was hushed.

She came in close and the tape went round him. It crossed his shoulders and ran the length of his back, over the long brown coat and the grey-green one beneath it, and she read it against the window light with her lips shut. She did not touch him. The tape touched him, and her fingers held the tape, and between her knuckles and the outermost cloth she kept the narrowest strip of air, as carefully as a child keeps the margin on a page.

Her hands were working hands. The knuckles were cracked and chapped red at the joints, and the ends of the fingers were the white of old chalk, bloodless, and when she drew the tape round his chest he felt the cold coming off them through every layer, the way you feel a window with your back to it. He did not like it. A person's hands should not be that cold in a room this warm, with the glass running. It was the concern any master had for anyone who came into his schoolroom underdressed, and he noted it as such, professionally, and set it aside.

"Breathe in," she said.

"I'm told I'm very in already."

"Out."

He breathed out. The tape went down his left arm to the wrist and she drew it round the cuff of the outermost coat, and her thumb came to rest on the cloth over the bone of his wrist. It was nothing. It was through every coat he owned. He felt it as plainly as a stroke of chalk down a clean slate, and he looked at the ceiling to be elsewhere, and found that the ceiling had nothing to say to him.

She looked up to read the length of his arm. Her eyes met his and stayed.

It was a second longer than the business needed. He knew exactly how long, because he had spent half his life counting silences in classrooms, and this one had a weight and a length he would have known anywhere. He waited for her mouth to thin as it had on the quay. It did not. It held, level and still, like a line of chalk drawn once and left.

"Done," she said, and the tape ran back into her fist.

She did not look at the children and she did not look at the board. She went out, and the latch he had mended took the door behind her with a clean click, and the room let out its breath in a long draught that stirred the papers on the front bench.

"Well," said Ansel, to the whole of it, still on his stool with his arms out because nobody had told him he could put them down, "that is the most thorough examination I have had since the ferry."

The children laughed, but it was a smaller laugh than before, and it did not last. He stepped down. He would have said he needed to see that the door had caught, and in a sense it was true, because the habit of the morning was already on him, and he went to it and put his hand to the latch.

On the row of pegs beside it hung a line of small mittens.

They were the only things in the room no child had claimed. They hung by their cuffs in a pale row, grey and brown and one the brown of wet rope, the thumbs worn thin with old use, and the children's coats had been hung around them and not over them, as if by agreement. He had seen them when he came in at dark and taken them for the leavings of last winter. He put out one finger without deciding to and touched the nearest.

It was warm.

Not much. The faint warmth a bench keeps after a child has got up from it, a warmth that had no business staying in a thing nobody wore, and he stood with his finger on it while the cold in his own bones went on regardless of the stove, the window, the day.

"Hester made those," said a voice behind him, a boy's, low. "Before."

He did not ask before what. He took the nearest mitten off its peg and slid his hand into it. It was far too small. His fingers went in as far as the second joint and stopped, and the warmth closed round them anyway, quiet and steady, asking nothing.

Behind him the whole room had gone quiet, and nobody told him to take his hand out.
