The Green was on the school step when they came out, folded once, with the sleeves tucked under.

Ansel stopped in the doorway with the lamp at his back and looked at it the way he had looked at the Grey on a frosty morning, as at a returned thing that has come home ahead of its owner. A scrap of paper lay on top, weighted with a pebble from the lake shore. He crouched and read it by the light from the door. It was written in a hand that shook at the tails of the letters. Warm enough now, Master. The wood has come. Thank the knitter for the loan of her best work, and tell her it has done what it was made to do.

He read it twice. Then he stood up with the jumper in both hands, and did not know where to put his face.

"Put it on," said Hester.

She had come out behind him with the tin held flat against her coat, and she said it to the lane and not to him. The thaw dripped from the eaves in the dark. Down the shore a gutter ran like a small tap left open.

"I am wearing a jumper."

"You are wearing a shell. I have seen it. Put on the other."

He pulled the Green over his head, over the Grey and under the coat, and the wool came down his ribs and settled, and the warmth took him from the shoulders to the waist as it had taken him on this same step with the lake wind going through his shirt. It was a good warmth. It did not stop at the skin. It went in a hand's depth and then slowed, and a little above the hem it ended, as plain as a rule drawn across a slate, and below that line the wool was only wool. He put his palm on the place and felt it, and knew that she saw him do it.

"It is the honest bit," he said, since a silence was too large to leave alone. "Mina told me so. I mean, she told the lane. The lane told me."

"I did not ask you to tell me."

"No."

She was looking at the dark end of the shore path, where it bent down to the quay and the Ice Road began. Her face in the lamplight from the door was the face he had known for a winter, shut at the jaw and plain, and under it, a thing moved. He had watched her too long not to see it. It was the look of a knitter who has found a thread that does not belong to the piece and cannot decide whether to cut it.

"I cannot think in a room," said Hester. "Walk with me."

He walked.

They went down the shingle and out onto the ice by the old way, past the Net Sheds with the Heron asleep inside them, and the planks gave place to a surface that rang under the heel. The Ice Road ran away from them in a line of lantern poles. Every lamp was lit. The Breaking was near and the town kept the road open to the last, and somebody had been out at dusk with a taper, as they were out every dusk, post after post, so that the whole long causeway across the lake stood in small gold beads on the black with the stars thick over it. The lake did not look like water. It looked like a floor in a great dark hall, and the lamps like a row of candles left by a congregation that had gone home.

He wore Tilda's scarf. It was lumpy and blue and it lay round his neck with its knot like a fist under his chin, and it warmed him in the plain loaf way it always did, and he put his chin down into it as they walked. Neither of them spoke. Hester kept a pace ahead and a little to the side, the tin under her arm, and her boots made small clean knocks on the ice. He matched his step to hers without meaning to, as he matched it to a slow child on the shore path, and found that it was no effort at all.

The ice spoke.

It spoke from far out, a long low boom that began at the north shore and ran under them toward the town like a loaded cart crossing a plank bridge, and he felt it come up through his soles and into the long bones of his legs. He had learned to take it for weather. Tonight it did not feel like weather. It felt like a voice that had been talking under his boots all winter, and he had been too busy with his jokes to ask what it said.

They passed the fifth pole, and the sixth. At the ninth, where the shore had fallen away behind them to a low dark line with a few windows burning, she stopped. She stood in the middle of the road between two lanterns with the light falling on her from both sides, and turned round, and he stopped too, an arm's length off.

She did not say anything. She held the tin against her chest.

He looked at her, and he let himself think about the boy.

He had not done it in the open since he was a boy. He had thought of him in pieces, in the dark, with the lamp out, the way a man looks at a sore tooth with his tongue and is careful not to bite. He had never once brought him out and stood him in a lit place and let him be looked at. He did it now, on the ice, with the lake under his boots, because there was nowhere else left in him for the boy to stand.

He had been small. That came first, always, the smallness, the sense of a body too little for the cold that had got into it. He had been put down on a quay in a winter of ice with a bag and a label, and the quay had been very long and the people on it very tall, and they had all had their backs to him, because it was a town that gave its kindness to the ferry and not to the child it carried. He had stood against a wall for a day. He did not remember crying. He remembered that the cold had come into him by the feet, as if the lake had decided he would do, and by evening it had been in his bones, and he had known it for a thing he would carry the rest of his life. That had been how the world was made. The world had the warm in it and the cold in it, and he was the cold.

Then the girl had come down to the water.

He remembered her boots, which were too big, and a grey scarf wound to the chin that was too long for her and had come out of the same bale as every scarf in the town. She had a fist held in front of her. She had not looked at him. She had walked straight down the planks with her eyes on the middle of the harbor like a person carrying a thing she would drop if she looked at it, and she had stopped in front of him, and put out the fist, and opened it.

A scrap of grey wool. A crooked first row. He had taken it because it was held out and there was no way not to.

And it had been warm.

He did not think a boy could describe it, and he did not think a man could either. It had been warm in the way a hand is warm. It had gone up his arm from the palm and across his chest and down to his feet, and the whole lake had been very small in the face of it, and she had turned and walked back up the quay without a word. He had stood holding it. That night in a loft over a net store, in a stranger's blanket, he had slept as he had not slept in his life, with the scrap pressed into his fist under his cheek, and in the morning the cold had been there and the scrap had been there too, and one of them had been stronger.

He had been warm that winter. The whole of it. From the quay to the thaw.

He had been a small boy, and he had given away every other thing he owned since.

"Why did you come?" said Hester.

It was very quiet. She had her eyes on his face and she did not look away, and the lantern on her left put a thin line of gold along her cheek. "To Larkmere. You could have gone to a school in a town with a railway. I read the post. I have seen the ones that took it before you, and the ones that did not take it, and none of them wanted the lake. Why did you want it?"

He had a good answer. He had four. They stood in order at the back of his throat, the one about the stipend and the one about the sea air and the one about the fine view of the water, and the fourth, which was a very good joke about the ferry. He looked at them all, and every one of them was a coat he had put on over the thing underneath. He took them off one at a time, as he had once taken off his coats on a cold stoop, and he stood on the ice in front of her with nothing between.

"I came for the winter I was warm," said Ansel.

The ice boomed.

It went under them, long and low and rolling, and neither of them moved. He did not hear what it said. He heard her, which was a different thing, the small quick catch of her breath, as chalk catches on a slate and the hand feels it before the ear does.

"I was twelve," said Hester.

"I know."

"I did not know you."

"You were not meant to."

"I should have. I have made a jumper for every person who stepped off that ferry for nine winters, and I could not have told you your name." She had begun to shake, a fine tremor in the hand that held the tin, and the tin rattled once against the button of her coat. "I measured you. I read your wrist. You stood in my workroom with your cuffs up and I read your wrist, and I did not find it. I looked for a boy on the quay this evening. I looked an hour. There is only a smooth place."

"It was a very small thing to you."

"It was not small." She said it fiercely. "I know what I put into it. I know the hours. I put my whole wish into that grey, because I wanted a thing I made to be enough, and I did not know what for. And it was for you. It was for a boy on a wall I never looked at, and I put it in your hand, and I forgot you. And you took it and kept it all that time and came back across the water to find whoever had made it." She drew breath. "And you did not say."

"I could not."

"Why?"

"Because you would have been kind," said Ansel.

She stood quite still. The lantern on her right hissed.

"You would have been kind about it. You are, you know, under the rest. You would have put a jumper on me in a day and been done, and I should have been a debt on your books, a thing to be paid off and closed. I could not bear that. I wanted to be a man you had met." He heard his voice go, and did not stop it. "I thought that if I stayed long enough, and was warm enough, and was no trouble, you might, in the end, look at me for a reason that had nothing to do with a tin."

The ice spoke again, farther off.

She came forward.

She did it in one step, and a second, and the toe of her boot struck his, and she did not draw back. The tin was gone. She had put it away inside her coat, and her hands had come up and stopped in the air, uncertain, and gone down. She stood so close that he could see the lantern in each of her eyes, two small gold beads, and the grey iris ringed round them like river ice round a stone. She had to look up. Her scarf had slid from her chin and the whole line of her jaw was bare, and her mouth was a little open, and he could not think of a single thing to do with his face.

She leaned in, and put her forehead against his.

It was a small cool weight. It rested at the bone above his brows, and he felt every inch where it touched him, and his eyes shut of themselves. Her nose was an inch from his own. Her breath came out and reached his mouth, quick and light and shaking, and it smelled of cold air and lanolin and the faint iron of the workroom racks, and it went across his lips and was gone, and was followed by the next. He breathed it in. He could not have said where the lake ended and she began. The cold came up through the soles of him, the whole winter's cold, the cold he had carried off the Heron, and the Green lay warm to the waist, and her breath lay warm on his mouth, and he could not tell any of it apart. It was all one place. The lake that had taken him by the feet was the same lake that had once been held in a child's fist and made small, and he stood on it now with the same cold and the same warmth and no difference between them, and thought that perhaps there never had been.

His hands were at his sides.

He had put them there. He had told them to stay, as he had told them to stay on a chalk tray in the schoolroom when she stood an inch from his chest, and they would not. They had closed to fists and opened and closed again. The fingers worked at the air as if they were drawing a thread. He felt them wanting her, the plain size of it, the want to put them on her waist, in the wool at the back of her coat, along the cold hard line of her jaw, and he held them where they were with the whole of his will, because a thing asked for was a thing that could be refused. He had spent his life being no trouble. He had never learned how a man asked.

She did not ask either.

He felt her not ask. It was in her stillness, the held breath, the forehead pressed hard enough to hurt. Her hands hung at her sides as his did. He could feel the effort of it come off her like heat off a stove. She had built her whole life on being exact, and exact people do not ask, they measure, and she stood in the lamplight and did not measure. She only stayed.

"Hester," he said.

"Do not."

"I wasn't going to say anything clever."

"I know." Her breath moved on his mouth. "That is why."

A hair's breadth. Less. He could have closed it. He could feel the whole winter standing in it, the quay and the tin and the frozen planks, and every door he had ever shut a laugh across, and not one of them was shut now. He did not close it. She did not either. They stood on the ice between the ninth pole and the tenth with their foreheads together and their hands at their sides and their breath going out between them in one cloud, and the cloud hung in the lantern light and did not part.

Under their feet the lake spoke. It rolled in from the north shore and ran beneath them, long and deep and slow, as it had been running the whole of the winter, and the ice trembled with it, and the lamps shook on their poles.

Neither of them moved away.
