The grey came up behind the far shore by degrees, as the lamp comes up in a schoolroom on the first short day of winter, and Ansel found he could see his own breath before he could see his hands.

He had stood on the shingle since the dark. It might have been the night entire. A night is hard to measure when it has been spent an inch from a woman with both arms behind your back, and the inch itself had gone out of true a good while ago, when the first of the town came down, because a shore with other people on it gives a man reasons to stand correctly. He now stood the width of a ruler from Hester's shoulder. His own shoulders had stopped complaining hours since and gone on to a dull ache that did not trouble to announce itself.

The town came down in ones and twos and then in families, the way a school fills on the morning of the visiting inspector, with a great deal of coughing and very little talk. They had put on their Winter Knits. He knew them by now as he knew the handwriting of his own pupils: the porter in his red blanket, gone shabby at one corner; the stout woman with the brown fringed shawl; the old woman who had sat on a coil of rope the first night and said Well, and had been quite right. Nobody had told him the custom. It was understood, like the rule against whistling while a sum was being done. A town stood on its shore at first light and watched the ice go, and did not make a speech about it.

Tilda Brook had planted herself at his left elbow with her second lumpy blue scarf wound twice round her neck and her needles going. She knit with her tongue between her teeth and her eyes on the lake, like a sentry who has been told the enemy will arrive at dawn and has resolved to look busy when it does.

"You've got a lump in your coat," she said, without turning her head.

"It is a very dignified lump."

"It's red. I can see the end of it." The needles clicked. "It's the worst red I ever saw."

"I am told it is rust."

"Give it to her before it goes out," said Tilda, "or I will."

Mina Cray came along the line in her flour-white apron with the chipped blue teapot held out in front of her like a lantern and a stack of cups hooked over one forearm, pouring for the town without a word. She reached the two of them and stopped. She looked at his arms, and at Hester's, and at the ruler's width of air between, and pursed her mouth.

"I'll not ask what you've got back there," said Mina, and went on down the shingle with the pot.

Gran sat on an upturned crate at the front of the shore with her cane of pale ash laid across her knees and her face to the lake. She had not turned round. She did not need to. Ansel had learned that she saw the whole of a room through the back of her head, as every good teacher does.

The deckhand who had coiled line on the Heron the whole way across came along the stones toward the slip with a coil on his shoulder, and nodded to Ansel in passing.

"She'll be out by noon if the ice minds itself, teacher," he said. He said it kindly, the way one tells a man the shop is open. "Anyone with a bag had best be on the quay."

Then he was gone toward the Net Sheds, and Ansel stood in his coats and understood that he had been given the size of the thing again, the other way about. The door that had shut behind him in the autumn opened at the Breaking, and it opened both ways. The Heron would run. The letter at the bottom of his pocket and the river it was addressed to were a ferry ride off, and she would be nosing out of the Net Sheds before the morning was old. Anyone could go. Either of them could. He had told Hester one true thing on the ice and had not yet said the rest of it, and the rest of it was only that he did not wish to be on that boat, and a man cannot be expected to carry a sentence like that across a schoolroom without dropping the chalk.

He had thought about the scarf all night, standing there, as the one thing he had ever made to be kept. All his life he had handed things to people who had given him nothing, so that nothing would be owed on either side, and the handing-on had been the sum of his art. This was the only gift he had ever made with his own two hands for one person to take and wear. He had thought it clean, the way a sum is clean when it comes out on the first try. He gave, she owed nothing, and that was how he had arranged it.

Out on the lake the ice spoke, low and long, and the town drew one breath together.

He brought his arm round.

The scarf came with it, all of it, in a heap in his fist, and in the grey light it was worse than it had been by the loft lamp. The edge swung in and out like a river that had lost an argument with its banks. There was a hole in it the size of a thumbnail. Near the bottom the length veered left as though it had been called away. He had made a great many poor things in his life, most of them on blackboards, and had wiped every one of them off. This one could not be wiped.

The murmur along the shore did not stop so much as drain away. He felt the town turn its face toward him as a back row turns when the master drops the chalk.

He laughed first, because he always did, and heard it go out thin over the stones, and let it. Then he held the scarf out across the ruler's width of air. His hand shook, and he found he could no longer tell whether that was the cold or the other cause, and that it made no difference.

"I made this," said Ansel. "Last night. It took the whole of the night and a great deal of patience that was not mine. It is the ugliest object in the county, and it is warm, and I would like you to have it."

Hester looked at it.

She looked at the heap in his fist for a long moment, and her face did none of the things it had done on the quay. The mouth did not thin. The eyes did not go to the wool and stay there. It was quite open. She put out one cold hand and took the loose end between finger and thumb, and drew it once across the pad of her thumb, slowly, as if checking it for harm.

He had seen her do that. He had seen her do it on the first night, on the planks, with a length of grey yarn that had slid off her knee and found the toe of his boot.

It came back to him whole, as a sum comes back when you stop trying to do it.

The yarn. He had picked it up. It had been faintly warm in his glove, a small warmth, the sort a river stone keeps at the end of a long afternoon, and on any other night he would have missed it. On that night, with the lake in his bones and a quay full of kindnesses handed on and gone, it had stopped him where he stood. It was the only warm thing that had stayed in his hand. When she took the ball back, the warmth had drained out of the glove and he had closed his fingers on nothing, as if he could keep it.

She had given first. Before the Green, before the swatch, before the wrist, before a single stitch of anything had been made for him, a length of grey wool had passed from her hand to his on the quay and been warm, and she had said thank you in a voice like a shut door, and he had gone away believing that he had given her a joke. She had been giving since the Heron. He had not seen it, because it was too small and too steady to be called a gift, and because he had never in his life been any good at taking.

He had had the sum wrong from the start. He found he did not mind in the least.

Her thumb stopped.

It stopped on the wool for the length of a blink, her eyes narrowing at the rust red as though it had answered back. Then it moved on, and she gave a nod, once, to the scarf.

"The third row is pulled too tight," said Hester.

"I was afraid it would come undone."

"It won't."

She said it in the flat voice, the dry one she kept for gauge, and she turned the loose end over in her fingers and found a second fault and did not say it. Then she lifted her chin out of her own scarf. It was the smallest movement. A ferry lowers her gangplank like that, with the weight of the boat behind it and no sound at all.

He stepped in, and the ruler's width of air was gone.

He laid the scarf round her over the grey one, twice, and the ends hung uneven, one longer than the other by a hand, and he could not make them match. His hands went to her collar to settle a fold and stayed there. Her own scarf was cold under his fingers, as it always had been, and the rust lay over it warm as a child's palm after a race. Under his thumbs, in the hollow of her throat, he could feel her pulse going hard and fast through two layers of wool. He did not move his thumbs. He told himself he was straightening the fold. He had been telling himself small useful lies for a great many winters, and this one was the least convincing he had ever told, and he went on telling it.

She gasped.

It was a small sound, and every soul on the shingle heard it. She drew breath all the way in, as one does stepping from a cold porch into a kitchen, and her eyes closed and her mouth came open on it, and under his hands her throat went still and then swallowed. The warmth went into her as it had never been let go into anything. It went right through the grey, the cold polite grey she had worn to the jaw all her winters, into the skin of her neck and the long bones of her shoulders and down. He watched it reach her. It took the color before anything else, up from the collar to her cheeks, and then the hands. Her left hand, which she had brought out at last from behind her, closed in the rust wool at her breast and held on, and the knuckles went from white to pink one at a time, like a row of children being called by name. At her back, where her elbow pinned it, the brown corner of a parcel showed. He saw it and did not look at it.

"Oh," said Hester, very quietly.

A murmur ran down the shore and settled. The porter took off his cap for no reason he could have given. Mina Cray, in the middle of a pour, spilled tea across the stones and did not notice. The cane struck the shingle once.

"Mark it," said Gran, to the back row. "The lot of you."

Hester opened her eyes. They were wet and very clear, and she did not look at the town, or at the lake, or at the scarf. She looked at him, from an inch off, with the color high in her face, and she was warm. He could see it. He could see her warm all the way through, as he had seen a frozen window clear from the middle outward. His hands were at her collar still. They ought to have come away. Her free fist held the wool at her breast, and she made no move to take his hands off her, or to put them back, and so they stayed exactly where they were, and neither of them breathed.

The ice went quiet.

It had been talking all night, and it stopped, and the shore heard the stopping. Then a single crack ran out from the shingle at their feet across the grey of the lake, long and white and straight as a ruling on a slate, and went on running until it was lost in the dark water by the far poles.

The shingle began to shake.

He felt it first in his boots and then in his shins. It was no longer a groan. It came up through the stones and into the soles of his feet, and into the soles of hers, and through her, he thought, and into the two hands he had at her collar, so that for a moment they were a single instrument being struck from underneath. The cold in the long bones of his legs, the cold that had stood there since his second step off the Heron, let go like a hand opening. He did not take his eyes from her face. He felt the toll go out of him down through the stones as a ferry slips her mooring, all at once and without apology, and what came in behind it was only the morning, ordinary and cold and entirely his own.

Hester's fingers tightened in the rust wool. Her lips parted. Behind her the whole grey lake lifted, and broke, and the ice went out with a roar.
