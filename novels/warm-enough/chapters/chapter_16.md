She did not know when her hands had stopped.

The lamp had burned down to a bead and the stove to a red seam between two pieces of black, and the workroom lay round her in a brown dusk, the racks standing about in it with their skeins hung like coats on pegs. The needles were still in her lap. The deep blue wool ran over her knee from the ball, and under her fingers the Blue had grown by a hand's breadth of sleeve, in rows she had no memory of knitting. She laid her palm on it. It was warm. She had known it would be before she touched it, because she had worked it with her guard down and her eyes shut.

She took her palm away and looked at it.

Outside, the step gave one dry crack, as boards do at the bottom of a hard frost. She waited for another. None came. The kettle ticked once on the stove, cooling, and the house settled round her, and she did not go to the window.

She drew the needles out and rolled the work up on itself, sleeve inward, and laid the cloth over it at the far end of the table, next to the shoulders that had been warm for one breath on a man in his shirtsleeves. She set it a forearm's length from her. It was not a long way. She sat and looked at the distance until the window went grey.

Gran came down at first light, by the loft stair, in stages. The cane went ahead of her on every tread and the rest of her followed it, and at the bottom she stood and took stock of the room like a woman counting a flock. She looked at the lamp, which had burned itself dry. She looked at the cloth. She looked at Hester, who had not been to bed, and said nothing about it, and went to the stove and fed it and set the copper kettle over the first of the flame.

Under her left arm, pinned against the shawl and its one small hole, she carried a book.

It was thick, in a cloth cover the color of old tea, gone soft at the corners from a great many hands, with a ribbon sewn into the spine that hung out at the bottom edge like a tongue. She brought it to the long table and let it down in front of Hester with both bent hands, as one sets down a sleeping child, and stood over it with her weight on the cane.

"The pattern book," said Hester.

"It has been in the loft chest since before you could hold a needle. I thought it wanted air."

"I have my own patterns."

"You have your own gauge. Patterns are another thing." Gran tapped the cover once with a knuckle like a knot in rope. "There's a page marked. You needn't turn to it."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good. Then you'll not mind it lying there." She went back to the kettle.

Hester did not touch the book. She looked at the ribbon, which lay out across the scrubbed wood faded to the color of a thing that has been handled for love and not for use, and then at the cover, and then at the back of her grandmother's head. She had learned a good deal from this woman, and one lesson was that whatever Gran set in front of you she had set there on purpose. The marked page was a stitch laid down to be found. She would not find it. She pushed the book the length of her hand away, and it was still entirely in the room.

"I'll not say it twice," said Gran, to the kettle.

Hester's hands went still. She knew what was meant, and it was not the page. It had been said once at this table in the first frost, in four plain words, and she had answered it, and she had been hearing her own answer ever since, in her own voice, a good deal too clearly. A rule for knitters who wanted to be wasted. She had been so sure of the ring of it.

"I haven't asked you to," she said.

"No." Gran poured. "You haven't." She took the cup in both hands and went up the loft stair with it, one tread at a time, and the door at the top closed with the gentleness of a woman giving a house its privacy. A board creaked overhead. Then the bed.

Hester sat in the quiet with the book at one end of the table and the cloth at the other, and the whole length of the wood between them.

She got up and went to the racks.

The moss green hank hung among the ones she did not use. It was the color of the north side of a wall, a green with a good deal of brown in it and no gloss. She took it down and drew the end across her thumb. The ply was sound and the twist was even, and it told her nothing at all, because wool that has not been knit has not yet been anyone's. She wound it into a ball without hurry, and the ball sat in her palm, round and plain, and was not remarkable.

She cast on with the tape lying cold at her collarbone, and she did not take it off to measure. She had the numbers already. The breadth of his chest, the length of the arm to the thumb bone, the width of the shoulders she had not wanted to know and could not lose. She cast on for them without once saying them to herself, and the foundation came off the needle even and sound, and she knit the hem.

It was a good hem. It was the best she knew how to make. She worked it as she had worked every hem of her life, with her mind turned politely to one side, and she watched the stitches come out correct and cool as pebbles, and she did not mind. Nobody looks at a hem. The cold of it ran up the needles into her fingers, the old familiar cold, and she was very nearly comforted.

Then she began the body in seed stitch.

It is a plain stitch, knit and purl and knit and purl, a small pebbled cloth that hides nothing. A cable has a front to stand behind. Seed has no behind. Every hesitation sits in it as a knot, in plain view, and she had chosen it from the whole shelf of her mind for that reason, and in the same breath wished she had not.

She worked the first rows with her jaw set. Correct, tidy, polite. The wool said nothing and she was relieved. She worked another. The stove ticked. Overhead the bed creaked and was still.

Then she stopped.

She sat with the tip of the needle in the next stitch and her thumb pressed against the yarn, and the whole winter lay in front of her like a skein she had not yet found the end of, and she thought, quite clearly, in her own voice: this row.

She had never done it on purpose. She had done it once by accident, in the first frost, with her thoughts turned out like pockets, and torn it to ruin. She had done a breath of it with a man standing in the Blue. She had never sat down in daylight with her eyes open and told her hands, this one, and meant it.

She meant it.

She did not think a name. She thought of a shoulder against hers along the whole length of her arm, in grey light, through every layer, a heat she had not made. She thought of the wrist she had measured with the tape, narrow and hard, with the pulse going under her finger, and she let the thought of it go through her hands as it was. She thought of three coats under her fists and the shiver running through them, and how she had held on. She let each one pass down her arms and into the wool a stitch at a time, knit and purl, and her breath went out of her very slowly.

The row came warm.

It came up through the needles into her wrists, slowly, the heat of a stone that has lain all morning in the sun and is carried indoors. It did not stop at the wrist. It went up the soft inside of the elbow and across to the chest where her breath had gone shallow and the tape lay, and then it reached her face. Her ears were hot. She had not flushed since she was a girl and she had no practice at it. The white went out of her knuckles first. They had been bloodless to the second joint since Longnight, and she watched the color come back into them from the palm outward, and flexed them, and they obeyed her without a tremor. She sat perfectly still with the needles in her hands and the row warm under her thumbs, one course of moss green the breadth of his chest, and her pulse was in her fingertips where she could feel it against the wood. It was slow and level, and then one stroke came early and the rest hurried after.

She finished the row, because it had been begun. She turned the work. She laid her palm flat on the stitches, and then, since a knitter tests what she does not trust, she lifted the work and set the row against the inside of her left wrist, where the vein runs close under the skin. The warmth lay along her pulse and matched it. She held it there until she found that she had closed her eyes.

Warm. A plain deep warmth that was not hers and was not borrowed, put there by the hand that had put it there. Under her palm the little knots of seed stitch were warm one by one, like a row of held hands.

She did not rip it out.

That was what she noticed afterward. In the first frost she had hauled the yarn from the needle and torn the row to nothing with a noise in her throat, and the wool had lain curled on the table, faintly warm, and she had not been able to look at it. Now it sat on the needles and her hand did not go for the end. She let it be what it was.

It stayed. She sat a long while to be certain of it. She got up and fed the stove, and walked the length of the table, and looked at the pale light on the lake through the glass, and came back and laid her hand on the row, and it was warm. She lifted the cloth from the Blue and slid two fingers under the fold at the shoulder, and that was warm too, as warm as the hour she had put it down. Two warm things on one table now, and neither of them would ever go cool of its own accord. Wool did not go cool. She had always counted that against it.

Warm things went. It was the row she had cast on first, years ago, and everything since had been worked on top of it. She had known a man once who went at a thaw. She had not been surprised, and she had not been warm again, and for a long time that had seemed a fair exchange. A cold garment cost nothing when its wearer left. A warm one stayed on the table afterward and went on being warm, with nobody in it.

She looked at the next stitch.

She knew exactly what it would take, because she had just done it. A thought, one chosen thought, held through the whole row from the first stitch to the last. It was not hard. A child could do it. A child on the ice had done it, with her tongue between her teeth and no skill at all, and had beaten the best work Hester had ever turned. She sat with her needle in the stitch and her thumb on the yarn and could not make her hands go.

One row could be an accident. The second row would be hers.

Her eyes went to the door, as they had gone all winter. Her coat hung on the first hook with its right pocket low. The second hook held nothing. It was an ordinary iron hook, black, a little bent at the tip, screwed into the wood at the height of a tall man's shoulder, and she looked at it for a good while, and it did not become anything else.

The stove ticked. Upstairs, the bed was quiet. The ribbon lay where she had pushed it, a thin pale line across the wood pointing at nothing, and she kept her eyes from the cover, and she felt exactly where the marked page lay inside it, as one feels a thin place in a fleece.

She was still sitting so, with her palm on the one warm row and the needle in the second, when the sound came across the lake.

It was thin and a very long way off, and at first she took it for the ice, which gives out bright notes in hard frost like a plucked wire. But ice does not keep time. It rang, and stopped, and rang again, small and flat and brass, a bell on a runner, out on the white. The winter post. Everyone in Larkmere knew that small voice from the far side of the lake, and it had never once failed to make a room go quiet.

She did not stand. She sat with her hand on the row and listened to it come.

It was far off yet. It rang again, a little nearer.
