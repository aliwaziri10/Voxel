Hester heard it through the ceiling of the workroom, before there was any light.

She had not been asleep. She had not slept a night through since the Heron, and she had stopped remarking on it. She sat at the long table with the lamp turned low and the blue ball in her lap and the needles laid across her knee, doing nothing with either, and the sound came down through the boards from the roof: a flat scrape, a pause, the small dry chink of ice breaking away and falling a long way. Then the scrape again.

Her grandmother slept in the loft directly under that roof and had never in her life slept through a noise she did not approve of. She was sleeping through this one. Hester listened to the next scrape and the one after it, and knew the manner of it before she knew the cause, as she knew a hand by its tension. It was careful work. It was done by a man who was afraid of being heard and did not know how to be quiet on a tiled roof.

She did not need to guess. She had been collecting the evidence since the first frost, the way she collected loose ends from the floor of the workroom, without ever deciding to. The shutter of the Kettle Room, which had banged in every wind since the autumn, had gone quiet. The gate at the head of Spindle Lane no longer shrieked on its pin. The lantern poles along the Ice Road, which had leaned out of true for as long as she had known them, stood straight at the foot where the shore ice met them, packed round with gravel and a turn of rope. The latch of the school door took its door with a clean click. The quay folk said it was the frost settling things, and the matter had been left there, since a town will accept a great deal of its own luck without asking where it came from. Hester had passed all of it. She had drawn her thumb across the new pin of the lane gate and found it sound, and said nothing, and put it where she put the things she had decided not to know.

She put on her coat and went out by the shop door.

The cold stood in the lane like a wall that had been waiting for her. The snow lay thick and unmarked, blue in the dark, and above the black line of the roofs the sky behind the Net Sheds had begun to go the color of undyed wool. Nobody was awake in Spindle Lane. The shutters were fast. A ladder stood against the eave of her own house, a plain ladder she had never seen, with its feet packed in the snow, and at the top of it, on the roof, a lantern hung from the ridge on a hook and threw a small yellow room of light across the tiles.

Ansel Brandt was in the middle of the light. He was on his knees on the pitch of her roof in every coat he owned, with a flat shovel in both hands, working at the ice that lay along the eave in a thick grey lip. Chalk showed at both cuffs even from the lane. The lantern shook on its hook. She watched it shake and watched him, and it was a little while before she understood that the light was not trembling on its own account.

He was shaking. It ran through the whole of him. It was not the shiver of a man in a draught, which comes and goes. It was a steady fine tremor from the shoulders down, like a tension carried through every stitch of a fabric, and when the shovel struck the ice she saw it travel back up the handle into his hands. His breath went up white. He was grinning. She could see the grin from the lane, set on his face like a darn on a worn heel, and above it his eyes watched his own hands work as if they belonged to another man.

"Come down," said Hester.

He started so badly that the shovel rang. He looked over his shoulder, and the lantern swung, and the grin went wider. "Good morning," he said, and laughed before he had said anything more, the high bright laugh with the shake in it. "I am afraid I have been caught. I had hoped to finish before anyone was about. I am nearly done with the eave."

"Come down."

"It is the ice, you see. It gets under the tiles in the night and lifts them, and the first real fall of snow brings the whole lip of the roof into the lane. Your grandmother's window is directly below. I should hate to be the man who let it." His teeth caught on the last word, and he laughed that off too. "Only a few minutes more."

She stood in the snow with her hands in her pockets. She looked at the eave, and the ladder, and the tiles along the near side of the roof, and saw that three of them, which had been loose since the summer and which she had meant to see to, had been reset and bedded in fresh pitch. She saw it the way she saw a mended row, by the care in it. Nobody had asked him. Nobody could have.

Her eyes went to his right hand on the shovel. The wrist was bound now, a strip of grey linen wound under the cuff by a man who had not been taught, with the ends tucked in anyhow. It was not a bad binding. It was not a good one.

"You are working that hand," she said.

"It is the only one I have on this side."

"It will not mend if you use it."

"It will mend if I ignore it. That is the whole principle." He laughed, and the shovel struck the ice.

"How many roofs?"

"I beg your pardon?"

"Since the first frost. How many."

He opened his mouth, and the laugh came ahead of the answer. "I could not give you a figure. A tile here. A latch there. The lane is full of things that want a nail." The shovel dipped. "The bases of the lantern poles were great fun. One goes out onto the ice with a bucket of gravel and a good deal of rope, and the whole lake looks on."

"At night."

"The lane is empty at night." He said it lightly. "Nobody has to stand about. It is a great deal less trouble."

She turned that over as she would turn a flaw. Nobody had to stand about. Nobody had to come out to a gate and say it was well done. Nobody had to hand him a blanket in return, or a pie, or a seat by the fire, or any of the things a town gave a man who put its gates right in daylight, and which he would have to hold in his hands before everyone and be seen to have received. He had put the work where it could not be thanked. It was a seam worked on the wrong side of the cloth, where no thumb would ever run along it, and the whole good of it was that nobody would find the thread.

She stood in the snow with that, and found it heavier than she had expected.

She had not laughed on the shingle, either. She had stood at the top of the beach with her hands in her pockets and watched him go down and get up and go down, with the whole school shrieking, and she had not been able to find it funny, as a person cannot find a dropped stitch funny while it is still running. A man who fell that often and that cheerfully had learned that falling was a thing to be done for other people's benefit. She had watched him laugh at his own wrist and put it away in his sleeve. She had gone home and not slept.

The grey light was coming. Along the edge of the harbor the first of the poles stood out of the dark. The lantern shook on its hook, and so did he, and she saw now that it had nothing to do with the roof. The roof was only a roof. Cold tiles, a cold shovel, a morning of ordinary frost: a man could stand those. This was the other cold, the one that had met him on the quay and gone into him to the marrow. She had seen it on a great many newcomers over a great many winters. It was the whole lake in a man, and no stove reached it and no coat shut it out, and it came up in a tremor like this whenever he stopped holding it off with work. The Grey sat on him under all the coats. She knew it was there as she knew a dropped stitch in her own knitting. It was being exactly as cold as she had made it.

She looked at the drop. The eave stood a full storey and more above the lane, the lane was frozen cobble under a hand of snow, and the foot of the ladder was packed in with ice. A man who fell from there did not get up laughing. He broke a leg, and the school lost its master, and the winter went on without him.

"You are shaking," said Hester.

"It is the lantern. It has a draught." He laughed. The shovel struck the ice. "I am perfectly fine."

"You are not."

"A little chilly, I grant. It is the hour. I am quite fine, I assure you, it is nothing at all. I have been a good deal colder and made a great deal less fuss. I am fine."

"Stop being fine at me."

It came out of her low and flat, and it crossed the snow like a dropped needle. He stopped. The shovel stopped, held in the air above the ice. The lantern, which she had thought was swinging, hung still. For the space of a breath he stayed on his knees on her roof with the light on one side of his face and the dark on the other, and she saw the grin stay where it was and the face behind it go quiet.

"Ah," said Ansel.

She waited. She had waited for such a word to be followed by more, on the quay and again in the schoolroom, and she waited now for the laugh that would come in and cover it. It came. It was a small one, and it did not fit him. "Well," he said. "I shall have to think what to be instead. I have never had occasion to try." He got a boot under him. "Mind your ladder. I'll come down."

He turned on his knees on the eave. His boot found the lip of ice he had not yet cleared, and the sole went out from under him as clean as a hank slipping a peg.

She heard the shovel go, bounding and ringing down the tiles and into the lane. She saw him slide, one arm flung out, the right one, and the hand catch the iron of the gutter and close, and she saw the bound wrist bend back under the whole of his weight. She did not decide to move. She had the rungs under her boots and her hands on the cold rails and was at the top of the ladder before the shovel had finished falling, and the roof was in front of her with its white lip, and he hung over the edge of it to the chest with one hand on the gutter and his boots scrabbling at the ice.

She got her fist in the front of his coat.

It was the long brown one, and she took the cloth where the lapels met, and the grey-green beneath it, and the third beneath that, a great wad of wool in her right hand, and set her left on the rail and hauled. He came up out of the dark of the drop onto the pitch of the roof like a net coming in, all coats and elbows, and she felt through the cloth against her knuckles the whole of him going. It was worse than the shaking she had watched from the lane. It was a tremor right through, quick and fine, of a cold that had been kept in a long time and was let out. She held on. She had her knuckles against his breastbone through three coats and the Grey, and she could feel every layer of it, the shiver under them like a pulse that had learned to run.

"Well," said Ansel, into her shoulder, in a voice that had lost its top. He drew a breath. He tried the laugh and it would not come whole. "The roof has views."

She did not let go. He did not seem to have noticed that she had not. Then he did, and she felt him notice: the pause in the shaking, the held breath, the cold weight against her fist going very still. Her knuckles were warm. She saw that from a long way off. She looked down at her own hand wound in his coat and felt it warm through the cloth where it pressed, and what she did about it was to open the hand.

"Down," she said. "Now."

"Yes. Quite." He put the right hand against his chest and held the wrist there, and she did not look at it. He turned on the rungs and began to go down, slowly, with the stiff care of a man who has used up his luck for the morning. At the foot he stopped in the snow with the lantern light behind him and looked up the ladder at her, and the laugh found itself at last, short and bright and not very steady. "It was a triumph," he said. "I should like that recorded."

He bent, took up the shovel out of the snow, and went away up the lane with it over his shoulder, in his coats, in the thin blue light, and did not look back. She watched him to the head of the lane, where the gate that no longer shrieked closed behind him with a click.

She was alone on her roof.

The lantern still burned on the ridge. The tiles lay about her white and sound, three of them freshly bedded, the eave cleared for the length of her arm. She stood on the pitch with one hand on the ladder and the other still half shut, as though it had been left holding the cloth. The ice lay under her boot, thick and grey and hard as a pressed fleece. It was cold. It was only cold. It was a plain cold that came up through the leather and stopped at the sole, the cold a person could stand on, and she stood on it and found that it was not the cold she was thinking of.
