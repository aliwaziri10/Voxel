He asked her to walk the Ice Road at dusk, and she went, because it was easier than finding the reason not to.

It was the deep of the winter now. The lake had been frozen so long that the ice had stopped groaning at night and lay silent and thick and dull under the snow, and the road across it was a trodden brown ribbon, a hand's width higher than the white on either side, running out from the end of the quay to the far shore. The lanterns had been lit already. They were lit at dusk, as they were every dusk, whether or not anyone stood out on the stones to see them kindle, and they stood down the length of the road in their poles, each one with its small yellow room of light, going away in a long dwindling line across the ice until the line ran into the grey.

Piers walked on her left, a pace off, with his coat unbuttoned and his hands loose at his sides.

He did not hurry, and he did not speak for the first dozen poles. She was grateful for that. She walked with her chin sunk in her scarf and her hands in her coat pockets and her boots finding the packed snow, and the cold came in through the leather of her soles and the wool of her stockings and settled in her ankles. It was an old cold. It had been there so long she hardly marked it any more, as one stops marking the tension of a long row. At her left shoulder, quite plainly, a warm thing walked. She could feel it. It came off the grey-blue jumper under his open coat the way warmth comes off a fleece laid to dry by a fire, not from the man but from the wool, a plain steady glow at the edge of the cold. She did not move toward it. She did not move away. She walked at the same pace, and kept her eyes on the road.

"I took my first bearing from the quay this morning," said Piers at last. "I thought it would be a very great feat. The lake hasn't moved a yard."

"Lakes do not."

"No. It's people who do the moving." He said it lightly, with the rueful tilt at the corner of his mouth, and she heard it and let it go by. "I'd forgotten how it looks from the ice. You come out of the town, and the poles go ahead of you in a row, and every one of them has a light on it, and it seems you might go on from lantern to lantern for a thousand miles and never be in the dark. I used to think it was the most hopeful thing I had ever seen. I think I still do."

"They are poles."

"They are poles with a lantern lit on each, at the same hour, every evening, by hands I've never seen." He turned his head. "That's all hope is, you know. It's lamps being lit at dusk by people who don't expect to be thanked."

She did not answer. She thought of a man who went out at night with a lantern and a nail in his pocket and left the gates mended in the morning, and she put it down quickly, as one lets a loose end fall.

They walked. The snow squeaked. Behind them the town got smaller, a low clutch of roofs under their smoke with the harbor lamps pricked along its edge, and the shore path swung round to the left and ran out along the water to a darker shape that was the school and the master's cottage on their strip of beach. She did not look at it. She walked with her eyes on the next pole, and the next, and her fingers curled in the lining of her pocket where it was cold.

"Your hands," said Piers. "They're blue."

"They are always blue."

"I know. I was there." He stopped. He had gone out on the road a pace ahead of her, and turned, and the lantern on the pole behind him laid a ring of gold on the snow at his feet and lit the ink on his fingers and the worn rib of the cuff. He held out his hand.

It was not a large gesture. It was a plain open hand, palm up, the fingers a little spread, the ink gone into the grain to the second joint. It was the hand that had held hers on the quay stones nine winters ago, and she knew every line of it, and it was warm. She could see it was warm. The frost did not settle on the cuff of the sleeve above it. It hung in the cold air quite steadily, a man's hand, offered, and under it the old wool burned along its turn.

"I run hot," said Piers gently. "It's the absurdity of this sweater. Give me them. They'll be right in a minute."

Her hand stayed in her pocket.

She did not tell it to. It stayed of itself, curled in the cold of the lining, and she looked at the open palm in front of her and felt her fingers close harder on the cloth. They would have been right in a minute. That was the truth. She knew exactly what would have happened. The blood would have come into them and the white gone out, and it would have been comfortable and simple and kind, and she would have been warm, and she did not take it.

She was thinking of a wrist.

It lay inside her own, as plain as a tension in a row, a long hard wrist with the pulse beating high under the skin and a cuff of moss green round the bone of it. She had held it, in a shop window, with her thumb on the inside. She had laid two cold fingers across it in the first week to read it. The wrist was in her own hand's memory, and her own wrist remembered it, and the memory ran up her forearm in the cold with a small steady ache, as a stitch aches that has been pulled to the wrong tension. It was not Piers's wrist. She kept her hand in her pocket and let it ache.

Piers waited for a moment. Then he lowered the hand, easily, as one lowers a needle, and he did not seem hurt.

"Walk with me a little farther," he said. "I want to say it where there's room."

She walked.

They went on to the place where the road ran straight out and the shore had fallen away behind them to a low line, and ahead the lanterns marched toward the north shore, small and gold, and on either hand the ice lay flat to the edge of the sky. It was nearly dark. There was a band of green at the rim of the west. The snow had gone blue. Their two shadows lay long on the road, thrown out from the nearest lantern, and joined at the feet.

"I am going at the thaw," said Piers.

"I know."

"When the map's done. I should have it finished by then, if the ice holds for the north shore, and I've a hope that it will." He looked out across the lake at nothing. "I'm going on the first ferry that runs. And I'd like you to come with me."

She had known it was coming. She had known it on the quay, with her fist round her scarf, and in the Kettle Room, with her thumb on his cuff, and she had known it in the lane when he looked across at the shop window. She had known it and had gone on walking toward it, as a person goes on knitting toward the end of a row she knows will be a mistake. She stood very still.

"There's a house," said Piers. He said it quite quietly. He kept his eyes on the lake. "It's a poor sort of house, and I've lived in it badly. But it has a long north window that looks onto a river I have never had the heart to draw, and a table in it that wants two. I'm not asking you for what I've no right to ask. I'm not asking you for the nine winters. I am only saying that there is a place that isn't a lake, and a road that isn't a road across ice, and that you are the one person I have ever known who ought to be in a room with a window." He turned his head, then, and looked at her. "I'd like to be the one who showed you."

For one second she could have said yes.

It was a very plain second. It had no weight in it. It came up whole out of nowhere and stood there in the blue air, as clear as a stitch on a needle: the white water at the foot of the quay, and the sound of a ferry's engines coming up under the deck, and the long slow slide of the Net Sheds going out of sight behind her. She saw the north window. She saw the river. She saw herself with her hands in her lap, in a room where nobody knew her name, and the whole of the cold she had carried for nine winters laid down on a table beside a pen. It was a good second. It was a door standing open. She stood in it, in the dusk, with the road under her and the lanterns burning, and she knew that if she had said yes in that second she would have meant it, and that he would have taken her arm, and they would have walked back to the town in the dark.

It passed.

"No," said Hester.

It came out of her without a catch. It was not harsh. It was a small, plain, level syllable, like a row knit without a snag, and it lay on the cold air between them and did not tremble. He nodded slowly, as a knitter nods at a row that has come out as expected. There was no surprise in his face and no grief either, only the gentle patient sadness of a man who has asked and been answered. He put his hands in his pockets.

"I thought you would," he said. "I wanted you to have been asked, that's all. I've had nine winters to think what I'd say if I could ask you anything, and it turns out to be that."

"I am sorry."

"Don't be." He smiled. It was the best smile he had, and it was all there. "I owe you the reason I left. I'll give it to you before I go, if you will let me. It's not a good reason. But it's yours."

"Keep it."

"I can't. I'll want it said." He looked at the road. "Not tonight."

They stood there. The cold came up round her boots, and her fingers lay curled in her pocket against the lining, and she waited for the cost.

It did not come.

She stood in the snow on the Ice Road and waited, with her whole body, for the thing a no does. She had known a no to cost. She had said it to herself on the quay on the night of the Heron and felt it go down like a dropped stitch running down the work. She had said it at the foot of a hook in a workroom, to a man's coat, and it had cost her a winter. A no was a thing that tore. It left a place in a person like a cast-off stitch, raw and sore, and she stood and braced for it with her hands in her pockets and her whole self drawn up small and tight. It did not come. There was nothing there. She had said no to a good man with a house and a window and a table that wanted two, and it had cost her exactly what it costs to put down a cup.

That was what frightened her.

She turned it over, standing in the failing light, with the dread rising slowly up the backs of her knees. It should have cost. If there was anything in her that had loved him, and she knew that there had been, and that it had been the plain part of her, the honest part, a no should have cost her a little. If there was anything left of it, it ought to have ached. And it had not, and she knew why. It was a thing with a hollow in it. A no costs nothing when the place it was meant to be paid from has already been spent, in another place, on a different account, and she had not known she had made the transfer. She had not made it. She had not said a word. Her hands had made it, one row at a time, in a shop window in the grey light, in a drift against a wall, at the foot of a ladder at dawn.

It was all spent. There was nothing left to pay a no with.

"Hester," said Piers, very gently. "You've gone white."

"It is the cold."

"Of course." He did not push. He stood with his hands in his pockets, in his thin jumper and open coat, with the lamplight at his back, and a patience on his face that she could have wept at. "We ought to go in. It's a long road, and I'm told there's soup."

"In a moment."

He nodded. He turned a little away from her, courteously, as one turns from a person who wants a minute to herself, and looked along the line of the poles toward the north, as if there were a thing in the dusk that wanted measuring.

She turned round.

She did not decide to. Her body did it, slowly, on the packed snow, as a skein turns on the rack when the draught takes it, and she stood looking back the way they had come. The Ice Road ran away behind her in its long line of lanterns, gold, dwindling, each one a little smaller than the last, down to the shore and the dark clutch of the town. And to the left of the town, where the shore bent out and the water lay black against the white, a single window was lit.

It was small and far off. It was the school. It stood on its strip of beach with the master's cottage beside it and one square of lamplight in its wall, facing the ice, a yellow hole in the blue. She could not have said how she knew the window. She had never in her life looked at it long enough to learn it. But she knew the exact shape of the light.

A figure stood in it.

It was only a shape, dark against the gold, a long way off across the lake, the width of her thumbnail at arm's length. It stood at the glass with its shoulders square and its arms at its sides. It was a man, and he was looking out across the ice toward the road, toward the line of lanterns and the two small figures standing between two poles, and he did not wave. He did not turn away. He did not lift a hand to the lamp or draw a curtain or step back into the room. He stood quite still in the lit square, as still as a motif worked into the cloth, with the whole of the dark lake between them.

She stood on the road with her hands in her pockets and looked at it, and the lit window looked back, and the figure in it did not move.
