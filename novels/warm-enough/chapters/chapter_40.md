The roar did not stop. It went on down the length of Larkmere Water in a long grinding thunder, floe riding up on floe, the grey plates of the old ice tilting and cracking and sliding out toward the middle of the lake, where the black water opened between them wider with every breath. Spray stood up off the broken edge and caught the light. The poles of the Ice Road leaned, and went over one at a time with their lanterns still on them, and were carried off.

Hester did not watch. She had watched the ice for nine winters. She stood with her back to it and her face a hand's breadth from his, and she was warm: the rust red at her throat, the long bones of her shoulders, her fingers, which had been cold for longer than she could have said and which she could now feel to the very ends. It was a plain, strange fact, like finding that a row she had given up for lost had been worked all along. The page had said the toll would be paid when the knitter was knit for. She had read it twice and believed not a word. She believed it now in her fingers.

The town had the tact of its years. Nobody said a word. The families turned from the shore one by one and went up the shingle toward their breakfasts with their backs very straight and their eyes on their own boots, and the porter went last, still holding his cap in both hands as though it might be asked for. The shore emptied behind them. The lake went on breaking, loud and glorious, and not one soul in Larkmere was looking at the two of them.

Along the quay the bench by the Net Sheds stood empty. She had sat on it in the autumn with a straight scarf growing on her needles and her face turned to the wall, and a man in four coats had held the end of a yarn out to her across the planks and made a joke about being unarmed. She had not laughed. She looked at the bench now with mild surprise, as at a stranger's work she had once mistaken for her own.

Ansel's hands were still at her collar. She could have said so. She could have lifted them away herself. She reached behind her instead, into the small of her back, where her elbow had held the parcel all night, and drew it out by its lumpy knot and held it in both hands between them.

She had spent the dark deciding how to give it. She found that she had decided nothing. There was no far edge to hold it by, no correct distance, nothing to keep her fingers back with. She set it against his chest, and his hands came down from her collar and closed over hers on the brown paper, and neither of them let go.

"Stay," said Hester.

It was one word. She had thought it would weigh more. It went out of her whole, across what was left of the inch, and landed, and nothing in her went after it to see what it had done. All her winters she had believed that asking would cost her the whole of her stock, and it cost her nothing at all. She was not afraid. She noticed it the way one notices, late and all at once, that a cuff has stopped pinching.

He laughed, because he always did. It broke in the middle, and he did not mend it. "Yes," said Ansel. "Yes. I had four things to say and I have dropped them all on the shingle."

He undid the knot. It gave at the first pull, the lumpy honest knot, and the paper fell open over his wrists and the cream lay in his hands.

In the plain light of a thawing morning she saw it as he saw it. It was not a handsome piece. The sleeves were not twins. The neck sat low on one side. It was the pale of wool before anyone has asked it to be anything, and at the left side seam, high up, where a man's arm would lift it, a thread-wide ladder ran down through the rows to the ribbing, open to the air. A cream jumper with one deliberate dropped stitch at the left side seam. She had made it, every stitch, and it was hers.

He found the gap without being shown. His thumb went to it and rested there, and she watched him feel the warmth come up through it into his hand.

"The left sleeve is long," said Ansel, who had looked.

"I know. I let it stand."

"Hester Tansley has left a fault standing."

"Do not make a speech."

"I would not dream of it," he said, and his voice was not steady.

He dropped the long brown coat on the stones, and then the grey-green one, and stood in the Green with the lake breaking behind him. She watched the Onion come out of the last of his layers the way a plain skein comes out of the wash, smaller than she had thought and a great deal more himself. He drew the Open Door over his head. The cream went down over the Green and over his shoulders, and his face came up through the low neck with his hair standing on end, and he went quite still.

She saw it reach him. It came into his face from the collar, as color runs up a skein, the whole length of him, collar to hem and out through the cuffs, and into the white tips of his fingers, which she watched go to the pink of a living hand and remain so. At the left side, where the ladder lay open, the warmth came through the seam and out into the air, and she felt it on her wrist from where she stood, a hand's width off. The door was open. It let the heat in and let it out, as she had known it would, and it was warmest of all at the gap.

He let it in. She saw him do it. His shoulders, which had been up round his ears since the Heron, came down.

"Oh," said Ansel, in a small voice she had never heard from him. Then, "It's warm." Then he said nothing at all, because there was nothing in it to be funny about.

Her hand was in his. She did not remember taking it. It was warm, and so was hers, the same warmth exactly, which she had not known could be done.

They went up through Larkmere with the thaw running in every gutter. He had gathered the coats and carried them over one arm like a man bringing in the wash. The lanes were empty, the whole town at its tables, and the chimneys smoked straight up, and water ran down Spindle Lane over their boots. She did not look back at the lake. She could hear it.

The workroom was as she had left it, the lamp burned out on the long table and the stove gone down to a red bed. The racks stood in their rows. No board creaked overhead.

He stopped inside the door. There were two hooks. She had hung her coat on the first for longer than she could have said.

He looked at the hooks. He looked at her. "Is this one taken?" he asked, and touched the second.

"It is a hook," said Hester. "That is what they are for."

He hung the long brown coat on the second hook, and the grey-green one over it, and the hook, which had hung bare through every winter she could have named, took the weight and held it.

She took off her own coat and hung it on the first.

She did not know where to put her hands. It was a foolish thing to find out at her age, and she found it out in the middle of her own workroom, in the red light of the stove, with the thaw loud in the gutters. She had said once, on the ice, that she did not know where hands went after a measuring. She was finding out.

She put them flat on the cream wool at his chest. The warmth came up into her palms as it comes up out of a hank lifted hot from the wash, and kept coming. Beneath the wool she felt him draw one long breath and hold it.

"Ansel."

"Yes."

"Breathe out."

He did, and it shook, and she felt it through the wool all the way down her arms.

He bent his head. He did not take her mouth first. He put his mouth to her throat, in the place under the jaw where the rust wool stopped and the skin began, and his breath went warm against her there, and every cold winter she owned came out of her at once in one long unsteady sigh. The linen tape slipped from beneath her scarf and fell to the boards. Neither of them looked down. For the first time in her life nobody in the room was being measured.

She had not taken off the grey scarf, and he had not asked her to. She found that she did not need it gone to be warm.

Her hands went under the hem. She slid them beneath the cream and the Green, and under the Green to his shirt, and under the shirt to the skin of his back, and she had never touched anything so unpolished and so alive. The warmth of the jumper lay on the outside of him, and his own warmth lay beneath it, and her palms were between the two, in the one place in the world where both were true. His ribs moved under her fingers. He said her name against her neck, once, and it was neither a joke nor a question. At his left side her hand found the gap in the seam, and the heat came through it into her palm and lay there, open and steady, a door that had been waiting to be walked through. She left her hand where it was. She felt him shake, and she held on, and the shaking went quiet under her palm.

His hands came up into her hair and then to her face, as they had on the ice, but they were warm now through and through and by no lent wool, and he turned her mouth up to his, and this time she did not wait to be asked. She had stopped once, in this room, with her mouth still wet from his and her hands still on him. She did not stop now. The kiss was long and slow and went on past every place she had ever turned back from. The stove ticked. She was aware of a great many things at once, and of her own gauge going to pieces row by row, as it had never been allowed to, and she let it.

At last she took his hand. It was warm, and so was hers. She drew him back past the racks to the corner where the carded fleece lay piled soft and clean, as it had lain waiting through all of the autumn orders, and as she went by she put her heel to the workroom door. It swung shut on the shop and the lane and the whole bright thaw, and the latch dropped, and the sound it made was a very small sound.

The light had climbed the racks by the time she woke.

It came through the window above the long table in a pale slant and found the skeins first, and then the stove's grey bed, and then her. She lay without moving, as one does on the first morning of a thing one cannot yet believe, and took stock as she took stock of any piece. Her fingers, warm. Her feet, warm. Her throat, under the two scarves, warm. The long body along her back, and the arm across her, and the cream wool of the Open Door spread over the pair of them, which was not what it had been made for and did what it had been made for all the same. Her hand lay against the left seam. The gap under her palm was open to the air, and still warm.

The grey scarf at her jaw was cold still, exactly as cold as it had always been, and she found she did not mind it. It was only a scarf. The rust one had slipped in the night and lay along her collarbone, lopsided, giving off its plain steady heat, and she drew it straight with two fingers and left the hem to hang uneven.

She did not count any of it. She had counted everything else.

His breathing was slow and even against her shoulder. He slept the way he did everything, entirely. A drift of water ran in the gutter outside, and a gull walked the roof, and the stove ticked as it cooled.

Out on the water, a long way off, a note went up.

It was low and a little cracked, a note with the winter's rust still on it, and it rolled out across the opened lake and in at every window in Larkmere. The glass above the long table hummed with it. It was the sound the town had sat out the cold for, the first of the year: the ferry testing her whistle in the clean light before she cast off from the Net Sheds. She had heard it every spring of her life. She had never once felt it go all the way down her spine.

Ansel did not wake. His arm tightened across her and eased.

Across the open water, in the first clear light of the year, the Heron sounded again.
