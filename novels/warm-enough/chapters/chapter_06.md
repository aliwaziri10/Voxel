The Kettle Room kept to itself in the hour between dinner and the lamps. The long tables stood scrubbed and empty, the benches pushed square, the window steamed to the sill, and behind the counter Mina Cray was drying cups in a flour-white apron with her sleeves shoved above the elbow. She did not turn when the door opened. She had never needed to.

"Sit," she said. "You look like a skein left out in the rain."

Hester sat on the bench nearest the stove with her hands tucked under her arms and the scarf wound to her jaw. She had come down Spindle Lane through a frost so hard it rang underfoot. She had not slept, and she was aware of that the way she was aware of a loose end in a seam, a small thing that she meant to deal with and was dealing with by not looking.

Mina brought the chipped blue teapot and two cups and a bowl of soup that nobody had ordered, and put them down in front of her with the air of a woman laying a charge. Then she sat across the table with her arms folded on it. She was broad and plain and unhurried, and she had the stillest eyes in Larkmere, and she used them like a pair of shears.

"Well," she said. "Go on."

"Go on what."

"A whole jumper, in a night. You, who take your time over a plain pair of cuffs." Mina tipped her head toward the window and the lane beyond it. "Half of Spindle Lane watched your lamp burn till the birds started. By breakfast the whole shore knew it had gone onto his step before there was light to see it by."

"It's a Winter Knit. It's my work."

"Does it fit?"

"To a hair."

"So they say. Over four coats, in front of the whole school." Mina turned her cup on the table. "You measure well."

"I measure everyone well."

"You do," said Mina. "You always have. It's your work, and he has been going up and down the shore path in it telling everyone it's wonderful." Mina poured. The spout of the old blue pot had a chip out of the lip, and the tea came out of it in two threads, the way it always had. "I was at my own door when he went by this morning. He waved. He was grinning like a lamp. I would swear on my own oven I could see his breath inside the thing."

Hester said nothing. She took a spoonful of soup because the bowl was there and Mina was watching, and it was good, and it went down warm and left her exactly as it found her.

"You came in white to the knuckle," said Mina, and reached across, and folded Hester's cold hands round the cup of tea without asking leave. The china was hot. Hester held it. The heat came to the skin of her palms and stopped there, as heat always stopped for her, and Mina watched it stop, and said nothing about that either.

"And you've had your hand in that coat pocket," said Mina, "since you sat down."

It was true. Her right hand had gone down inside the coat to the length of grey folded double and was holding it, and she had not given it leave. She took the hand out and put it on the table and looked at it as though it belonged to a stranger who had followed her in.

"It's yarn."

"I know it's yarn. It's grey, and it's the one lump of anything you haven't put down since the Heron came in, and you went out to the quay that night with a face like a shut cupboard and came home with it. Don't think I don't hear. The whole quay told me about the ball rolling." Mina's voice stayed level. It never did anything else. "Since the Heron you have said the word schoolmaster more often than the word tea. For you that's an epic."

"I have not."

"The Onion, then. The children have him as the Onion. I think it's the best thing the town has come up with in years. It suits him. Every coat in the county and a gift for being cheerful." Mina leaned forward on her folded arms. "And you've given him a jumper to go under all of them that's cold as a church."

The spoon stopped halfway to Hester's mouth.

"It is exactly what it was meant to be."

"That," said Mina, "is what I'm afraid of."

"It is correct in every row. There is not a flaw in it, there is nothing the town can say against it, and he's wearing it. He will wear it all winter. That is what a Winter Knit is for." She heard her voice come up and did not stop it. "If the town wants a jumper to love, it can knit one for itself. I am not a hearth. I am not paid to be anyone's hearth."

"No one said you were."

"You said it just now."

"I said it was cold." Mina did not move. "That's a different sentence, and you know it, and you've gone red to the ears."

"Mind your teapot," said Hester, and stood. The bench scraped. She was aware of the bowl, still half full, and aware of the cup she had not touched, and of the hand that wanted to go back into her pocket and the other that would not let it. "Thank you for the soup."

"Hester."

She stopped at the door with the latch in her fist.

"I didn't say it to wound you," said Mina. "Eat a proper supper at home."

The cold met her in the lane and took hold of her at the knuckles. She walked up Spindle Lane with her chin sunk in the scarf and the lamps beginning to come on along the quay below her, one and then the next, small and yellow against the dusk. The harbor was grey with ice. Beyond it the Ice Road stood marked out across the lake by its poles, and she did not look at the end of it where the school stood on its shore with one window lit.

The workroom was warm and smelled of lanolin. She hung her coat on the first hook. The second held nothing, which was its habit. The coat swung once and hung there with its right pocket low, and Hester stood with her back to it and her hands open at her sides, and told them to be still.

Gran was on her stool by the stove with the cane across her knees and the copper kettle ticking behind her. She had been there a good while. The tea at her elbow had a skin on it. She said nothing when Hester came in, and went on saying it, and Hester crossed to the long table and began to square a stack of cards of mending wool that had been square already.

"He wore it to school," Gran said at last.

"I know."

"The Brook child says he told the whole room it was wonderful."

"He tells everyone everything is wonderful. It's a condition."

"Mm." Gran took up the cup, found the skin on the tea, and put it down again. The lamp found the hole at the edge of her shawl as she settled, dark against the grey wool, and Hester's eyes went to it and away, as they always did. She had offered to mend it once, a long time ago, and had been refused in a tone that closed the subject for good. "A flawless knit sits like a shell, Hester. Nothing goes in. Nothing comes out. It's a lovely thing to look at, and no one has ever been warm in one."

"I know what you are going to say."

"Then there's no need for me to say it."

"You are going to say it."

Gran turned her head, slowly, and looked at her granddaughter across the length of the room with eyes that had seen a great many winters and had not been fooled by one of them.

"Leave one door open," she said.

It was quiet in the workroom. A coal settled in the stove. Hester set the cards of mending wool down on the table, one on top of the other, and squared them with both hands.

"That is a rule," she said, "for knitters who want to be wasted on a stranger."

Gran looked at her a moment longer. Then she took the cane, and gathered her shawl, and got to her feet in three stages, hands and knees and the rest of her. "Is it," she said, and went to the loft stair, and climbed. The treads took her weight one at a time. The door closed above, gently, as though the house were being asked to keep a confidence.

Hester stood alone in the workroom with the stove ticking and the whole long day pressing in at the back of her eyes. She did not go to the stove to bank it. She went to the racks.

The blue hank hung where it had hung since the summer she dyed it, on the top pole at the far end, out of the reach of anyone who was not tall. She had never found a use for it. It was a deep blue, nearly black in the lamplight and as true as dye gets by day, and she had thought when she dyed it that it was the only color she had ever made that she liked. She did not ask herself why her hand had gone up for it. She took it down and set it on the table and wound it into a ball with quick, hard, even turns, and when it was wound she stood a moment with the ball in her palm, looking at nothing.

The Grey's last hank stood on the rack by the window where she had set it aside. She had not wanted it in the room.

She would do a cable. She would do the best cable Larkmere had ever seen, a rope worked down the front, a twist on either side of it, every crossing sharp and every strand lying in its place. It would be a jumper to make a knitter's eyes ache. She would cast on the broad, true foundation of it tonight and see how it went. She did not take the tape from round her neck to check the width of the front. She had it already, the breadth of his chest and the length of his arm to the thumb bone and the width of his shoulders, which she had not wanted to know and now could not lose. She did not ask herself whom it was for. She had a rule about asking.

The stitches went onto the needle, and the first rows of the ribbing came away from her hands as crisp as a new selvedge, and she was pleased with them, and then she was past being pleased. The rope began to rise. She crossed it, and crossed it again, and thought of nothing but the next crossing. The lamp burned. The stove ticked. She thought, and she would have sworn it to anyone, of nothing.

It was in the middle of a crossing, down where thought does not go, that she felt a pulse. It was nobody's pulse. It was slow and level, and then one stroke came early and the rest hurried after it. She felt it in the needle, and the needle was in her hands, and she looked down.

The row on her needles was warm.

It was not the warmth of the stove, or of her own hands, which were never warm. It was the warmth of a good cuff, set into the wool the way twist is set into yarn, and it lay across her fingers and would not be argued with. It came up through her fingertips and into the first joints of them, and the white went out of the ends, and for a moment she did not take her hands off it. She wanted not to. That was the thing she could not look at afterward.

She took the needle in her fist and drew it out.

The stitches came off all at once, a row's worth of them slipping loose and shivering, and she pulled the yarn through them, hard, and then again, back along the row and the one beneath, her hands going faster than she could follow. It came away in long crinkled loops that sprang up from the blue and curled over the scrubbed boards. She went on until the cable was gone and the foundation was gone and there was nothing left on the needle but the memory of it, and then she sat with the empty needle in her hand and her breath coming short.

She was afraid. She could not have said of what. She could have told anyone the gauge of every row in the room, and the twist of every ply, and she could not have told them that.

One jumper was out on the shore path on a man who called it wonderful to anyone who passed. The other had lasted a few rows on her needles and she had torn it out. She had made a thing she was afraid of being cold, and begun a thing she was afraid of being warm, and she did not know which fear was the larger or why she had two.

The ripped yarn lay curled on the table in the lamplight, blue as the dye pot, in loops that would not lie down. She laid her hand flat beside it, and then on it. It was faintly warm.
