The girl came into the workroom holding a flat tin out in front of her in both hands, with her elbows locked and her eyes on the far end of it, the way a child holds a skein for winding.

Hester did not look up from the rack. She had a length of grey lambswool across her thumb and was drawing it through, slowly, feeling for the thin place, and she finished the draw before she said, "You were sent for blankets. The lamps were lit an hour ago."

"I went for the blankets."

"Then where are they?"

"In the chest. I left them in the chest." Tilda came farther in. Her tongue was between her teeth, and she took it out to speak and put it back. She had been crying at a point on the way, or had been cold, or both, and her nose was red at the tip, like a thumb pressed hard on a seam. "I didn't mean to look. I want that said first. The chest is at the foot of his bed, and I had to lift the pillow to get my arm round the corner of the lid. And it was there. It's not as if I went hunting."

"What is?"

The girl brought the tin the last three steps and held it out across the bench.

It was a plain flat tin a hand wide, dented at one corner as though a heavy thing had come down on it, with the paint worn through to the metal where a thumb went. The lid was scratched pale along the edge. Hester set the lambswool down. She did not take the tin at once. A bad feeling had come up in her at the sight of it, out of the same low place under the ribs where Mina's sentence had gone in, and she stood with her cold hands flat on the bench on either side of her work and looked at the thing in the child's hands as one looks at a dropped stitch three rows below the needle.

It had been a poor afternoon, and she had not yet finished counting it. She had stood in the schoolroom with her hands open and said that she was afraid, which she had not said to anyone, and she had said keep one thing, which she had not known she meant until it was out in the air. He had stood an inch from her and said there was nothing he could keep. Then she had walked home through slush with every row of the Green in her head, lent out to a stranger's shoulders, and she had come into this room and taken down the grey lambswool because her hands wanted a task and would take any.

"You opened it."

"I shut it again." The words came in a rush. "It isn't stealing if you bring it back, and I couldn't bring it back to him, could I, he'd have laughed, he'd have said it was nothing, and it isn't nothing, there's wool in it. It's your wool. Isn't it? I thought it was. It's got your first row on it." She stopped and swallowed. "That's a beginner's first row. Nobody does it like that except a person who's only just learned."

"Give it to me."

The tin was lighter than she had expected and, along the bottom, warm.

She noticed that before anything else. She had thought it would be cold, tin and iron and the thaw standing in the walls, and the metal had in it the faint steady warmth of a stone that has lain all day in the sun. It came up through her fingers. It was not much. It was the warmth of a thing that had been kept a long time against a body and had not quite let go of the habit.

Overhead, in the loft, a cane struck the boards once.

She did not look up. She knew who was on the stair. She had known for a minute, by the stopping of a floorboard that had been creaking, and she had said nothing about it, and Gran had said nothing back. Hester turned the tin over in her hands, and found the lid with her thumbnail, and lifted it.

A scrap lay at the bottom, folded once, no bigger than the palm of a child's hand. Undyed grey. The honest grey of a fleece before anyone has had an opinion about it. She lifted it out between two fingers and it opened as it left the tin, the way a leaf opens, and she saw the edge.

The first row was twisted.

She would have known it in the dark. It was a cast-on in which every stitch had been put onto the needle the wrong way about, so that the base of the piece ran in a little corkscrew ridge instead of a clean chain. Every knitter did it who had ever learned. It was the mistake of the first week. A girl made it once, was shown it once, and never made it again for as long as she lived, and Hester had made it exactly once. She could see the place where she had known she was wrong and gone on regardless. She could see the small tight knot at the join where an angry hand had pulled the yarn harder than it needed. It was her own mistake, with her whole signature on it, and it was the winter she was twelve.

She remembered nothing of giving it.

She tried. She stood in the lamplight with the scrap spread across her flat palm and went back through the winters as she went back through a garment for a fault, row by row, looking for the place. There was a quay in it, and cold, the early cold with a ferry coming in, and a great many people with their backs to her. She could not find a boy. She could not find her own arm going out. It was a blank, a stretch of fabric knit in plain stitch and then pulled flat, and the harder she looked the smoother it ran.

But she remembered how it had felt to make it.

That came up in her instead of the other, and it came all at once, like a breath held too long and let go. She felt the hours of it. She felt a child's hunched back, and a lamp set too low, and needles too long for her hands, and a great fierce small wish that went into every loop. It was only that. It was the wish that a thing she made should be enough. She had not known, at twelve, what she was wishing it for, and she had not cared. She had pushed it into the grey with her thumbs, row on row, as hard as she could, and the scrap lay on her palm now, as old as every winter since, and it was warm.

It was warm as a fleece is warm at the end of a long afternoon of sun. Faint, and steady, and quite undiminished.

Wool holds the honest feeling of the hands that made it. She had told a hundred customers so, in a voice she kept for the purpose, and she had never once stood in a room and felt it hold. The wool had waited all this while. It had lain in a tin under a man's pillow in a stone cottage through a whole winter of weather and kept a child's meaning at the heat of a body, as faithfully as the day she cast off, and nobody had told it that the child was gone.

"Is it yours?" said Tilda.

"It is a mistake of mine."

"I know that. But is it yours?"

"Yes."

"He had it under his pillow." The girl's voice had gone very small. "He puts his hand on that tin when he thinks we're all looking at slates. He keeps it by him like a mitten. Why would he keep it by him?"

Hester closed her hand.

The scrap went into the dark of her fist, and the warmth of it spread into the heel of her palm and the root of her thumb, the first place in her whole hand to be warm without any help from another body. She stood very still and let it. Then she opened her fingers, and folded the scrap in half and in half again exactly as it had been folded, and laid it in the tin, and pressed the lid down with her thumbnail until it clicked.

"Go and sleep," she said.

"But"

"I will see to it. Go on. Go home to your own bed and say nothing to anyone."

"I never say anything."

"You say everything."

"Only afterward," said Tilda, with dignity, and then her face crumpled, and she went at the door. She went backward at first, watching Hester's face, and then she turned and ran, and the door banged on the lane.

Hester stood with the tin in her hands.

"Take it back to him yourself," said Gran, from the top of the stair.

She had not heard her come down a step. She turned, and the old woman was there in the dark of the stairhead, a small bent shape in a shawl with one hole in it, her two hands on the ash cane and the lamplight finding the tip of her nose. She said no more. She looked at the tin, and she looked at Hester, and the whole of her long hard face said that she had known where it was for a good while and had been waiting for a child to be nosy.

"It is not mine to return."

"Then it will be a pleasure to be told so." The cane tapped once. "Go on, girl. He will be at his slates."

She went.

The night was clear and soft and loud with water. The thaw had come in properly while she sat at the bench, and every roof along Spindle Lane dripped into the dark in a hundred different notes, and the snow in the lane had gone to grey slush that took her boots to the ankle. Down on the lake the ice spoke. It was a long deep note with a crack at the end of it like a thread parting, and the sound came up through the soles of her feet and was gone. She went along the shore road with the tin inside her coat against her ribs. It lay there the whole way, and it was warm, and she felt it at every step the way one feels a skein held tight under the arm.

Twice she stopped. The first time was at the turn of the road, where she took the tin out and put her thumb on the lid to see whether it was still warm, and it was, and she put it back. The second time was on the school steps. She stood on the topmost of the three with her hand lifted to knock. The window beside the door was lit, and through it, between two desks, she could see the master's table and the man at it.

He was sitting in his coat with his back to the board and a pile of children's slates at his elbow. He had a stub of chalk in his fingers and was not using it. He was not doing anything at all. She had watched him for a winter, and she had never seen him do nothing. He sat with his shoulders drawn in and his eyes on the table, and as she looked, his right hand went up to the left side of his chest and pressed there, flat, once, and came down again.

She did not knock. She opened the door, and the stove ticked, and the long room lay in its brown light with the desks in rows. When he looked up, his face went still by degrees, as a lamp goes low when the wick is turned.

He was wearing the Grey. She saw that first. She saw the collar of it above the coat, the flawless undyed neck, and under his eyes the color she had hoped not to see again.

She crossed the room and set the tin on the table between the slates.

He did not touch it. He looked at it. He looked at the dent in the corner and the scratched lid, and then he looked up at her, and she saw him not say any of the things that were ready in his mouth. It was the strangest sight she had seen in him yet, the laugh standing up and being refused.

"Tilda brought it to me," said Hester. "She found it when she went for the blankets. She did not take it from you. She asked me to say that."

"I see."

"I opened it."

"I thought you might have."

"I know this row," she said.

She opened the lid. She took out the scrap and laid it across her palm in the lamplight, where he could see the little corkscrew of the first row and the clean plain rows above it, and the grey with the lamp in it, and she held it out to him. She did not push it at him. She held it at the length of her arm and let him see it whole.

"I made this."

"Yes."

"It is from the winter I was twelve. I do not remember giving it to you." She heard her own voice, flat and level and dry, and under it the other thing, the long unspooling. "I remember nothing about you. I have looked. I stood in my own workroom for an hour trying to find a boy on the quay and he is not there. I can tell you the weight of the needles. I can tell you what I wished while I made it. I cannot tell you who it was for."

He stood. He stood slowly, with the table at his hip, and his hands came down to his sides and stayed there, loose, open, the palms turned a little forward, the hands of a man who has put down everything he was carrying and is waiting to learn what happens next. She had never seen them still. She had only ever seen them doing, moving a chalk or a cup or a coat, passing it on. They hung beside him now with the fingers a little curled, empty, like two things he did not know how to use.

"I was on your quay for one winter," said Ansel. "When I was a boy. The cold boy, the one nobody could warm. A girl came down to the water with this in her fist and put it in my hand. She did not say a word. She went back up the quay." He drew a breath, and it shook a little at the top. "I was very cold, Hester. And then, for a while, I was not."

The thaw dripped on the roof. The ice spoke out on the lake, long and low.

"You give away everything," she said. It was hardly more than a breath. "I asked you this afternoon to keep one thing. And you stood there and said there was nothing."

"I know."

"And it was in a tin under your pillow."

The scrap lay on her palm between them, warm, in the lamp. His hands were open at his sides. He was looking at it, and not at her, with a face she had no name for in any trade she knew.

"I have never given it away," said Ansel.
