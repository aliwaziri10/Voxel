By the second week after Longnight, Ansel knew to the inch how much of him was warm, and it was the width of a scarf.

The scarf sat at his throat, lumpy and blue, with the great fist of a knot under his chin that he had tried to undo on the first morning and had since given up on. The children stared at it. The smallest girl had asked him in front of the whole class whether it was true that he slept in it, and he had said that he had been advised by the Law of the Land to do so. The rest of him was as it had always been. The lake still stood in his legs and the cold still sat in the long bones, and the Grey lay on his ribs like a sheet of frost, level and exact. But the band of warmth at his neck was a fact, and he had learned to live in it as a man lives in a house with one lit room, doing the whole business of the day near the door of it.

He stood with his back to the stove at the end of the afternoon, which was a habit of the winter, and rubbed the chalk from his hands, and Tilda Brook did not go home.

She had been given the broom. She held it upright in front of her, a good head taller than she was, with her chin on the top of the handle and her patched boots planted, and she had not swept a single board. She was looking at him with the stillness of a girl who has been working up to a question for a week.

"It's only warm at the neck," she said. "Isn't it?"

"I beg your pardon?"

"The scarf. You're only warm where it is." She nodded at the stove behind him. "You keep standing with your back to that. People who are warm all over don't."

"You are very observant." He laughed first, as he always did, and then said, since there was nobody in the room to hear him, "Yes. It is a very good neck. It has never been so well looked after."

"That's because it's only a scarf."

She said it as one tells a very slow infant the day of the week. She put the broom against the wall, which was a relief to the wall, and came and stood in front of him with her hands behind her back, and he understood that he was about to be taught. He had seen that stance in a good many staff rooms. He folded his arms and gave her his full attention.

"Wool keeps what the knitter felt," said Tilda. "That's the first thing. It isn't magic, it's just how it is. If you knit cross, it comes out cross. If you knit sad it goes all soggy, like a hat left out in the rain. And if you knit and you *mean* it," she hunted for the word and abandoned it, "it comes out warm. It doesn't matter if you're good. Mine's not good. It matters if you mean."

"I see," said Ansel, who did not, quite, but felt it for the first time on the very edge of seeing. "And Hester's?"

"Hester's are the best in the world. Everybody says. But she does them very, very *correct*." She gave the word its full weight, scorn and reverence together. "I asked her once why they were like that, and she said gauge."

"Gauge."

"I don't know what that is either."

He laughed, and it came out less bright than he had meant. Tilda considered him and went on.

"The second thing is you've got to let it in. You can't pour tea in a cup with the lid on. If you hold it off, it just sits on the outside and it's wasted." She looked at him sternly. "You let mine in."

"I did."

"I saw."

"I had no choice in the matter."

"Everybody's got a choice," said Tilda. "That's why it's the second thing."

She held up three fingers, which was the number she had got to, and looked at them as if checking them against a list. "And the third is it stays. Whatever it was when it was made, that's what it is. It doesn't get better and it doesn't get worse. Mine will be warm when I'm old, even if it gets more holes. And the Grey will be." She stopped, and her mouth went small. "Well. It'll be what it is."

"Yes," said Ansel quietly. "I rather think it will."

"And the cold's not yours," said Tilda, in a rush, as if to get past the Grey as quickly as possible. "Everybody off the ferry gets it. It's the lake's. A fire doesn't touch it. Coats don't. Only wool does, and only if it's true." She drew breath. "So you need a whole one. A scarf's only a scarf. You need a jumper, a proper one, that a person meant. All the way round."

"My dear Tilda." He bent a little to be on her level, as a master does, and found that his voice was not entirely under his hand. "I could not ask anyone to do such a thing. It would be a very great imposition. Nobody has the time."

"I didn't ask you if I could," said Tilda.

There was no answer to that, and he knew it, and so did she. She took up the broom again, and looked at the floor, and put one stroke across it for form's sake. At the door she turned. "Don't tell Hester I told you the rules. She says they're not to be said. They're to be understood." She considered this. "I don't understand them any better that way."

She was gone before he had thought of a joke.

He walked home by the shore path in the last light with the scarf warm at his throat and his hands in his armpits, and he did the sums as he walked. He had done them on the back of a slate every quarter-day since the Heron. They were not hard sums. They had one answer.

He was the eldest. That was the whole of the arithmetic. He was the eldest of a family in a river town a long way down the water, in a house where the money had always come from whoever stood oldest, and he had been the oldest for a great deal longer than he had been a man. A river runs one way. A child learns early which direction the asking goes in such a house, and it goes downstream, never up, and the one at the top of the stream does the carrying. He sent nearly all of it home. He kept enough for coal and a loaf and the chalk the school did not supply, and the rest went into a folded paper at every quarter-day, to cross the ice in the carter's bag, and what came back across it in the other direction was a letter, in a round hand, that said everything was well.

He had not asked anyone for anything since he could remember. He did not know how it was done. It was a language he had never been taught.

The cottage was cold. He lit the stove, and stood in front of it, and the heat touched his hands and his face and stopped there. The wages lay on the table in their small brown envelope where he had left them, counted, with the sum for home already set apart. He looked at the smaller pile that was left. It would do for coal until the thaw. It would not do for a doctor.

That was the shape of the winter, and he had known it from the first. A master who took to his bed lost the week's pay. A master who took to his bed twice lost the post. He could stand a cold. He had stood one since the quay. A fever was another matter, a fever that put him on his back for a month, and the paper that crossed the ice in the carter's bag would go thin, and nobody at the other end would say a single word about it, and that would be the worst part. He could not afford to be ill. He had worked it out on a slate in the dark of the first week and had not once let himself look at it since.

He put his hand flat on his chest, through the Grey and the three layers, to the place where the letter lay folded small. The first one. The one with his name across the outside in a round, careful, child's hand. It had been there since the Heron, and it was unopened. He knew whose hand it was. He had not once let himself say whose. He turned the letter against his ribs, once, through all that cloth, and took his hand away.

He did not sleep.

He lay on the bed in the Grey and the scarf with the stove ticking down, and the cold came up from the floor into his bones in its slow tide, and the warmth at his throat lay against his pulse like a small lit lamp in a very large dark house. He lay a long time. At midnight, or near it, he got up, and put on the brown coat and the grey-green one and the one with the missing button, and went out.

The lane was white and entirely still. He had walked it a great many nights since the first frost, with a nail in his pocket and a lantern, and he did not take either now. The poles at the head of the lane stood in their rings of frozen gravel with the lit glass on top, throwing small yellow rooms of light on the snow. The town was shuttered and asleep. His breath went up in front of him, white, and he walked in it down Spindle Lane without any purpose he could have given, past the gate that no longer shrieked, to the house at the end.

There was a light in the window.

It was a low lamp, turned down to a bead, and it laid a long amber square on the snow outside the glass. He stopped at the edge of the square. He had not meant to stop. He had meant to walk by, as he had walked by every night, and go home to his bed and his sums. But the glass was clear in the middle where the frost had not taken it, a round clean pane the size of a plate, and he saw her through it.

She sat at the long table with her back half turned to the window and the lamp at her elbow. She was knitting.

He had watched her knit by day, in the first week, the needles flying and her eyes hard on the work, the clean slate of her face gone to chalk with concentration. This was another thing. Her eyes were closed. Her hands went on without them, the two needles lifting and dipping in a slow even rhythm, as a sleeper's breath goes on, and a length of deep blue wool ran from the ball in her lap through her fingers. It was the blue of good ink. He saw that, and did not understand why he had seen it, and saw it again.

And her face.

It was not the face he knew. The mouth was not a stroke of chalk. It had softened, quite loose and a little parted, as a child's does when it is wholly taken up with a thing it loves and has forgotten that anyone could see. The line between her brows was gone. The grey scarf had slipped from her jaw, and the lamp laid a thin gold edge along the cheek and the throat. She looked years younger. She looked entirely unguarded, like a house with every door left open to the weather, and she did not know it, and he stood in the snow outside her window and was the only man in the world who did.

He did not move.

He had gone to a great many doors in his life. He had gone to them with letters and messages and baskets, with a bag on his shoulder and a joke in his mouth, to hand a thing over and be thanked and be gone. He had never gone to one to ask. He did not know the words. He stood at the edge of the amber light with the cold coming up through the soles of his boots and the Grey lying on his ribs and the scarf warm at his throat, and a dozen things came up in him to say, and every one of them was a joke. He could think of nothing to say that was not a joke. The jokes came in order, neat and bright, as they had come on the Heron, and one by one they would not do.

He wanted to knock. It was as plain as a sum. He wanted to cross the snow and put his hand to the wood and have her open it, and see her face change. He wanted it more than he had wanted anything since the quay.

The needles went on. A length of blue lay across her knee.

He crossed the lit square of the window, because it was the only way to the door, and the snow squeaked under him, and he held his breath. He stood on the step. The latch, which he had not touched, was an arm's length away. Through the wood he could hear nothing at all, but he knew what was on the other side of it, and that it was warm, and what it looked like.

He raised his hand to the door, and held it there, and did not knock.
