The last coal had not gone out.

She had thought it would. She had watched it draw in on itself in the iron, a single red eye no bigger than a thumbnail, and had written it off the way a knitter writes off a stitch that has slipped the needle, as gone and merely not yet noticed. But it held. It lay in the dark of the stove like a loop kept on a pin, small and red and refusing to be let go, and it gave out just enough light to show her the near half of the hut.

Outside, the wind had changed its mind. It no longer threw itself at the door. It had gone round to the shutter on the far wall and settled there, and what came through the boards now was the softer sound of snow being laid against them in layers, one on another, with a patient hiss, like felt worked between two palms.

Beside her, Ansel Brandt had stopped keeping watch.

She knew it before she let herself know it. A man who is keeping watch can be heard keeping it, in the held breath and the careful stillness of a body arranging not to move, and she had listened to that arrangement beside her for a long while. Then the held breath had begun to let go. It let go a little at a time, as the tension goes out of a piece that has been blocked and left to dry, and the shoulder against hers, which had been as hard as a plank since the door, was slowly not.

He went in pieces. First the breath, which lengthened. Then the hand on his knee, which lost its grip on the cloth. Then, at the last, the head. It came down on her shoulder with the whole slack astonished weight of it, and stayed.

She did not move.

She was very good at not moving. She had sat out a great many nights at the long table with a row going wrong in her hands and had not moved for those either, and she sat now as she would have sat with a hank of fine lace across her knees, waiting for it to settle and see what it meant to do. His temple lay against the point of her shoulder. His hair, which smelled of cold air and chalk dust and a little of peat smoke, had come up under her jaw. She could feel the shape of his skull through it. It was heavier than she would have guessed. It was the weight of a man who had been holding his head up on purpose for a very long time.

The scarf had ridden up to her jaw, as it always did. His breath found the grey wool and went through it, damp and slow, and then his weight shifted on the breath and the wool at her throat slid, and the next one came on bare skin under her ear. Once. Then again. She felt it in her back teeth.

She kept her eyes on the coal.

He had not slept all winter. She was certain of that, as she was certain of a skein dyed in a hurry, by the unevenness of it: the grey under his eyes, the quick bright jokes that tripped on their own heels, a man living off the top of his own breath. The cold did that. It did not let a man down into sleep. It kept him standing at the surface of it all night with his coats buttoned and his chalk in his pocket, and called that rest. She did not know what had changed. It might have been the stove, or the dark, or the sheer length of the night, or the warm that had lain along his side. She knew only that the cold had not left him. She could feel it still, a long even chill in the arm that lay against hers, deep in the bone where no coat reached. But it had company, and he slept.

She waited until she was sure of it. Then she waited a little longer, because sure and certain were different gauges.

His hand had come off his knee.

She had felt it go. It lay now on the cloth of the blanket between them with the fingers curled loosely, and her own hand lay an inch from it, on her knee, where it had lain since the stove went down. She looked at the inch. Nothing in the hut asked her to cross it. It was only a hand, asleep, going nowhere, and what an asleep hand asks is not a request.

A long while passed. The snow went on laying itself against the boards. Then, with nothing in it to say that it was meant, his hand turned over and opened, palm up, and lay against her knuckles.

It was warm.

It was always warm. That was the thing about him she had not been able to put a name to for the whole of the winter, a man who shook with the whole lake in his bones and whose hands, at the end of every arm, were warm to the wrist. She had put her fist into the front of his coat on a roof, in the dark before the light, and her knuckles had been warm through three layers of cloth. She had opened the hand and gone down the ladder and told herself it was the shovel.

She laid her fingers across his palm.

It took her no more than a breath, and it cost her the rest of the night. His fingers closed on them. They did it slowly, in sleep, without a word, the way a loop closes when the yarn is drawn through, and then they were laced, his and hers, palm against palm under the brown blanket, and his thumb lay along the side of her hand and did not stir. Her fingers had been white to the second joint since the door. She felt the color come back into them from the palm outward, the old ache of it, as a hank that has been left stiff in cold water comes up pliant when it is lowered into warm. She sat very still and let it.

Level breath on her neck. A weight on her shoulder. A hand.

She had her other hand free, and it did not know what to do with itself.

It wanted work. It had always wanted work, and a night in a stone hut with nothing to put in it was a hunger of its own. She moved it by degrees, an inch at a time, inside the folds of her coat, to the place under her ribs where the roll of cloth had ridden all day. She drew it out. She did it as one draws a pin from the hem of a sleeping child. The roll came free and lay in her lap under the blanket, and she unrolled it on her knee, and in the red from the iron she saw the moss green.

It was the right sleeve. It was the last piece of the Green, a tube of seed stitch on four needles, finished to a hand short of the cuff. The color in that light was nearly black, a green with a good deal of brown in it, and the little knots of the pattern stood up along the fabric like a field of seed after a frost. She had worked every other piece of it. The body lay in her workroom under its cloth. The left sleeve was done, with its cuff, and the left cuff had come out warm, which she had not told anyone. The hem was cool, because the hem had been knit first, politely, before she knew what she was making.

She tucked the one needle she was not working hard under her arm, against her ribs, and took the other in her right hand. She had never knit one-handed. She did it now. It was slow and clumsy, and she would have been ashamed to be seen at it, and the stitches came out even.

The needle clicked.

It was a very small sound. It went up into the low roof and the dark, and she thought it would wake him. It did not. He breathed on, long and slow and ordinary, and the click went on, and between them, under the blanket, his hand lay in hers and was warm.

She knit a row.

She did not tell her hands to mean it. She noticed, partway along, that she had not needed to. The row came off the needle warm, as the rows had come for a good while, and she lifted the sleeve with the free hand and laid the new stitches against the pulse under her jaw, where the scarf had slipped, and held them there. It was warm. It lay against her throat with the same slow certainty as the breath that came there from the other side. She found that she was holding hers.

She counted back.

She did it as she counted back through a piece to find a fault, row by row, down through the seed stitch and the long hem to the first place where the wool had changed. She had always said it was the workroom. The first row she had meant had been in the workroom, in daylight, with her eyes open and a bell on the ice, and she had put it into the record of herself with a date and a reason. But the wool did not keep her record. It kept its own, and it said that she was wrong. The first warm row had not been that one. It had been further back. It had been a ladder in the dark, and a man on his knees on her roof with the lantern shaking and chalk at both cuffs, and her fist in his coat, and her own knuckles going warm against his breastbone while her mind stood on the pitch of the tiles and gave the cold the blame.

She had gone home from that roof and sat down at the table. Every row since had been his.

It was no use saying she had not known. She had known like a person knows the next stitch, below the level of words. She had called it a cold hand, and a polite hem, and a sleeve for a man who needed one, and she had gone on meaning it row after row for the whole of the winter and kept the accounts as though the sum were different. The wool had been honest. It had gone on being warm in her hands the entire time, and been patient with her.

She looked at the green in her lap.

It was warm, and it would stay warm. That was the whole of the trouble. Wool did not learn and it did not forgive and it did not cool, and whatever she put into it tonight it would hold for forty winters, on a hook, in a loft, on a pair of shoulders going down a road. It would hold this. The hut, and the stove light, and his hand. It would go on meaning every stitch of it long after the person who knit it had given up the argument. She had known that all her life, and had always counted it against the craft. She counted it now, and for the first time it did not come out the same.

There were not many rows left.

She knew exactly how many, and did not say the number. When the cuff was bound off the Green would stop being wool on needles and become a jumper, and a jumper was a thing for a body, and a thing for a body was a thing to be given. She would have to hold it out. She would have to stand in front of him in a lit room with it over both her arms and let the whole of its meaning lie across the cloth for anyone with a palm to read, and wait, and not know. She could not keep it. It had been cut to his shoulders and no one else's. She had been working toward that moment with her eyes open for a month, and she had managed not to look up.

She could make the sleeve last. She could take the rows slowly, one a night, and leave the cuff unbound on the table for the rest of the winter, an unfinished thing that nobody had to be handed.

She knit the next row slowly. Then she noticed that she was knitting it slowly, and she made the one after it at her ordinary speed, and meant that one as well. If it was to be finished it would be finished warm. She had never made a polite thing since the first row, whatever she had told herself, and it was too late to start tonight.

Warm things went. She knew that too. A warm thing was put on, and what was put on walked out of doors on a pair of shoulders, and the shoulders did not ask the knitter where she would like it to go. It had been the first lesson of her life and the last one she had wished to learn. She sat with the needle in her hand and the weight on her shoulder and the knowledge of it, plainly, as one sits with a bill. It did not make the row cold. She put her thumb against the stitches and they were warm.

The needle clicked, and clicked.

The coal went on holding in the iron. The snow settled on the shutter in a long slow hush, and the light from the stove moved a little on the stones as the coal breathed, and she did not look up. His hair lay against her jaw. His breath came and went at the bare place under her ear, long and even and unguarded, and she let it, and worked her row.

He spoke in his sleep.

It was only a sound, at first, without consonants in it, low and warm against her collar. Then a word came up out of it, half a word, blurred at the edges. "Fine," said Ansel, to the dark, with a ghost of the laugh in front of it.

She did not answer. She did not stop the needle. She turned the sleeve on her knee and began the next row, and under the blanket the hand that held hers tightened, once, slowly, as a loop draws up when the yarn is pulled through, and did not let go.

The needle went on clicking in the low red light.
