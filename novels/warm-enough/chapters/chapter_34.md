He came down Spindle Lane without a coat.

Hester saw it from the Kettle Room window with a cup of Mina's tea going cold between her hands, and for the length of a breath she took him for another man. He walked down the middle of the lane in the wet noon light with his arms swinging, in the Green and nothing else. The brown coat was gone. The grey-green one was gone. The collar stood open at his throat and the chalk lay white at both cuffs, and the thaw let go of every eave in the lane and dripped on his bare head, and he did not hunch from it. He walked as a man walks who has been let out.

"Well," said Mina, behind the counter, to the cup she was drying. "There's the Onion."

Hester said nothing. She had not said much since the ice. She had come down to the Kettle Room because a woman who has not eaten properly in two days is a nuisance to her friends, and because her own workroom had begun to feel like a room with a stitch missing, which was a thing she did not intend to examine. There was a bowl of soup in front of her that she had not touched. The warmth of the room stopped at her skin, as it always did.

Outside, the lane had begun to notice. A woman on her step with a basin of washing stopped with the basin on her hip. A man mending a gate put his hammer down. Two doors along a shutter went up that had no business going up at noon, and a face appeared behind it. Nobody called out. A town that has waited the whole of a winter for a thing to happen does not startle it by calling out. They only stood where they were, one after another down the length of Spindle Lane, and watched him go by in the one jumper, and Hester saw each face take the news and pass it to the next, a loop slipped and picked up, slipped and picked up, all the way to the quay.

He reached the door. He stopped with his hand on it and looked through the glass at her, and his whole face did what it had done on the ice. It opened. She felt it as a pull on a thread low in her ribs.

Then he came in, and the room went silent.

It went all at once, as a bench of needles stops when the knitter at its head lays hers down. Four tables of people with their spoons in the air. The baker's wife with a loaf half out of its cloth. Not one of them looked at Hester. Every one of them looked at him, at the Green, at the open collar, at the bare plain cuffs, and then, with great care, at their soup. Hester was aware of her own face. She laid it flat, as she would a seam that wanted pressing.

"Good noon," said Ansel, to the room. He drew breath to laugh, and she watched him decide against it, and he only smiled, which was worse. "I am told there is soup."

"You are told right," said Mina. She had not stopped drying the cup.

A shriek came from the end bench.

Tilda Brook was standing on it. She had come up from the school at the dinner hour with her slate under her arm and a bowl of broth in her fist, and she stood on the bench with the broth slopping and her blue scarf trailing to the boards and her face alight. "No coats!" said Tilda. "Not one! It is before the Breaking. I said sooner. Mina. Mina, I said sooner."

"You said by the Breaking," said Mina, "and I said you were a child with a hopeful nature."

"I said sooner!"

"You did." Mina set the cup down at last. She looked at the schoolmaster, and at Hester, and at the child on the bench, with the expression of a woman who has been told the price of a thing and finds it fair. She went through the door behind the counter and came back with a plain square cake under a cloth, and put it on the end table with the air of a woman paying a debt in full. "A whole one. I want it noted I was ahead on the clock. I did not allow for the schoolmaster being a fool for a woman."

"Mina," said Hester.

"Or the woman," said Mina, and went back to her cups.

Ansel had gone the color of madder dye and was laughing, openly now, helplessly, with one hand over his mouth, and the room let its breath go and began, very softly, to talk. It talked round them the way a ply winds round its core. Nobody looked at the table in the corner. Everybody knew where it was.

He sat down across from her. The bench was narrow, and the table was narrower, and his boot came to rest against hers under it and stayed. She did not take hers away.

"You are not shaking," said Mina, putting soup in front of him with a hard clap. She said it flatly, as one reads off a measure.

"I'm told it's the soup."

"It is not the soup."

Hester looked at him. She did it with her thumb, in her mind, the way she would have drawn a length of new yarn across it, and she knew what she would find before she found it. The cold was still there. It stood in him as it had stood in him all winter, the lake's own cold, in the deep of the bones. But it had drawn in at the edges. The white was gone from his lips. His cheeks had a color. The tips of his fingers, round the spoon, were the ordinary pink of a man's, and the lines at the corners of his eyes, which had been cut deeper every week since the Heron, had eased. The toll was not lifted. It was lifting, as a frost lifts from a field, from the margins in, and the margins were where she had put her mouth.

She laid her spoon down. It rang on the china, and Tilda looked round from her cake.

"Hester has gone red," Tilda told the room.

"Hester has soup," said Hester. "Sit down. Tilda. Sit."

Tilda sat. She sat with her tongue caught between her teeth, cutting the cake into portions with the care of a girl measuring out a row. She cut one for Mina. Mina took it without a word and ate it standing, looking at nobody.

They did not stay long. It was not possible to stay. Every time Hester raised her eyes a head turned away from her, and she understood that the room had been looking at her the whole of the meal and had only been quick about stopping. When he rose and said that he would see the knitter home, because it was the thaw and the lane was running, the whole Kettle Room said nothing at once.

The lane was worse.

It had a doorway every ten paces, and in every doorway there was a neighbor. A woman was sweeping a step that Hester had watched her sweep the morning before. A man on a ladder was mending a gutter that had not leaked. A boy sat on a wall with a slate on his knee and wrote on it nothing at all. "Good noon, Winter Knitter!" called a woman, with a bright wet eye. "Fine thaw!" called her husband, behind her. "A fine thaw, truly," said Ansel gravely, to each in turn, and bowed, and walked on at her side with his hands clasped behind his back like a buyer walking a line of fleeces, and Hester walked with her chin in her scarf and her cold hands in her sleeves and wanted to lie down in a snowbank.

She wanted him in daylight. It was a plain, ridiculous want. She wanted his mouth in the full open noon, with the thaw coming down on both of them and no one's eye in it, and she wanted it so hard that her hands shook in her sleeves, and she could not have it. The whole lane stood in a ring of doorways like pins in a cushion. He walked an arm's length from her. The back of his hand brushed hers once, through her sleeve, and she felt it in her teeth. Neither of them looked down. A woman at a window waved a dishcloth.

And under it, shut, where she did not go, lay the page. She had not told him. She had carried the line about the knitter in her head since the morning Gran's bent finger lay on it, and she had put it away in a drawer in her, folded small. It was a thing about her. It was not a thing to put in his hands with the soup. She held it shut as she held her own cold fingers shut in her sleeves.

At the Spindle House she put her hand on the latch, and found it open.

The workroom was empty. The stool by the stove stood bare, and the loft door above was shut. The racks were full. It was the thaw, and she had dyed through the dead of the winter to keep her hands busy, and the long poles hung heavy with it, rows and rows of skeins from the door to the far wall, cream and fawn and a deep brown and a clear leaf green she had not been able to look at for a long while. The lamp was not lit. The light came grey and soft through the damp window, and lay across the hanging wool in long pale bars, and it smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke and the clean wet fleece of the end of the winter.

She stood in the door and was aware, with every inch of her, of the man who stood behind her in it.

"Hester," said Ansel.

She did not answer. She walked in, down the long aisle between the first row of poles and the second, and the skeins parted round her and closed behind her with a long dry rustle, and she knew the instant she was in among them that nobody in Larkmere could see her. She turned round. He had come in after her. He stood in the aisle in the soft light with the wool hanging on either side of him like a curtain, and he looked at her as he had looked on the ice.

"There is a neighbor," she said, "at every door in the lane."

"There is not one in here."

"There may be."

"I should be very surprised." He took a step. "I should be very surprised, Hester, and very annoyed."

She did not remember crossing the last of it. She remembered his hands. They came to her back, the two of them, flat, warm, one at the small of it and one between her shoulder blades, and drew her in, and she went against him with the whole of herself. He was warm. It was a thing she would have given a year for, a plain animal fact. The Green was warm along every inch where she pressed to it, from his shoulders to his waist, and under it the long hard length of him was warm too, in the hands and the throat and the mouth, and only a little, only just, cold at the heart. He bent his head. She stood on her toes. His mouth came down on hers among the hanging wool.

It was not slow. There had been no room left in either of them for slow. She had her hands in the open collar of the Green and the warm skin under it, the pulse in his throat beating against her knuckles as quick as a shuttle, and his mouth was open on hers, and his hands moved on her back as if he were trying to learn it, up the spine, down, the whole of the long muscle of it under her coat, and she made a sound she had not known she owned. Her back met a pole. The wool swung with it. A hank of fawn brushed her cheek and fell away, and a cream one came down over her shoulder and lay across the two of them, damp and sweet and heavy, and she turned her face into it and into him together, and the lamp-dark of the room was the color of the inside of a fleece.

His thigh came between hers. She felt it through her skirt, the plain solid strength of it, and the bar of the pole against her back, and the whole workroom turned on its spindle. He said her name against her mouth. He said it again against her throat. His lips found the pulse under the edge of the linen tape and stayed there, and her head went back against the pole, and the skeins on either side of them swung out and in, out and in, slow as a drawn breath.

"Hester." It was very low. "Say if I should stop."

"No," said Hester. "Do not."

A neighbor knocked at the door.

It was three raps, bright and neighborly, the knock of a woman with a basket and a perfectly good reason. They went through the workroom like a needle through a loose weave. Hester's eyes opened. His mouth had left her throat. His hands were still at her back, spread flat, and his chest was going against hers, and neither of them moved at all.

"Winter Knitter!" called a voice, through the wood. "I've brought a pie for the schoolmaster!"

All round them the wool went on swinging, cream and fawn and brown, slow and long, and did not stop.
