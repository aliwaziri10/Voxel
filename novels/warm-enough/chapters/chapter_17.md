The bell came across the ice at the dead end of the morning lesson, and Ansel lost the room in one stroke.

He had known he would. He had written the long division of the ferry fares on the board, and every head in the room was bent to it, and then the sound went over the roof, small and flat and brass, and every head came up at once, like a field of oats turned by a wind. The smallest girl was already on her feet. There is no master living who can hold a room against a sledge. He put the chalk down on the ledge, said that any child who was not at the quay in the time it took to say a grace might consider the sums adjourned, and was left talking to the stove.

He went after them in every coat he owned. The shore path was packed hard and ringing, and the cold stood in his legs as it always did, a lake's worth of it from the knee down, but his neck was warm. It was the width of a scarf and it was entirely his. He ran as far as the first lantern pole, found that his breath would not go the whole distance, and walked the rest with his hand pressed flat on his ribs, where the letter lay, the one he had carried since the Heron and not opened.

The children scattered at the quay as water does at the foot of a weir, back to their mothers and their doorways, and he stood on the stones at the head of the Ice Road with the whole white lake in front of him.

It was midwinter light, low and level and the color of a pale lemon, and it laid the shadow of every lantern pole across the ice in a long blue line. Far out, past the thirtieth pole, where the road bent to the north shore, a dark shape was growing. He could hear the bell now without trying. It rang, and rang, and the shape put out two thin dark lines behind it that were the runners and a smaller darker shape that was the driver, bent forward like a man reading.

"It is the post," he said aloud, to nobody. "A sovereign power. One does not argue."

"It is a sledge," said Hester Tansley, at his elbow.

He did not jump. He laughed first, because he always did, and then turned his head, and she was there. She had come down the lane with her coat buttoned to the throat and her grey scarf pulled up to the point of her jaw, and her hands were bare, as they always were, red and white at the knuckle. She stood a good pace off from him with her eyes on the ice. Behind her came Gran, planting the pale cane at every step as if she were driving a stake in, and Tilda Brook ahead of them both, who had plainly run the length of the shore to fetch them, with her lumpy blue scarf flying out behind her like a signal.

"It brings the letters," said Hester, "and the bill for the coal."

"It is a great deal more than that. It is the whole of the outside world on one runner." He looked at her hands and put his own into his armpits, out of a habit he could not account for. "I let the school out. I am told the technical term is surrender."

"The technical term is being outvoted by a sledge." She did not turn her head. The corner of her mouth moved, and it was not a smile, but it was a near neighbor of one, and it was gone before he could take hold of it.

He stayed as he was, with his shoulder an inch from hers, and found that he could feel where she stood as one feels a stove in a dark room. It was nothing. It was the length of a sleeve. A wind came off the lake and went through the three coats as if they were paper, and he saw her bare knuckles go from red to white on the end of her arm.

He had the outer coat half unbuttoned before he knew he meant to. It was the long brown one, the best of the three, and it was already coming off his shoulder toward her, because a thing that is wanted is a thing to be given, and he had never in his life been able to see a cold hand and keep his own coat on.

"No," said Hester.

She said it very low, without looking round. His hand stopped on the button. She did not move away from him, and she did not take the coat, and for one breath they stood so, her bare fingers a hand's width from the brown cloth, and he could hear her breathe. Then she put her hands into her sleeves, quite deliberately, as one closes a book, and he did up the button again with great care, and neither of them said a word about it. He had stood in front of a room of children for a good many years without once having to think what to do with his hands.

The bell filled the whole shore. The sledge came in along the last of the poles with the team blowing and steaming and the runners hissing on the packed snow, and the driver hauled up so hard at the quay stones that the whole load slewed and stopped with a jingle. He was a man wrapped in so many blankets that he looked like a parcel of them, with a face at the top of them. He did not get down. He rummaged in the bag at his feet, and held out one hand to the quay, and in it was a letter.

He held it out to Ansel. He said nothing. He had apparently been told whom to hand it to.

Ansel took it. It was thin, and cold from the ride, and soft at the edges where it had lain against the bag in the dark, and his name was written across the front of it in a round, careful, child's hand. It was the same hand. He had known it before he had it fully in his fingers, as one knows a voice in the next room. It was his sister's hand. He turned it over once. The back was sealed.

The winter post was the only road. It was the sole thing that crossed the ice in either direction between now and the thaw, and everything he had and everything he owed rode on its back. He had a paper in his pocket that would go down the water in this same bag, and a letter in this hand that had come up it, and between the two lay the whole length of the river, and not a word of it was ever in the letter.

He did not open it. He put it into the inner pocket of the brown coat with the other, so that the two of them lay folded together against his ribs, one with the seal broken by nobody, one with the seal whole, and he felt them there like a pair of cold coins. He said, brightly, to the driver, "Thank you. Splendid weather for it," and the driver looked at him as if he had said it in a foreign tongue.

Then the blankets at the back of the sledge moved, and a man stood up out of them.

He was tall and slightly stooped, as a man gets who has spent years bent over a table, and he stepped down onto the quay stones with the care of a man whose legs have been folded a long time. He wore a jumper under his open coat. It was grey-blue, worn nearly to a shine at the elbows, and the cuffs were frayed and darkened with ink, and so were his fingers, up to the second joint, the stains gone right into the grain of the skin. It had been a good jumper a long time ago. It hung on him as a thing hangs that has been lived in for years and not once been argued with.

He was not shivering. Ansel noticed it first of all things, because he noticed it in everyone now. The man stood on the open stones of the quay in a midwinter wind with his coat flapping and his jumper thin, and he did not hunch, or stamp, or draw his arms in, and he looked round him at the town and the lake with an expression of great and easy pleasure, as a man looks at a room he had not expected to be glad of. Ansel looked at the thin elbows and the open collar and thought that the man was either a fool or the luckiest creature on the lake, and could not decide which, and was ashamed at once of the thought.

He had a pleasant face. That was the unfairness. It was open, brown from a sun that was not Larkmere's, with lines at the eyes that had come from laughing, and a mouth that had a rueful tilt to one corner, as though it had been apologizing to people for years and had never meant a word of it any less. Ansel liked him at once. It happened in the first instant, and there was nothing to be done about it. He had liked a great many people at first sight, and been right about nearly all of them.

"Passenger for Larkmere," said the driver, to the quay in general, with no more weight than he had put on the bag. "Piers Ellory."

Nobody moved.

It was not that anybody stopped. They had all been standing still already. But a thing went out of the stillness as a lantern goes out of a window, and what was left was another kind, and he knew it as a master knows a room the instant a door has opened that should have stayed shut. Tilda had turned right round on her heel with her mouth open. Gran's cane, which had been driven into the snow at every step, had stopped halfway and did not come down. And beside him, an inch from his sleeve, where he had been feeling her like a stove in the dark, Hester had gone white.

He saw it happen. There was no mistaking it and he did not try. It was not the pallor of cold, which she wore every day of the winter in her knuckles and her ears. It went up out of her collar and across her face from the throat, as color leaves a slate that has been wiped, and left her eyes very dark and perfectly level. She did not gasp. She did not take a step. She stood with her hands drawn up into her sleeves, and the whole of her went still in the way of a thing that has taken a great weight and has not yet decided which way to fall.

"Who's that?" said Tilda, loudly, to the sky.

"Hush," said Gran.

He knew he should look away. He did not. He stood with his sister's letter against his ribs and watched a stranger look at Hester Tansley across a few yards of trodden snow, and he did what he had always done when a thing was too large for him, which was to find the generous way of holding it. He thought: she knows him. He thought: this is a man who matters to her, and I have liked him for ten breaths together, and so I shall be glad. I shall be wholly glad. I will be glad for her if it kills me.

He set about it with every resource he had, and it would not hold.

It sat low in his chest, a good deal lower than the letters, a cold small weight like a stone dropped in the bed of a shallow river that turns the whole water round it. It did not shift when he breathed. He tried a joke on it, privately, the best he had, and the joke went down into the water and was not seen again. His hands, which had been in his armpits for warmth, had gone perfectly quiet. He took them out and let them hang. He thought of putting a hand under her elbow, and did not, since she had said no to him once already that morning and he had not yet found out what the word had been guarding. He heard his own pulse in his ears, under the one warm band of the scarf. He did not step toward her, and he did not step back, because both were a decision, and he found that he did not have one in him. He was a master of a roomful of children and one stove, and he stood on a quay in every coat he owned and was glad with all his might and was nothing of the sort.

Piers Ellory came up the stones with the easy stride of a man who had never doubted the road. He stopped three paces from her. He smiled, and it was the best smile Ansel had seen on any man, wide and tired and warm, and rueful at the corner, and he said her name as a ferryman calls a landing that he has not made in many winters and finds unaltered.

"Hester."

He lifted one hand in greeting, ink to the second joint, the palm open and the fingers a little spread.

And beside Ansel the white-knuckled fingers of her right hand came out of her sleeve, slowly, and closed round the grey wool of the scarf at her throat.
