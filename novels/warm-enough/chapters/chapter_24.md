The rope was old hemp, stiff with frost, and it went round her waist in a knot she had not tied. She had checked it twice anyway. It would hold.

Ansel stood at the other end of it with the empty pack frame on his back and every coat he owned buttoned over the one beneath. His breath came out in short white bursts. His lips had gone the grey of undyed wool, and he grinned through them as though a stranger had handed him a holiday.

"Perfectly warm," he said. "I want that written down."

"It is not written down."

"Then it did not happen, and I am fine." He laughed first and answered after, which was how he did everything. "Lead on. I will carry whatever you put on me. I have been wanting to be useful since breakfast."

She led on.

The slope above Larkmere went up in long white rows, each drift laid over the one before it with the hard even ridge of garter, and the wind had pulled the whole fabric tight. Her boots broke the top of it at every step. Below them the lake lay flat and blind under its crust of ice, the Ice Road a dotted seam down the middle, and the lantern poles so small from here that she could have picked them up between finger and thumb.

She was going for fleece. Not carded wool, not spun, not anything a shop would sell: the raw stuff, straight off the sheep, with the grease still in it. The cold snap had emptied the town. Every skein worth the name had gone onto a back in the long night at the Spindle House, and what was left on her shelves was tidy and nothing else. At High Fold the hill farmer kept old ewes whose wool came off the color of fresh cream. She wanted that. She had wanted it since the snap broke, stubbornly and without words, the way she wanted the next row on a piece whose shape she had not yet been told, and she had said nothing about it to anyone because she could not have said why.

The rope tugged at her waist. She stopped and did not look round.

"Slower," said Ansel, cheerfully, from further down. "Not for me. I only wish to admire the view."

"There is no view. It is snow."

"It is a great deal of snow. I find I admire it."

His teeth were rattling. She could hear them through the wind, a small dry clicking like needles in a bag. It was the toll, and it was no use to say so: the whole lake in his bones, indoors and out, and fire and coats had never once touched it. He had walked up a mountain in it and made jokes. She shortened her stride to his. She told herself it was for the rope, and the rope took no notice.

He had stopped joking a good while ago. She noticed it as she noticed a dropped stitch, late, by a change in the fabric: the climb had gone quiet behind her, with only the rattle of his teeth and the scrape of the frame, and the quiet was worse than anything he could have said. She turned round. He was far down the slope, white to the eyebrows, and he lifted a hand and waved it with a flourish, and she turned back.

The way steepened toward the top. The drifts gave out and the ground under them turned to a tilted shelf of rock with a crust of ice on it, and the path ran along it like a pulled thread.

"I will go first here," said Ansel.

"You will not."

"I weigh more than you do. With the coats, I weigh a great deal more. I will break the trail and you may say clever things at my back." He did not wait for the answer. He went past her on the narrow ledge with his shoulder turned to the rock, and the rope between them drew out and went taut, and she followed him with her eyes on the grey wool of his coat and her mind on her feet.

Her left boot went at the third step.

It did not slide. It simply was not there, the ice taking it out from under her without a sound, and she went down on one knee with the whole of the slope pulling at her. The rope snapped tight. It yanked her forward and up, hard, across the ledge and into the back of him, and he said "Oh," in a voice with no joke in it at all, and his arm swung round and his hand closed on her elbow.

He held her there.

She had her face against the rough wool between his shoulders. It smelled of chalk dust and cold and a little of pine. She could feel the quick hard going of his breath through all the coats, and she could feel that he was not shivering. For this much of a moment he was not. His hand on her elbow held with an even tension.

"I have you," said Ansel.

"I know you have me. I have the other end."

"Then we are well matched." He laughed, shakily, and the laugh came through his back into her cheek. "I should say I did that on purpose, but you would inspect my knots."

She stood up. She took her elbow out of his hand with the care of a woman drawing a thread from a seam, and she did not look at him, and she said, "Go first, then," and her voice was exactly as even as she had meant it to be.

It was the only thing that was.

Dunstan Rudd's house crouched under the lip of the hill with its turf roof pulled down over its ears. Smoke leaned from the chimney and went flat in the wind. The door was already open when they came up, and in it stood a very large, very grey man in a coat that had been mended more often than it had been made, looking at them without surprise.

"Dunstan," said Hester.

"Hm," said Dunstan.

"Good morning!" said Ansel, and swept off his hat, which had ice in the brim. "What a fine day. Cold, of course, but handsome. A very handsome cold."

Dunstan looked at him. He looked at the pack frame, and the coats, and the grey lips. "Sit," he said.

The house was one room, and the fire in it was a peat fire, low and red and giving off a heat she could feel on her face and nowhere else. Ansel went to the hearth and put his hands out to it and stood very close, and she watched him do it and watched the fire do nothing. He turned his hands over. He gave the room a bright, apologetic look. A ewe lay by the settle with her legs folded under her and regarded him with flat yellow eyes.

"I want the cream fleece," said Hester. "The old ewes. Last shearing."

"Shed."

"All of it?"

"Most."

"What do you want for it?"

Dunstan thought about this for a long while. The fire ticked. "Spring," he said, and that was the price, and she knew better than to ask for more of it.

The shed stood against the back of the house, low and cold and thick with the smell of grease. The fleeces lay in their sacks along one wall, piled to the rafters, each one rolled up on itself with the cut side out. She slit the first sack with her thumbnail and drew out a lock.

It was heavy. It was damp with its own oil. It was the color of cream and of old bone, and when she drew it across the pad of her thumb the way she drew every yarn she had ever bought, it did not catch. It slid. It left a slick film on her skin, and her fingers, that had been cold since the autumn, since the first frost, since as long as she could honestly recall, went warm.

"Warm," she said.

She had not meant to say it aloud.

Ansel was in the doorway with the sky white behind him. He had stopped laughing. "Is it?" he said.

"It is only grease." She said it flatly. It was the truth. No one's hands had been in this wool; it held nothing but the plain body heat of the animal that had worn it, the way a sleeve keeps the warmth of an arm for a little while after. Row by row, it had been nobody's. It would belong to a pair of hands as soon as she began. She turned the lock over, and the warmth sat in her fingertips.

"What will it be?"

"Wool."

He looked at her for a moment with his head a little on one side. Then he laughed, easily, and took the first sack in both arms and swung it onto the frame, and did not ask again. She was grateful. She would not have known what to put in the gap.

They filled the frame between them. He took more than he should, and she said so, and he said it weighed nothing, which was a lie so large and so cheerful that she let it stand. When the straps were buckled he straightened under it and his knees gave once, slightly, and came back. His face had gone grey again, and his hands, in their mittens, would not quite close on the straps.

She tied the rope round his waist herself. She did it with her own knots.

Dunstan took a sheepskin from the peg by the door. It was old and cracked at the seams and it smelled of the shed. He held it out to Ansel without a word.

"I could not possibly," said Ansel, and put his hands behind him, as though a gift were a thing that might bite. "You will want it. It is a great deal of weather up here."

"Wear."

"I have coats. I have all my coats."

"Wear." Dunstan did not lower the arm.

Hester watched Ansel look at the sheepskin, and at the farmer's face, and at the sheepskin again, with the expression of a man handed a bill he suspected he could not pay. Then he put it on over the coats. It held nothing but sheep. He fastened it to the throat with an air of enormous gratitude, as if it had been spun from gold, and Dunstan grunted and turned his face away.

At the door Dunstan stood with his face turned up. He had not said a word in a long while. The sky above the pasture had a look she knew. It had gone from white to the dull grey of old felt, flat and low, and the wind that had been pushing at her all morning had dropped as if a hand had been laid flat on it.

"Closing," said Dunstan.

"How long?"

"Go. Now."

They went.

The first of the descent was easy, a long soft slide through the drifts with the frame creaking behind him, and she thought they might do it. The pasture fell behind. The ledge came up under them, and the ice on it had thickened while they were inside, and she tested each foot before she trusted it. The rope ran slack between them, then tight, then slack. She had her left hand wrapped in it for the grip. Behind her the cream creaked on his back, and she thought of the jumper she would make of it, and where she would begin, and what she would leave out. She did not follow the thought to its end. She had not yet decided whether it was allowed.

It was her fingers that failed her. The grease from the fleece had gone into the weave of her glove, and the hemp slid through her palm as oil slides on a spindle, and at the last turn of the ledge her foot went and the rope ran out through her hand and did not stop.

She did not fall. His hand had her elbow before she knew she had gone.

He had come down two steps in a single stride with the full frame swinging on his back, and he held her up by the arm with one hand and the rock with the other, and the pack pressed them together from behind so that she could not have moved away if she had wished it. She did not wish it. That was the trouble. She stood quite still with the length of him along her side through all the layers, her slick hand flat against his chest where she had thrown it out, and she could feel his heart under the coats, going hard. He was breathing as though he had run.

Her hand had left a dark print on the sheepskin, a flat shining palm of grease. She looked at it.

"Hester," said Ansel.

"Do not."

"I was going to say that you are standing on my foot."

She stepped off it. He did not let go of her elbow. Neither of them moved.

Above them the cloud had come the rest of the way down. It came without a sound, a flat grey weight spread over High Fold from one edge of the pasture to the other, and the sheep on the slope lifted their heads as one animal and began to walk. The first flakes slanted past her cheek. The light went out of the snow. The whole white world drew in around the two of them until it was no wider than the rope, and above the pasture the sky closed like a lid.
