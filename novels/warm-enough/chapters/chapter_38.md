She finished the Open Door long after the house had gone quiet, with the lamp turned low and the stove open a hand's breadth, and she did not trust herself to look at it.

It lay across the long table with its arms out. She had not taken the tape to it once. She had knit it from the width of a man's back under her two palms on the ice, and from the length of a shoulder to a wrist as her fingers had learned it, hanging on to a collar. The sleeves were not quite twins. The left had run a shade long where her hands had hurried, and the neck sat a little low on one side, and she had looked at every one of these faults in turn through the nights since the third pole and let each of them stand. A Tansley piece had never gone out of the workroom with a fault in it. She could have mended any of them in the time it takes to boil a kettle.

It was warm. She did not need her thumb to know it. The warmth came off the wool across the width of the table, the way warmth comes off a fleece hung fresh from the wash over the stove, and it was in the collar and the shoulders and the long run of the body to the hem. She had put it there row by row, and it had not been polite once.

One thing was left.

She took up the finest needle and found the left side seam, high, where a man's arm would lift the wool when he reached for a shelf or a child or a latch. The stitch was a small cream loop among a great many like it. She set the point of the needle under it. Her hand, which had never in her life been unsteady over wool, was not steady now. The tape lay against her collarbone beneath the scarf, cool and ordinary, and she breathed out against it and lifted the needle.

The stitch let go.

It went the way a loop goes that has been waiting to be let. The loop slid from its row and the one below it parted, and then the next, and the ladder opened down through the body of the jumper, a gap no wider than a thread, running toward the hem. She did not touch it. It ran until it met the ribbing, and the ribbing took it and held it, exactly as she had known it would, and the work was done.

There it lay: a cream jumper with one deliberate dropped stitch at the left side seam.

She put her thumb into the ladder. The warmth came up to meet it. It did not rise to her as it did from the rest of the wool, steadily, from the whole cloth at once; it came to the gap, and through it, and out into the air of the workroom, and she felt it on her wrist and then in the crook of her elbow. It was a door, and it was open, and anyone at all could have found the fault in it. She had made it so that they could.

Her hands had begun to shake. She folded the jumper with the seam turned in, and then, because that was cowardice, she turned it out again. She wrapped it in the brown paper that lay in the shop drawer. She tied the string in a knot she would have unpicked from any apprentice's work, a lumpy, honest knot with the ends left long, and she looked at it, and she let it be.

She could not stay with it in the room. It was too warm there. The house was full of the heat of a finished garment and she could not get her breath past it.

Her coat hung on the first hook. She took it down and put it on over the scarf and the tape, and the parcel went under her arm and then, because it would not stay there, behind her. The second hook hung beside the first with nothing on it. She put out her free hand and laid it flat against the iron, the way one tests a skein for damp, and it was cold, and she took the hand away.

The loft above her was silent. The lamp she left burning.

Spindle Lane ran with water. The thaw had come down off the roofs in the dark and found every gutter, and the old snow lay in grey rags along the walls, giving way a little more with every breath of the night. She walked down it between the shuttered shops with her breath going before her. The lanterns on the quay burned small and gold, and they had not been lit by her tonight, and she did not know who had lit them. Past the Net Sheds the shore path ran out to the shingle, and the lake lay beyond it, wide and dark, and as she came out onto the stones the ice began to talk.

It groaned. It was a long low note, longer than the boom of the autumn or the crack of the first freezing, drawn out as a plied yarn is drawn when it has been twisted past what it will bear, and it went on and on beneath the whole lake before it let go. Then the quiet. Then it began again from the far side, and she felt it in the soles of her boots.

Everyone in Larkmere knew the ice would go at first light. Nobody had been told. The poles of the Ice Road had begun to lean in the afternoon, each a little off true, and the surface between them had gone from white to the grey of old felt, soft and pitted and dull. The Heron lay in her cradle in the Net Sheds with her doors wide, and past her the harbor water stood open at the shore edge, black and quite still. By morning the lake would be moving. By noon there would be open water to the far shore, and a ferry that could cross it, and a door in the whole season that had stood shut since the autumn.

She had made a jumper to go through that door. She stood on the stones with it hidden behind her and did not know how to hold it.

He was already on the shingle.

She saw him by the quay lamps, a little way along the shore where the school path came down, standing at the water's edge in his three coats with the lumpy blue scarf wound round his neck. He had his back half turned to the town. He stood very straight, like a row held taut between two needles, and his arms were behind him, both of them, with the shoulders held in a line she knew, because she had held her own that way for nine winters.

She meant to stop at an arm's length. She had a good eye for an arm's length and had taught him one on a planked quay in the thaw. Her feet took another measure. She walked down the shingle, and the stones turned and spoke under her, and he heard it and turned his head, and she did not stop until there was an inch of cold air between his coat front and her own.

It was a very small distance. She could feel every part of it. His breath went out white and hers went out to meet it, and the two of them hung together in the inch and did not mix.

"You are up," said Ansel.

"The ice is loud."

"It is." He did not laugh. She waited for it out of habit, as she waited for the click of a needle at the end of a row, and it did not come. His face was quite bare. It was worse than the laugh. "I could not sleep either. I marked the register, and sharpened a pencil until it was a splinter, and then I put the splinter on the stove and watched it go."

"Is that what you have behind you?"

"A splinter," he said gravely. "I am keeping it for the Breaking."

"I have nothing behind me."

"Neither have I," said Ansel.

Her mouth did not thin. She noticed that it did not. It stayed as it was, a little open, as it had stayed on the ice, and the cold came in at it.

He did not move his arms. She did not move hers. She stood with the string of the parcel bitten into the first two fingers of her right hand and the paper warm against her back through the coat, and she could feel, quite plainly, where his shoulder was in relation to her mouth, and what the width of two people's coats came to when set edge to edge. Her fingers ached with it. They were cold. They had always been cold, and the one warmth in the world that had ever reached them stood an inch from her with its hands hidden. They wanted his collar. They wanted the back of his neck where the hair lay flat. They held a piece of string.

Below his hip, where the long brown coat swung open, a length of wool hung out past his hand.

It was rust red. It was a bad red, a brick going to orange, and the edge of it ran crooked. It swelled and then narrowed over a hand's length like a row cast on in a high wind, and where it narrowed the stitches had been pulled in too hard, and just beyond that a loop had been left fat and open as though its maker had been told to loosen and had gone on loosening. She knew bad knitting at ten paces. She knew its faults the way she knew her own gauge. She had never, in all her winters, seen a faulty piece that she could not have named the faults of in one glance, and she looked at this one, and the faults came to her one after another, and she did not want to correct a single one of them.

The warmth reached her across the inch.

It came through the cold air like a palm laid on the inside of her wrist, and it was plain and steady, and it had been put into the wool by hands that were clumsy and slow and had not stopped. It was not hers. It could not have been. It was in the wool she could see, and in the air between them where his breath was, and her pulse knocked against her own collar like a stitch being forced through a gauge too fine. She knew what she was looking at. She knew it with the whole of her hands. She did not ask.

She stood with her own warm parcel at her back and his at his, and they were so close that if either of them had drawn a breath too deep the coats would have touched.

All her life she had handed things over. She had handed over a great many parcels, by the far edges, with her fingers well back and her face correct. A correct thing could be defended. It could be thanked and hung on a peg, and nothing further was owed on it, and the person who made it went home with her gauge intact. She had held out the charcoal in just that way and he had taken it in both hands and said it was perfect. It had been perfect. She had not had to be anything beyond that.

This one she could only mean.

It had no defense. It had a door in it that any hand could find, and it was warm, and it was hers, every stitch, from the collar to the place where the ribbing took the ladder. If she gave it, she gave it open. He could take it and thank her in the quay voice and fold it into his arms, and she would see him put it on and go on being cold in it, wearing it like a shell, and she would know what a thing she had made was worth against a man who passed kindness on before it landed. Or he could take it and let it in. She did not know which was worse. A gift that was refused could be borne. A gift that was received could not be gotten back.

Her arm did not move.

"Hester," said Ansel.

"Yes."

He did not go on. His throat moved, and in the dark she saw him take the sentence he had been about to say and set it down, slowly, as one lays a live stitch on a spare needle. The cold came off the lake between them. His hands stayed where they were. She could see by his shoulders what it was costing him, because she knew the posture, and she knew exactly how long a person can stand with an arm half raised before the muscle gives and the thing is dropped.

"The ice has to speak first," he said at last.

It was what she had told him on the quay with the Heron going into the water. He said it softly, in a voice with no joke under it, and she heard how carefully he had kept it.

"So I told you."

"I have been listening for it all night."

"It has been speaking all night."

"Yes," said Ansel. "I know. I mean the other part."

He looked at her. He did not look away, though she saw him think of it, and decide against it, and let the decision show. His eyes went to her mouth and then, with a visible effort, up again. The inch of air stood between them drawn as tight as a single thread, and she could have laid her thumb on it. She thought of the dropped stitch lying at the left seam of a jumper in brown paper. She thought of every row of it. She thought: now. Her elbow bent. The paper rustled against her spine.

Out over the water the ice gave one long groan, lower and longer than any of the others, rising from the whole width of the lake at once, a sound like the last turn of a cable pulled past all patience. It went through the shingle. It went up through the soles of her boots and into her knees and the long bones of her thighs, and she saw it go through him as well, a shudder that ran up the length of him inside the three coats and was gone.

Neither of them moved.

The lanterns burned along the quay. Their breath went up together and hung in the inch and thinned out into the dark. Behind her back the cream wool lay warm against her palms, and behind his, she knew now without being told, lay rust-colored wool, crooked, and warm. The ice groaned and went on groaning, and they stood with their hands hidden and the cold between them, and waited for it to break.
