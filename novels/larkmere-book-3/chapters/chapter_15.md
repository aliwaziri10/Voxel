The curl of oak lay at Tobiah's feet in one piece, and he stared at it as though another hand had made it.

Bryony did not praise him. She had been taught in this yard that a plank is praised by being used, and that a boy who is told he has done well will do it twice more and then do it carelessly. She bent, picked the shaving up, held it to the light so that he could see through the end of it, and dropped it on the pile.

"Again," she said.

The shingle behind her was empty. She had known it would be without turning her head. A man who can cross a beach unheard with a board on his shoulder can cross it unheard with nothing, and she let the sound of the lake fill the place where his boots should have been.

She showed Tobiah the vise first, since a plane can do no better than the board it is set to. She set the Fairlie board in the jaws with its edge to the sky and ran her palm along it from the near end to the far, and the wood lay down under her hand on the way out and lifted against it on the way back.

"That's the grain," she said. "Feel it? It runs along the board the way the current runs along a bank. Plane with it and the wood lies flat for you. Go against it and she tears, and you've lost a day to a hand's breadth."

He put his palm where hers had been. His fingers were stained brown at the tips with propolis, and wax lay under the nails. He ran it along the board once, out and back, and his mouth tightened.

"It catches coming back."

"Then you know which way to push."

He pushed. The plane ran a third of the way and chattered, and a ragged gouge opened in the edge where he had let the toe dip. His ears flared red. She waited for the whistle, because he whistled through his teeth when he was cornered, and none sounded, and she noticed that.

"Weight on the front at the start," she said, "and on the back at the finish. Your hands do nothing but steer. The rest is your shoulders."

"You said lean."

"I said lean. I didn't say fall on it."

The corner of his mouth twitched and was put away. He set the iron to the end of the board again and tried once more.

The sun sank lower in the open yard door. It laid the long shapes of the sawhorses across the floor, and her father's tools hung on their racks along the north wall in the order he had hung them, the spokeshaves by size and the chisels by width. The stove she had lit at noon to draw the damp out of the shed ticked as it cooled. Above the bench, on its peg, hung the adze. She had carried it back from the Sheds that morning and hung it where it had hung since before she could reach it. When Tobiah's glance climbed to it, she shook her head once.

"Not that one. Not for a year."

"I didn't ask."

"You were thinking of it."

"I was thinking it's heavy," he said, with dignity, and returned to his board.

They worked. The shavings left him in longer ribbons as the afternoon wore on, and then in shorter ones as his arms tired, and she circled him as she would have circled a hull on the stocks, correcting a wrist here and an elbow there. He let her. Once she set two fingers under his forearm to turn it a quarter inch, and he did not flinch, and she took the fingers away before he could.

She laid a straightedge along the finished edge and held the board up so the light shone under it. A thread of day showed in the middle, no wider than a hair.

"Hollow," she said. "You've dipped in the center."

"It's flat."

"Look under it."

He bent to the thread of light and was silent. Then he picked up the plane again without being told, and she had to turn away to the rack so that he should not see her face.

It was the same hand she had seen on the frames of the Heron, the same set of the head over the work. She had not expected that. She had come back to the yard braced for her father's tools, and had not thought to be ready for his habits in a boy's shoulders.

The iron wanted honing before the next pass. She showed him the oilstone on the bench, the angle held steady by the heel of the hand, the long strokes pushed edge first, the burr turned off the back on the second face. He did it badly, then less badly. When she told him to try the edge he drew the ball of his thumb across it, as she had, and a red line opened. He studied it with more interest than hurt.

"Now you know what sharp is," she said, and handed him the rag.

He wrapped it round the cut and returned to the stone one-handed, and the strokes ran longer than before.

He worked another hour. The light turned amber and then the color of old brass, and a wind rose off the lake with the smell of the blossom in it. When he straightened this time he did it slowly, and set the plane down on its side, as she had taught him, and stood with his hands hanging.

"Do you think I'm stupid?" he said.

She had a chisel in her hand. She laid it on the bench.

"No."

"For not reading them."

The lake slapped at the shingle beyond the door. Nowhere in the yard did a board creak.

"No," she said again.

"You wrote every month." He was studying the plane, not her. "I know you did. I know how many. I'm not stupid."

"I never thought so."

"I was ten." The words left him flat, one after another, as if he had been laying them in a row for a long time and was only now allowed to count them. "I was ten when you left, and Papa was in the ground by then, and the house was cold, and the yard was shut. You climbed the thaw road in the morning. I stood at the gate. Ewan walked down the Rise that evening with a lantern and took me up to the kitchen, and I've lived in it since."

She did not speak.

"He fed me. He taught me the hives, and when a bee stung me he scraped it out with his thumbnail, and when I couldn't sleep he sat up. He never made me say it. He never said a word against you, not one, and I listened for it." Tobiah's ears were burning now, and he let them. "He's the reason I'm not a stranger. To anyone, to the whole town. To myself, I think."

"Tobiah."

"I'm not saying it to hurt you. I'm saying it because you didn't know, and you ought to."

She had not known. That was the plain, ugly measure of it. In all the letters she had written, she had asked her brother after the weather and the bees and whether Ottoline's knees ached, and never once, in all those springs, had she asked who put him to bed. She had not asked because she had been afraid of the answer. The answer was a name, and she had known it all the while.

Ewan had raised her brother. Ewan, who had never written to the coast, whose silence she had read as anger and had been wrong about, because it had been work. He had been carrying the one person she had left behind her up the Rise every night, and he had never once sent word that it was safe.

Her chest ached with it, the way a hull aches that has been set on a cradle too short for her. Gratitude ran in her wider than she could measure. Under it ran the old guilt, quiet since the thaw road, and now it had a shape. Neither cancelled the other.

"Thank you for telling me," she said.

"Don't tell him I did."

"I won't."

He nodded once, shortly, picked up the plane, and turned his back to her, which was how Fairlies said whatever they could not say. She studied the set of his shoulders a moment and let him be.

"Keep the iron sharp," she said. "I'll be back before the light goes."

"You don't have to."

"I know."

At the door she glanced back. He had set the plane down again. He crouched at the pile of shavings, picked out the first curl, the long one she had held to the light, and slid it inside his shirt, and buttoned the shirt over it. She turned up the lane before he could catch her watching.

She did not take the tool roll. She wiped her hands on a rag, crossed the sill and climbed.

The Rise was warm at that hour. The lane rose between orchard walls with the blossom piling pale at the foot of each, and every few steps the wind lifted a handful of it and threw it over her boots. She did not run. She kept the even pace she used to walk a plank, and she tried the words on the way, as she would have tried a joint, saying them once to an orchard wall and listening for the ring.

"Thank you for my brother."

It rang hollow. He had not been hers to thank anyone for, not after she left him at the gate. She tried again.

"I owe you."

That one rang sound, and she knew he would not take it, because a debt is a claim, and he would sooner let the claim rot on the shelf than collect. If she stopped at the gate, the words would sink to the bottom of her with the rest, and the next spring would find them there, more awkward than before. By the time the gate of the hive yard stood in front of her, she had no words at all.

The latch clicked, the same click she had made in the thaw, and she remembered the sting in her wrist and his thumbnail laid flat beside the barb.

He was in the third row, with his back to her, bent over a hive with its lid in his hands. The yard hummed round him in the low evening note of foragers coming home heavy, and the light lay along the roofs in long yellow bars. He heard the latch and did not turn at once. He had never turned quickly toward a sound.

"Bryony," he said to the hive.

She stopped where the gate had left her, as every child raised in Larkmere stops, to be told what the bees would stand for.

"Sharp," he said. "Come through anyway."

"I've come from the yard."

"I know. I saw the smoke of your father's stove from here. It's been cold seven springs." He set the lid on the stand and straightened, and his eyes met hers, and then he searched her face. "Is he all right?"

"He's planing."

"Then he's all right."

"He dips the toe."

"He dips everything the first time," said Ewan. "He stops when it's been said twice."

"Ewan." She crossed the grass and stopped an arm's length off, nearer than she had stood to him since the Sheds. "He told me."

"Told you what?"

"What you did. Since I left."

His face changed by the width of a hair. It closed, the way a hull closes round a cargo, and for a moment she thought he would say that it was nothing. She had her answer ready for that. She did not need it.

"He's seventeen," Ewan said. "He talks."

"He said you're the reason he's not a stranger."

"He's not a stranger because he's a Fairlie and the town knows it. I only kept the kitchen warm."

"Don't." She heard her own voice go level, the voice she used to a plank that was lying to her about its thickness. "Don't make it smaller. I've spent the whole of my road making things smaller. I want to say it, and I want you to let me."

He stopped. He put the hive tool in his back pocket, which was what his hands did when they wanted to be rid of an excuse, and stood and waited.

She reached out and took his hand.

It was warm. The knuckles were scarred in a dozen places, white and pink and old, and wax lay under the nail of the thumb. She closed both of hers round it, her scarred hand and her whole one, and her thumb crossed the ridge of his knuckles and over the old stings, once, the way she would have run her thumb along a seam to find where it had been mended.

He stilled, entirely.

She read it in his hand before she saw it in his face, a stillness that began at the wrist and ran up the arm and left every muscle waiting. Under her thumb his pulse struck, and struck, and then slowed on purpose.

"Thank you," she said.

She had nothing more. She had thought there would be a speech, a long straight plank of one, laid fair from end to end. There was nothing but the two words and the hand.

"Tobiah raised himself," Ewan said. His voice had gone rough at the edges. "I only fed him."

"That isn't so."

"It's near enough."

"It isn't," she said, "and you know it, and I'm not letting go until you say so."

"The supers want weighing."

"Then they'll wait."

He did not say so. He did not take his hand away either. Behind them a hive hummed, low and steady, and then another answered it along the row, and she did not let go.
