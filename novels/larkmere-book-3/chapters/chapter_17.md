The plank held.

She eased the first cramp a quarter turn and waited for the oak to spring, as green wood will when it has been told what to be and has not agreed. It lay against the third frame as if it had grown there. She eased the second. The lap at the belly of the curve had closed to a line no wider than a pencil stroke, and a bead of Marrick wax had pushed out along it, pale as tallow, and stood in the groove.

Ewan waited at the stern end with his sleeves rolled to the elbow. Long before the green panel of the lantern paled on the seaward wall she had said "Now," and he had taken his hands off the board one finger at a time, like a man easing a wedge out of a joint that might still want it. He had not gone up the Rise since. He had swept the wet shavings into a heap beside the box and sat on the trestle with his boots crossed, and she had not asked why.

She cut a feeler from a cedar offcut, thin as a fingernail, and drew it down the lap from the stem to the third frame. It ran clear. No catch, no gap, no hair of light. She drew it down a second time, because a seam that passes once has only shown that it can.

"Fair," she said.

It was the word her father kept for work he would not praise any further, and she heard it leave her mouth in his flat voice.

"Is that the top of the scale?" Ewan asked.

"In this yard it was."

"Then she's had a better night than either of us."

She did not answer that. She did not need to, because the corner of her mouth had answered, and he had seen it, and she bent to the cramps.

She was winding the wet sacking off the box when the shingle began to talk. Boots, many of them, not in step, and under the boots a lid chattering on a pot. She straightened with the sacking in her fist and caught Ewan's glance. His face showed nothing, which in him was how surprise showed.

He tipped his head at the door. "The Sheds were lit all night. The quay will have seen it."

"And?"

"The quay talks."

She crossed to the landward door and opened it herself. The wind off Larkmere Water pushed in at her ankles with the blossom riding on it. Beyond the crowd the cloud Ewan had predicted would hold off had held off, and it lay along the Far Shore low and grey, the lake under it the color of pewter. The morning stood on the shingle in a crowd: quay folk with their caps off, neighbors from the lanes behind the harbor, and each of them carrying an offering. A kettle, black on the bottom. A loaf in a cloth. Offcuts of ash and elm under a woman's arm like firewood. A coil of tarred line. At the front, a tin of nails.

She knew the man holding it. On the quay at ice-out he had voiced what he thought of her, low, and had not troubled to see that the wind failed to carry it, and the wind had carried it. Today he studied his boots. The tin shook once in his hands, and he pushed it at her without a word, and she took it, because her hands knew what to do with a tin before she did.

The nails inside were of no two lengths. Rose-head and cut, wire and square, a lifetime of one man's drawer.

"It's hot," the woman with the kettle announced, and handed it to Ewan instead, since Bryony's arms were full. Wax lay under his thumbnails against the black iron. He thanked her with a nod, and she nodded back, and both of them were plainly glad it was over.

They filed in over the sill, wiping their boots on a floor of shavings, and stood round the Heron's cradle with the manners of people at a launching who have not been told which end is the bow. Nobody touched the hull. A man at the back told the man beside him, low, that she had bent that board with steam and no mallet. The woman with the elm laid it on the trestle with both hands, as one lays down a tool borrowed from a neighbor.

"It'll split," she warned.

"Elm splits if you glare at it," Bryony replied. "I'll find a use."

"She will," he told the kettle's steam. "She'll find three."

She had learned in her father's yard that a dry roof means hands. She had counted two pairs. She studied the faces round the cradle now and counted again. A hull that has had a plank drawn keeps the shape of the gap for a long while, and the water finds it every season. She had reckoned herself pulled out of this shore as clean as that, and the hull closed over. She had not reckoned that the hull would keep her shape.

Behind the crowd a stooped woman in a shawl pinned with a nail worked her way forward to the stem post. She stopped where the old keel showed its scarf beside the fresh wax, and laid two fingers on the oak, and drew them away.

"Your father would be proud," she said.

That was all of it. She delivered it flat, as a person remarks on weather that has held.

Bryony's throat closed as a seam closes on swollen timber, from the edges in. An answer was ready, the plain one she gave to everyone, and it did not come. She set the tin on the trestle with care, as she would set down a tool her hand could not be trusted with, and crossed the shed to the stem post and stood with her back to the room and a palm laid flat on the old keel. She counted the frames aft from the stem, and then counted them again.

Behind her he was thanking them. She could not make out the words, only the voice, low and level as slack water, and the shingle taking the town home one boot at a time, and the lid of the kettle beginning to chatter on the stove where Ewan had set it down.

When she had counted them twice her face was her own again.

The loaf was still warm. She cut it with a chisel, the sharpest edge in the shed. He studied the chisel and kept quiet, which cost him. He ate his half at the stove with the kettle in his fist and the steam on his chin.

"I should go up," he told her. "The foragers have been out since light and nobody has told them the plank's set."

"Go, then."

"After the kettle."

She tipped the tin out on the trestle. The heap slid and rang. She pulled a scrap of oak from the offcuts and drove a nail from each lot, three taps and a fourth, then levered each out with the claw of the hammer and ran her thumb down the shank for a bend or a bloom of rust. A quay drawer would hold what the lake had left it: nails the damp had eaten, nails already used and straightened once too often. She expected the town's castoffs and meant to put them where they would not matter.

Not one bent. The shanks ran true, the points were sharp, and a handful had been wiped with oil, lately, before they were put in the tin.

She stood with the last nail between two fingers. She had expected castoffs and been handed the best of the town, and she could not have said which she would have borne better.

"Well?" he asked.

"They'll hold."

"They're nails."

"They're oiled nails," she said, and found she had to turn to the stove.

The man with the tin had waited until the others were back on the shingle. He crossed the sill alone and held out an envelope without meeting her eyes.

"Post," he said. "Coast mark."

He was gone before she had taken it.

She knew the hand before she turned it over: square and black, every letter set down like a plank on the stocks, and none of them leaning. She slit the flap with her thumbnail and read it standing, with Ewan three steps off, busy with the kettle and not watching her at all.

"Bryony. I am coming up to see the Heron. Do not tidy the yard for me. I want a hand on her before I hear a word about her. Your bench stands as you left it, oiled. Keep her sound in the heel. Abel."

She read it twice, as she would check a measure before the saw, and a third time for the line about the bench, which was no longer than the others and carried more than all of them together. Abel Trent did not oil a bench he did not mean to use.

She folded the letter in half and in half again, down to the size of a shim, and found that her hands were less steady than the folding.

Her tool roll lay on the trestle by the cradle, oiled canvas with the leather thong coiled on top. She opened it far enough to reach the pocket at the back, behind the spokeshave, where the paring from the keel note lay beside Ottoline's letter, and slid Abel's in with them. The thong took two tries to knot. Ewan had not asked what the letter contained. He had not glanced up from the kettle since the door, and in him that was an answer to a question he was not putting.

She had not known she was cold until the wool settled on her shoulders.

He had crossed to the door without a sound she could hear. Her own coat hung on the nail beside his, dry from the stove and warm in the lining; he had passed it by and taken his. It closed round her smelling of smoke and wax and him, heavy as a wet sail, hanging to her knees. His hands settled the collar against her neck, and one of them stayed.

It rested at the back of her neck, the heel of it against the top of the spine and the thumb on bare skin below the skull, in the hollow where she carried a day at the drawknife. His thumb knew the place. Nobody had told it.

She did not lean back or step away. She held still, as a plank holds still in the vise while the glue takes. The Sheds floor was littered with the town's offcuts, and the skin under his thumb had gone warm, and she had been missed, and she had spent seven springs sure that a leaving made no sound at all.

"It's from the coast," she said.

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say the kettle's boiling dry." His thumb did not leave the hollow of her neck. "It can."
