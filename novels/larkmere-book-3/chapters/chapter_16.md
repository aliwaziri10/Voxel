She let go of his hand when the sun touched the orchard wall, and only because the plank would not wait.

"There's a board for the starboard garboard," she said, to the row of hives. "It wants steaming tonight, and the bend wants two pairs of hands. I can't hold it alone." Her jaw set round the next words. "I'm asking."

It was the first request she had made of him since the thaw. He answered yes before she had finished, and she nodded once, the way a person nods at a sky that has held, and walked ahead of him through the gate.

They walked down the lane in the long gold light with the blossom lying thick in the ruts. He kept a step behind. His right hand still held the shape of hers, and he left it open at his side, because closing it would have lost the shape.

At the end of the shingle she stopped at the yard door. It stood shut, a peg through the hasp. She lifted the peg and pushed the door wide on the empty floor, and he glanced over her shoulder at what a boy had left. The plane lay on its side on the bench. The iron was honed, the oilstone beside it still dark with water, and a folded rag held a brown stain the size of a thumbprint.

"He's gone up to the kitchen," Ewan said. "Ottoline will have caught him at the gate and fed him."

"I told him I'd be back before the light failed."

"You are back. He isn't here to see it."

She studied the rag a moment longer. Then she set the peg in the hasp again, and he watched her test it with her thumb.

The Net Sheds waited at the end of the strand with dusk gathering under their eaves. She entered by the landward door, and he heard the iron clang of a stove lid. He stayed on the shingle. The taper was in his coat where it always was. He lit the lantern as he lit it every dusk, with his back to the door and his body between the flame and the doorway, and the wick, trimmed that afternoon, took at the first touch. Green light spilled over the wet stones and the toes of his boots. He pinched out the taper and put it away.

"Ewan?" she called from inside.

"Coming."

She did not ask what he had been doing. He crossed the sill with his ears open for the question, and it did not come.

The shed smelled of cold oak and old pitch, and under them, newly, of iron warming. A deep box stood by the landward wall on two trestles, its lid sealed with strips of wet sacking, one end open over a squat stove with a copper boiler on top, and the stove was drawing already. Beside the Heron's cradle lay the plank, a single length of her father's oak, twice his own height and planed thin at the edges, with the garboard line drawn along it in pencil, once, and then once more beside it.

She hung her coat on the nail by the door and held out a palm for his, and he gave it, and his coat hung beside hers with the smoke in it. Her cheeks were flushed from the stove. He told himself that was all it was.

"It goes in the box now," she said. "It wants the length of a long supper in there, and no more. Too little and it cracks. Too long and it goes to rag."

They lifted it together and slid it into the box. She laid a test strip in after it, an offcut the width of two fingers, and sealed it with the sacking, slapping the last strip flat with her palm. She stepped away with her hands on her hips and studied the boiler and the seal all over again.

"I forgot the clamps," she said.

"Where are they?"

"In the loft. The wedge box, under the window." She was already studying the lid, where the first thread of steam had begun to lean out of the sacking. "I'll fetch them."

"You'll stay with the box," Ewan told her. "You know how long a long supper is. I don't."

She did not argue, which was her answer, and he took it up the stair.

The loft had kept the day's warmth under its roof, and the warmth smelled of her: oak dust, lamp oil, oiled canvas. The grate held grey ash. He had left the stove glowing once and walked down these boards with the glow behind him. A blanket sat folded square at the foot of the cot, corners meeting. Under the window, where a head could rest on the frame, he found it and lifted out four cramps, cold and heavy, their screws greased. He did not look at the window again.

By the time he reached the floor the boiler had begun to sing, low and rising, the note a hive takes up at dusk when the last foragers are in. Bryony knelt at the cradle with a lump of his wax warming in her fist, working a rope of it into the deep groove cut along the keel where the plank would lie. She pressed it home with the heel of her hand. He set the cramps on the trestle in a row and waited to be told what else he was for.

She did not turn. "Sleeves. Steam finds every cuff."

He rolled them to the elbow. When she did turn, her glance fell on his left forearm, where the old stings showed white as flecks of cappings, and she lifted it away at once, as from a hot lid. He let that pass. She wiped her hands on a rag and stood.

For a while neither spoke. The iron ticked as it warmed through. Wind found the eaves and tried the loose boards, and a seam of green light from the lantern outside lay between two planks of the seaward wall, thin as a blade, and shivered whenever the flame leaned.

"It'll hold off till morning," he remarked.

"What will?"

"The rain."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

She conceded it with a tilt of her head, and almost smiled at the boiler instead of at him. Then she broke an oat cake from the tin on the trestle and held out the larger half without looking round. It was dry and a day old, and he ate it standing, and neither of them remarked that it was the first either had eaten since morning.

When the boiler's note rose a half step and steadied, she laid the rag down. "Now."

He pried the sacking strips off the lid with the hive tool, one after another, and the lid lifted on a roll of steam that swallowed the green seam and spread it through the shed, so that for a breath neither of them had a face. The air filled with hot rain on cut oak, and under it ran a thin sweetness he knew from his own thumbnails. She hooked the test strip out with the poker and bent it round the Heron's stem post. It followed the curve, sighed, and did not crack.

"It'll take it. Wrap your hands."

She meant the wet sacking. They wound it round their fists and drew the plank out together, he at the stern end and she at the stem, and it slid out of the box limp and steaming, warm as a hive wall at noon, and heavier than a board has any right to be once it has stopped holding itself up. Water ran off it and spat on the hot iron. She walked her end to the stem, laid it in the groove she had waxed, and called over her shoulder: "Feed it."

He fed it aft along the frames, hand over hand, the board bowing as it travelled. She seated the first cramp, a hard ring of iron on the frame, and then the second, and ran down the board pressing it home with her palms. At the belly of the curve, where the third frame stood, the bend stopped and began to push against them. He saw her find the place. She knelt and sighted along it, and rose with her mouth set.

"Two cramps and my weight won't do it. It wants a hold from the shoulders until it sets. Here."

She took his wrists, one in each hand, and set his palms on the plank at the belly of the bend. Then she stepped in front of him and laid her hands down over the backs of his, and her back met his chest.

The last time it had met him there, two coats and a pry bar had been in the way. Now there was one shirt each, and the steam, and wet sacking round their fists. She leaned into him as she would have leaned on a wall she had tested, and the wall held still.

"Lean. Not with your arms. With all of you."

He leaned. The oak gave a finger's width and stuck, and the whole of her braced against it, and he braced against her.

"Whatever I say, don't let go. It sets in a hold or it springs back and splits."

"I'll hold."

Under their palms a faint tick ran along the grain, the first warning, and her whole back stiffened against him. "Don't," she said, to the oak or to him, he could not tell. He tightened his hands and did not breathe, and the tick ran out and was not followed. Her breath left her in a rush that stirred the steam between his wrists.

He had not stood this near her while she worked since the year before the thaw road took her. He had forgotten, or had made himself forget, that she worked with her whole body, heels to crown, and that every board she bent bent in her first. Steam clung to his jaw and to hers. His breath stirred the sawdust in her hair, and the flat pencil behind her ear sat a palm's breadth from his mouth. His forearms bracketed her ribs, and he knew what lived under the left one. He kept his thumb a hair clear of it, because a flinch now would split a night's work, and neither of them would ever mention why.

"Talk," she murmured. "Anything. It keeps my hands from wanting to shift."

"About what?"

"The bees."

So he told her, low, close to her ear because there was nowhere else to put his voice. The guards had shut the entrances by now, and a hive at dusk was the quietest crowd in the valley, thousands of sisters all awake and none of them speaking. In a dearth the poorest hives turn out their drones to die in the cold, though never in blossom, when every hive is rich and forgives idle mouths. The blossom would hold a week if the wind kept off, and the supers would fill the week after, and then he would have to cut whatever he was feeling on the day.

She did not answer that. Her eyes were shut. He could tell by her lashes, which had stopped moving in the steam, and her breath slowed against his forearms until it ran in time with the boiler's note, and he let his own fall in with it.

The tick sounded again, once, deep in the oak, and was gone. Then the board took the curve with a long sigh, as a hive settles when the lid goes on, and laid itself against the third frame. The cramp bit and gave a single click. Neither of them had spoken for a long time.

"Not yet," she said, though he had not stirred.

The stove ticked. The wind leaned on the rafters and eased off. Heat drained out of the board by slow degrees, and the green seam crossed the back of her hand under his, and shivered, and neither of them let go.
