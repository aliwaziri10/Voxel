The sun cleared the hedge with warmth in it for the first time that year, and Ewan had the wraps off half the hives by then. He cut the ties with his hive tool, drew each wrap away from its box, shook the straw out of it and slung it over the terrace wall to air. Meltwater steamed in the ruts of the path. The willow at the turn was shedding catkin dust onto the hive roofs, and the first foragers were already out in it, slow and heavy, with pale pollen packed on their legs.

A thin line of black still lay in the groove of the hive tool's foot, rot from the frames of the Heron. He had not wiped it. He told himself the day had begun with other work.

Tobiah had carried the spare smoker fuel into the Honey House at first light and had not come out again. Ewan had stopped listening for the door.

He did the first hive as he did the first hive of every year, with his ear. He crouched at the landing board, laid his head against the wall of the box and listened. The note that came back was low and even and thin, a colony thinned by the winter and keeping itself warm. He gave the entrance a breath of smoke, lifted the lid a hand's breadth and cracked the seal of propolis. The cluster sat in its dark fist between two combs with its edge against the stores, and the capped honey above it was plentiful. He eased the lid shut. He did not pull a frame. Nothing in a hive that has wintered is improved by cold air.

The hive in the second row he did not open at all. It was the one he had shaken the swarm into, scoured with thyme, and he knelt and put his ear to the wood. The hum inside was round and busy, with a fine high thread through it that meant a queen had begun to lay. Two foragers dropped onto the board with pollen on their legs and walked in as though the hive had always stood there.

The gate latch clicked at the bottom of the yard.

Bryony had stopped just inside it, as anyone raised in Larkmere stops at a hive yard gate in the first fine weather, to be told whether the bees were slow or sharp. She had a crate on her hip and sawdust in her hair, and the sun lay full on her face. He had not seen her in plain sun since she returned. The veil hung folded at his belt, as it did on any morning this mild, and it came to him that he had not thought to offer it.

"They're slow today," he said. "Come through."

She crossed the yard with the crate on her hip, careful on the wet ground, and stopped three paces off, at the edge of what the bees would take for theirs.

"Ottoline says to tell you it's the first fine day."

"I'd noticed."

"She said you would. She said you'd know what it meant."

"It means she has been at the kitchen window since light, counting my wraps."

She shifted the crate to her other hip and did not answer that. "These were in the Sheds. The old frames. Ottoline says you'll want them before the flow."

"Set them on the stand."

Neither of them mentioned the roof.

"And the oak in my father's yard wants carrying down to the Sheds," she said. "I'll not ask Tobiah."

"I'll ask him."

"He won't speak to me."

"He won't speak to me either, the whole way down. He'll carry it."

"I didn't ask him to speak."

"No," said Ewan. "Oak he can manage in silence."

She set the crate on the stand beside the open box, harder than she meant to. The bees on the rim of the box rose off the wood in a small, cross cloud, and one of them found her left wrist, on the soft inside where the sleeve had ridden up, and drove in. It was the left cuff, the one where a single bee had stayed behind in the lane. He did not say so.

She did not flinch. She looked at the wrist and then at him. "It's in me," she said.

His hand had her wrist before he had decided to cross the gap.

He turned it palm up in the sun. The barb stood in the skin with its small sac still pumping, and he laid his thumbnail flat beside it and scraped it away sideways, as he had scraped it from every hand ever stung in his yard. Never pinch. A pinch squeezes the rest of the venom in. The barb came out. A bead of blood rose where it had been, bright as a haw berry, and the skin round it began to lift and whiten.

Under his thumb her pulse ran quick and shallow. His thumb had not needed to search for it. Then the back of her hand lay open in the sun, with the scar across it that the drawknife had left, and his thumb travelled the distance from her pulse to the end of that scar and stopped where it had always stopped. He had not told it to.

In the Sheds there had been two coats. Here there was only skin, and he found he could stand it half as well.

"It's out," she said.

"It's out."

"Then you can let go."

"It swells if you work it."

"I'm not working it."

"Your hand was."

She did not take it back. He held her hand as he held anything that might fly, loosely enough that it could leave, and he knew precisely where the leaving would begin: low in the wrist, a quickening under his thumb. She had gone once on less warning than that. His hands, which shook whenever they were empty, had not shaken since he took her wrist.

The crate had struck the stand hard when she set it down, and the top frame had cracked along its comb. A bead of old honey stood at the break, dark, gone set at the edges. His free hand was on it before the rest of him knew it had left. His thumb took up the bead and laid it on the sting, a dab, as he had done for every stung hand since he was a boy, because honey drew the heat out of a sting. Only after, with the sweet already spreading on her skin, did he think: whose day.

It was old comb. He had cut it in no year he could name from the frame, and the honey in it held the feeling of one day, and he could not have said which. That was the whole trouble with honey. He never knew.

She lifted the wrist to her mouth. It was what anyone did with a stung wrist, and she did it before she could have thought. His hand went with it, because he had not let go.

Her eyes closed. They stayed closed while the bees muttered on the rim of the box. When they opened they went to the willow and rested there, and her face did what a colony does when the lid comes off in cold weather: it drew in around what mattered and gave the light nothing.

He could not read it. A beekeeper learned what was in his jars from the faces of the people who tasted them, and he had never before been afraid to look.

"It's stopped stinging," she said.

She did not say what it had tasted of. He did not ask. He did not know what he would have done with the answer.

Two flat notes sounded from the Honey House door.

Tobiah stood in it with the sack of fuel hugged to his chest and his ears pink to the tips. He whistled again through his teeth, thin and sour. It was the whistle he kept for lying, and he was not lying, so it was only noise, and he looked at nothing while he made it.

Ewan opened his hand. Her wrist slid out of it.
