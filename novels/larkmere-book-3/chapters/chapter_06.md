The sun cleared the hedge with warmth in it for the first time that year, and Ewan had the wraps off half the hives by then. He cut the ties with his hive tool, drew each wrap away from its box, shook the straw out of it and slung it over the terrace wall to air. Meltwater steamed in the ruts of the path. The willow at the turn was shedding catkin dust onto the roofs, and the first foragers were already out in it, slow and heavy, with pale pollen packed on their legs. The ones that had found the crocus under the south wall carried orange instead. In the Marrick house below the terrace the kitchen curtain twitched whenever he cut a tie.

A thin line of black still lay in the groove of the tool's foot, rot from the frames of the Heron. He had not wiped it. He told himself the day had begun with other work.

Tobiah had carried the spare smoker fuel into the Honey House at first light and had not come out again. From inside came the short, hard sounds of a boy setting jars on a shelf with more force than jars required. Ewan had stopped listening for the door.

He did the first hive as he did the first one of every year, with his ear. He crouched at the landing board, laid his head against the wall and listened. The note that came back was low and even and thin, a colony thinned by the winter and keeping itself warm. He gave the entrance a breath of smoke, lifted the lid a hand's breadth and cracked the seal of propolis. The cluster sat in its dark fist between two combs with its edge against the stores, and the capped honey above it was plentiful. He eased the lid shut. He did not pull a frame. Nothing in a colony that has wintered is improved by cold air.

The box in the second row he did not open at all. It was the one he had shaken the swarm into, scoured with thyme, and he knelt and put his ear to the wood. The hum inside was round and busy, with a fine high thread through it that meant a queen had begun to lay. Two foragers dropped onto the board with pollen on their legs and walked in as though the colony had always lived there.

The third row was quiet. He knew it from the end of the terrace, the way a man knows from the lane that a kitchen is empty. No forager stood on the landing board. He crouched and laid his head to the wood and heard the wind in the entrance and nothing behind it.

He lifted the lid and did not trouble with smoke. The cluster hung dead between two combs, bees head-down in the cells with their tongues out for what they had not reached, and the capped stores sat one frame over from them, whole and never touched. The cold in the dark of the year had packed them tight round the queen, and they had kept their fist and starved beside a full larder. It happened in a hard winter to one box in a yard. It did not choose the weak.

He lifted the frames out and leaned them against the stand and swept the dead onto a square of sacking with the soft brush. They were light as husks. He laid the sacking on the terrace wall and kept his place over it a moment. The cold had meant nothing by it. He stayed there anyway. Willow came first, then blossom, then the swarms, then the limes, and the limes had never asked how many boxes he had brought through the winter. He had one fewer than at the first frost, and the crates would want filling all the same.

The next box in the row hummed under his ear, thin and sure, and he had the lid off it when the gate latch clicked at the bottom of the yard.

Bryony had stopped inside it, as anyone raised in Larkmere stops at a hive yard gate in the first fine weather, to be told whether the bees were slow or sharp. She had a crate on her hip and sawdust in her hair, and the sun fell full on her face. He had not seen her in plain sun since she returned. The tool roll hung across her shoulders on its leather thong, oiled and tied the way it was tied for a road. A colony that keeps its workers bunched at the entrance has not made up its mind to stay, and he saw the roll, and put the thought where he put the others. The veil hung folded at his belt, as it did on any morning this mild, and it came to him that he had not thought to offer it.

"They're slow today," he said. "Come through."

She crossed the yard with the crate on her hip, careful on the wet ground, and stopped three paces off, at the edge of what the bees would take for theirs.

Her glance dropped to the tool in his fist and caught on the foot of it, where the black lay in the groove.

"That's the Heron," she said.

"I haven't come to it."

"You could have wiped it."

"I could," he said, and put the tool in his pocket with the Heron still on it.

"Ottoline says to tell you it's the first fine day."

"I'd noticed."

"She said you would. She said you'd know what it meant."

"It means she has been at the kitchen window since light, counting my wraps."

She shifted the crate to her other hip and did not answer that. "These were in the Sheds. The old frames. Ottoline says you'll want them before the flow."

"Set them on the stand."

"Two have split bars. I can plane new ones."

"I haven't asked."

"No," said Bryony. "You'd let them rot on the shelf first."

He had no answer to that. It was true of most things.

She nudged the near foot of the stand with her boot. It sat in a pool of meltwater, and the wood had gone black to the knee. "That wants a stone under it."

"It wants a new leg."

"Then it wants both."

Neither of them mentioned the roof.

Her eyes travelled along the second row to the one he had not opened. "Is that them? The ones from the lane."

"That's them."

"All of them?"

"All but one."

She turned back her left cuff, a short hard turn, and held her arm out into the light to show him it was bare. "It left me at the quay."

"It had the sense."

Her breath ran out through her nose, and for the length of it she was nearly laughing. She put that away. "The thaw road's drying," she said, to the hedge.

"It dries first," said Ewan. "It always has."

"And the oak in my father's yard wants carrying down to the Sheds," she said. "I'll not ask Tobiah."

"I'll ask him."

"He won't speak to me."

"He won't speak to me either, the whole way down. He'll carry it."

"I didn't ask him to speak."

"No," said Ewan. "Oak he can manage in silence."

In the Honey House a jar struck a shelf hard enough to ring. Neither of them remarked on that either.

Her glance crossed the sacking on the terrace wall, where the dead of the third row lay heaped, and he watched her read it once, to the end: the cluster, and the capped stores one frame away. She said nothing. She set the crate on the stand beside the open box, harder than she meant to. The bees on the rim rose off the wood in a low, cross cloud, and one of them found her left wrist, on the soft inside where the cuff was still turned back, and drove in. It was the same cuff the bee had climbed in the lane. He did not say so.

She did not flinch. She looked at it and then at him. "It's in me," she said.

His hand had her wrist before he had decided to cross the gap.

He turned it palm up in the sun. The skin was cool from the crate and smelled of oak dust. The barb stood in it with its tiny sac still pumping, and he laid his thumbnail flat beside it and scraped it away sideways, as he had scraped it from everyone ever stung in his yard. Never pinch. A pinch squeezes the rest of the venom in. The barb came out. A bead of blood rose where it had been, bright as a haw berry, and the skin round it began to lift and whiten.

He watched the welt for spreading. A sting will take a whole forearm in a body that cannot bear it. Her color stayed and her breath kept its own pace, and the welt kept the size of a button.

"That was me," she said. "I set it down hard."

"You did."

"They'll hold it against me."

"They've short memories," said Ewan. "They'll have forgotten by evening."

Under his thumb her pulse ran quick and shallow. His thumb had not needed to search for it. Then the back of her hand lay open in the light, with the scar across it that the drawknife had left, and his thumb travelled the distance from her pulse to the end of that scar and stopped where it had always stopped. He had not told it to.

In the Sheds there had been two coats. Here there was only skin, and he found he could stand it half as well.

"I should have offered you the veil," he said.

"I never wore one."

"I know you didn't. I should have held it out anyway."

She watched the willow. "It's one sting."

"It's one sting," he agreed, and did not let go.

"It's out," she said.

"It's out."

"Then you can let go."

"It swells if you work it."

"I'm not working it."

"Your hand was."

She did not take it back. He held her hand as he held anything that might fly, loosely enough that it could leave, and he knew precisely where the leaving would begin: low in the wrist, a quickening under his thumb. She had gone once on less warning than that.

The tool roll still hung across her shoulders. The thaw road lay dry beyond the hedge and would have her at the coast by morning. Only a keel with a soft heel and an old woman's letter held her to the Rise or the Sheds, and Ottoline knew it, who had written the letter. Tobiah had known it since the lane. Ewan knew it best. He kept the one grip that would let her go.

His hands, which shook whenever they were empty, had not shaken since he took hold.

The flow was still a long way off, and he was grateful to the weather for it. Cut a frame today and the jar would carry this morning up the Rise to every table in Larkmere, the sting and the pulse and his own thumb, and every neighbor who took a spoon would learn from it what he had never said aloud. A beekeeper could not choose what the comb took in. He could only choose the day, and the days did not wait for him to be ready.

The crate had struck the stand hard when she set it down, and the top frame had cracked along its comb. A bead of old honey stood at the break, dark, gone set at the edges. His free hand was on it before the rest of him knew it had left. His thumb took up the bead and laid it on the sting, a dab, as he had done since he was a boy, because it drew the heat out. Only after, with the sweet already spreading on her skin, did he think: whose day.

It was old comb. He had cut it in no year he could name from the frame, and the honey in it held the feeling of one day, and he could not have said which. That was the whole trouble with honey. He never knew.

She lifted the wrist to her mouth. It was what anyone did with a stung arm, and she did it before she could have thought. His hand followed it, because he had not let go.

Her eyes closed. They stayed closed while the bees muttered on the rim. When they opened they found the willow and rested there, and her face did what a colony does when the lid comes off in cold weather: it drew in around what mattered and gave the light nothing.

He could not read it. A beekeeper learned what was in his jars from the faces of the people who tasted them, and he had never before been afraid to look.

"It's stopped stinging," she said.

"Leave it on until it dries."

"I wasn't going to wipe it."

She did not say what it had tasted of. He did not ask. He did not know what he would have done with the answer.

Below the terrace the kitchen curtain hung perfectly still, which in that house meant a woman holding her breath.

Two flat notes sounded from the Honey House door.

Tobiah stood in it with the sack of fuel hugged to his chest and his ears pink to the tips. He whistled again through his teeth, thin and sour. It was the whistle he kept for lying, and he was not lying, so it was only noise, and he looked at nothing while he made it.

Ewan opened his hand. Her wrist slid out of it.
