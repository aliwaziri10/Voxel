The hives had been saying it all night, and by first light he had run out of ways not to hear.

It was a thick sound, a hum with a drag in it, the note of a colony with nowhere left to put anything. He walked the first row of the terrace with the smoker held low and lifted a lid, then the next. The supers were full. The comb stood capped to the edges in wax gone pale and flat as a hawthorn petal, and when he tipped a frame to the dawn it hung heavy and shone at the seams. One more warm afternoon of this flow and the bees would decide the house was too small. Half of them would go out over the hedge in a roar with the best of the stores in their bellies, and what they left behind would sour under the cappings.

So he would cut today. He had known it since the orchard, when he had told her ready or not as though the choice were his.

The rule had never been hard before. One day's cut made one feeling, and he had carried whatever he was up the path to the shelf every spring of his life, and the town had climbed the Rise to buy a dry afternoon or a morning of fog and carried it home in a jar. Nobody was hurt by it. Nobody was made to taste anything they had not come up the hill to buy. But a cut could not wait for a calm morning, because bees kept no calm mornings on his account, and honey from two days run together tasted muddled and was good to nobody. He had one day. He would have to stand in it as he was.

Then he loaded the barrow with supers and wheeled them down the path, the hum following as far as the gate.

The Honey House was cool and stone-floored and smelled of wax and the shut-up sweetness of a hundred cut days. He lit the stove under the water can and set the uncapping knife in it to warm, and the stove's heat found his forearms. While the knife warmed he did what he had done the night before without asking himself: he looked at the shelf below the top one. It stood bare. He had cleared it by lamplight and wiped it down, and had called that tidying.

Above it, on the top shelf, the squat glass jar of dark honey sat sealed with its disc of Marrick wax, a strip of blue linen tied round the neck, turned so the blank side faced out. He did not look at it long. He had never needed to.

The first frame slid from its super, and he stood it over the pan. The knife came out of the can steaming. One stroke down the capped face, and the cappings fell away in a long pale curl into the pan, and the cells beneath stood open and straw-colored and began to lean. A bead gathered on the lower bar and let go.

The work took his hands, as it always did. They knew the angle of the knife and the turn of the frame and the wait while the drip thinned. Under the hands, where nothing held a tool, the other thing went on.

He had a method, from the years of not hoping. You cut the entrance down to a finger's width and nothing came in. He stood in the cool and drew the knife down a second frame and set about narrowing it. Hive weights came first. After them the clover coming on along the lane verges, the elder not yet open, the dandelion clocks gone to seed on the terrace wall, the swifts: anything with no person in it.

The hedge had a petal in it. He had walked out of the orchard with one in his hair, and she had not taken it out. Clover led him down to the lane, and the lane to a bee on a sleeve, and her scarred hand under his with the swarm walking over both. Each road he sent his mind down came back by a new way to the same landing board, like a forager that strays off the line and finds home regardless.

You cannot narrow the entrance against what lives inside the hive. He had known that about bees all his life.

Honey ran over his thumb where the knife had skimmed a cell. He cleaned it off with his mouth, as keepers do without thinking, and held the taste: clover, a thread of hawthorn, a sweetness that tasted of nothing but itself. Then he thought, on purpose, of her mouth. He had no right to it, with the warm knife in his other hand and the pan filling at his feet, and he thought of it anyway, the corner of it drawn in when she listened to the Heron, and the sweetness on his tongue stayed sweetness. It was plain and thin as any afternoon. It had never once told him a word about himself. Of all the laws of the honey, that was the one he had never forgiven.

"You'll want the second pan," Ottoline said from the door.

He had not heard her come. The keys at her belt hung silent, which meant she had been standing there a while. She looked at the pan and not at him.

"The first is at the brim, and your knife has been doing nothing since I came up the path."

He looked. The pan was full to the lip. The knife hung cold at his side.

"It won't wait," he said.

"No."

"The supers."

"I can hear the supers from the kitchen." She came one step over the sill, which she did not often do. "One day's cut, Ewan, one feeling. You know the rule as well as I do. Whatever you carry in at that door goes in the pail, and Larkmere will have it on a spoon before the clover is over."

"I know."

"Then think of anything but her."

He said nothing.

"It won't work," Ottoline said. "You can't practice it and you can't hide it. But a man ought to go down trying, and it will keep your hands out of mischief."

"I've been thinking about the hedge."

"And how is the hedge?"

"It has her in it."

"Hm." She turned one key on its ring a quarter turn, as if it were the wrong key, and went back out into the light. At the corner of the wall she called without turning round, "Try the weather."

He tried the weather. The weather was a storm in a loft and a blanket scorched at the hem, and he went back to the frames.

Tobiah came in when the second pan was half full. He had his veil bunched at the back of his collar and an armful of cloth squares, and he did not say good morning. He hung the squares over the rail of the bench, lifted the full pan from the floor and carried it to the straining cloth. Then he bent over it and breathed in once, and set it down too carefully, the way he handled a frame with the queen on it.

"It smells like Bryony," he said.

"It smells of clover and a hot knife."

"I know what it smells of."

Ewan turned the frame. A boy could not smell a feeling in a pan. He could read one on the man standing over it, a man he had lived beside for years and who had never been hard to read, and that was how a keeper learned what he held, from other faces. Here it was, before a single jar was sealed: a face gone flat and braced.

"I'm not tasting it," Tobiah said.

"Nobody asked you to."

"Everybody will." He shook out a cloth square and smoothed it on the bench, and smoothed it flatter. "They'll carry it home from the quay and eat it off a spoon at the table and go quiet."

Ewan saw them. He had not meant to. The stooped neighbor who had told her that her father would be proud, and the quay man whose boat the storm had broken, each with a jar under one arm. The town had come down to the shore with a kettle and a loaf and worked beside her on the shingle, and now it would climb the Rise and find out what he had been the whole while. Nobody would say a word to him. They would taste it, and look at their spoons, and have it round the harbor by evening.

He had watched her face over four spoons at his aunt's table and learned more from it about four days of himself than seven springs of his own tongue had told him. He knew what a face did.

"Then they'll know," he said, and set the knife in its can.

Tobiah looked at him as though there were more, and what he said was, "I'll tie. You write."

"You don't have to."

"I'm not doing it for her."

"I know."

So they did it in the long light from the door. The honey went through the cloth into the settling pail, slow and clear, and Ewan ladled it into plain round jars while Tobiah drew a square of cloth over each mouth and tied it down with string, in a knot that would have held a hive roof in a gale. He did not hurry and he did not speak. Ewan wrote the strips, one for each jar, in the hand the town knew: the flower and the weather of the day. He wrote clover and hawthorn, and under that, dry, a wind from the south, and then he wrote it again, and again.

"You could put rain," Tobiah said, reading over his arm.

"It isn't raining."

"Nobody would check."

"The honey would."

Tobiah went back to his knots.

By the time the last cloth was tied the sun had gone long and yellow through the door, and the hum from the terrace had dropped to the low even note of a yard at rest. They carried the jars to the shelf two at a time. One was left over on the bench, and Tobiah, with the last pair held against his chest, stopped.

"You cleared this shelf last night. I saw the lamp."

"It wanted clearing."

"That's what Ottoline says."

He set his two jars at the end of the row, squared them with one finger, and went out. The gate latch clicked across the yard.

Ewan stayed with the last jar in his hands. It was heavy and warm from the stove and it told him nothing, as none of them ever had. He set it at the end of the row.

They stood on the clean shelf beneath the squat dark jar, plain and round, their cloth tops tied, their paper strips turned to the room. Above them the blank glass faced out. Along the whole length of the shelf below it, every strip said the same two flowers and the same dry wind.
