The Heron's planks made a better table than anything the quay had ever owned.

They lay along the harbor wall on trestles, pale boards of new oak still smelling of steam, and the town had set its supper on them as if the wood had been waiting years to be asked. Round loaves. Cheese on cloths. A long dish of lake trout laid head to tail and glazed with its own fat, crocks of pickled onion, a pan of oat cakes too hot to touch. Children knelt on the cobbles with their elbows on the ends of boards that would carry the Heron's weather before the week was out.

Nobody had decided to call it a plank supper. A neighbor had said the planks were too fine to lie in a shed another night, and another had asked where else they would go, and by noon the trestles stood on the quay and every kitchen in Larkmere had sent a dish. Ewan had hived two swarms before dinner, one out of the willow and one off a lamp bracket on the quay itself, with a sheet and a shake and plenty of language. He walked down the Rise with propolis on his sleeves and the hum still in his ears, and the first sight that reached him was the space.

There had been a ring around her on the night the ice broke, an arm wide, kept clear by nobody's decision. It was gone. People stood in it. A woman passed her the cheese without being asked. A child held up a fish scale on a finger for her to admire, and she admired it with the gravity she gave to a plank.

She stood at the end of the boards with sawdust in her hair and a heel of bread in her fist, and she saw him, and her face did what it had learned to do. It eased, and she let it.

The man by the bollards was beside her. He was the one who had said it to his boots. He cleared his throat to the whole length of the table.

"You made the table," he said. "Sit."

She sat. She took the place at the end of the bench with her back to the harbor wall and glanced up, and Ewan took the place beside her before he had thought about the other benches. The bread passed down the boards. The trout passed after it. A fiddle began, badly, and a neighbor told it to stop.

They did not say much. They had said everything the day required. They had talked since dawn over planks and tarred line and the spring in the third strake, and they had talked about the swarms and the limes, and about nothing, which had turned out to take longer than either. They had not spoken of the Honey House or the jar. Neither of them had spoken of it since the evening her hand lay over his on the cloth, and the not speaking had grown into a room they both lived in.

She picked the bones out of her trout with two fingers, quick and tidy, laying them in a row along the board's edge.

"You eat like you're marking a plank," he said.

"I eat like I mean to finish."

"You've a bone left."

"That's yours."

She slid it onto his plate. It was the smallest gift he had ever been given, and he ate around it for a long time.

The light drained. Swifts stitched the dusk above the roofs and were gone, and the quay man with the taper began his walk along the posts, one gold flame after another down the wall. The talk dropped as the children were carried off. Down the shore the Net Sheds stood dark under their unlit lantern, and Ewan rose and said he had a wick to see to. She did not ask which wick. She nodded once and kept cutting bread, and when he returned along the shingle with the smoke of the taper on his hands the green light was burning on the wall, and she was sitting on the long bench under it with a blanket across her knees.

He did not ask where it had come from. He sat down at the end of it.

The lake lay dark and flat in front of them, and the harbor lanterns lay in it, broken and mended with every ripple. Behind the quay the last voices climbed the Rise in twos and threes. A dish rang once on stone. Then it was only the water and the lantern's thin creak on its hook.

"Is it enough?" she said, to the lake.

"Is what?"

"The day. The planks. All of it. It's not the Heron yet. I keep counting what's left."

"She'll go on in the morning."

"I know."

"Then you've done today."

She shifted along the bench, and without a word she drew the blanket's far edge across his knees, as he had once offered her a corner in the loft with the care he gave a frame full of bees. He took it. The wool was warm from her. It smelled of oak dust and cold iron and the lamp.

"Tell me what the swarm said," she said.

"The first one said it was tired of the willow. The second said it had never been tired of anything and wanted a better lamp."

Her breath left her nose, which in her was a laugh.

"I had that lamp bracket replaced in my head," she said. "With copper. I thought, if I can't have a hull, I'll have a bracket."

"It's yours."

"It's not."

"It could be. They'd swarm to it."

He caught her laugh through the wool before he heard it, a shake where their shoulders met. It ran out of her and left her softer. She leaned, a little, and the lantern's green lay across her hands in her lap, over the white seam of the old scar.

"And the third plank," she began. "Tomorrow I want to take the third plank and"

She stopped. He waited for the rest. The water slapped at the steps and drew back.

Her head dropped onto his shoulder.

She did it all at once, with the blunt weight of a person who had been awake since before the light. Her hair fell across his collar and lay against his neck, and the sawdust in it prickled and smelled of the shed. She did not stir. He sat with his arm held out from his side, not knowing where it belonged, and listened to her breath lengthen and slow.

Two breaths. Three. He tried to hold himself apart from the rhythm and in four breaths it had caught him, as it had in the loft in the storm, slow and level and in step. He did not try to break it this time.

He knew what would follow. One of them would wake. She would draw back and fold her hands and say it was late, or he would, and the night would end with the swift courtesy of a lid going on a hive. It would be nobody's fault. It would be the sensible course, and he was a sensible man, and he knew exactly how to do it.

Under the blanket, in her sleep, her hand turned over on her knee and slid across the wool until its fingers found his. They found them the way a forager finds its own door in the dark, without searching, and curled into his palm.

His hand closed on them. He had not decided to.

The lantern creaked. A swarm hangs from a branch that way, he thought, each bee holding the next and none of them the branch, the whole weight of it hanging quiet in the dark and waiting for morning. It was an odd notion to carry with a hand in his, and he kept it.

His arm turned numb to the elbow and then to the wrist. He did not move it. Her hair stirred against his neck at every breath, and the harbor lanterns died one by one along the quay until the green was the only light left, and in the long slow rise and fall of her breathing he stopped listening for anything at all.

For seven springs he had slept like a hive in a lean year, with a guard posted at the door and listening for a step on the gravel. Last night he had posted no guard.

He woke to grey.

It was the first light, flat and cold, with no sun in it. The lantern had burned down to a blue bead behind its green glass. His right arm hung dead from the shoulder and his neck ached, and there was a weight on his shoulder that was still, and warm, and breathing.

Her head had not shifted. Her hair lay across his collar, and her hand lay in his on the blanket, and the fingers had not let go.

There was a second blanket over them.

He knew it before he understood it. The first lay across their knees. This one lay over both of them to the chin, heavy, grey, with a scorched hem he knew. It had hung on a stove rail in a loft, in a storm. It smelled of pitch and warm iron, and it had not been there when he slept. Nobody had spoken. Nobody had touched him.

He lay still, and his heart struck once, hard.

If she woke first she would sit up and fold her hands, and if he woke first he would have to be the one to move. He knew both ends of it as he knew the turn of a season. So he did nothing. Beyond the shed wall a hive on the Rise took up its morning note, a long way off, and the first foragers began to rise from the terraces into the cold.

Out on the gravel behind the Sheds, a ring of keys sounded.

It was an old woman's pace, a step, a pause, a step, the ring swinging at a belt and falling silent each time she rested. He would have known it in his sleep. It receded over the shingle in the grey. He did not turn his head. Her fingers lay in his, and the keys climbed the shore, thinning, until the gulls had them.
