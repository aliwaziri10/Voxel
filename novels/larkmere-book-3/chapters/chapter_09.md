The orchard broke into blossom overnight. By first light the petals were loose on the wind, drifting down the slope through the apple rows and out across the shingle in a thin pale slant, and they settled on the Marrick punt where it lay turned over on its trestles at the foot of the Rise. Bryony brushed them off the planking with the flat of her hand. They were back before she had finished.

It was a flat-bottomed boat with a patched seam. The patch ran along the starboard side in a long scarf, cut by a hand that had known what it wanted and not how to get it, and the wax in the old seams had gone as brown and brittle as a dead leaf. She laid her palm on the bottom boards and rocked her weight forward, and the whole flat floor gave under it, which a sound floor does not do.

She had asked for the job the evening before, at the Net Sheds door, with the green light on the water behind her and her eyes on the shingle.

"It's only a punt," he had said.

"It's a boat. It carries a hive and a man across the lake. It leaks."

"It's leaked longer than I've had it."

"Then it's had long enough."

She had not told him why she wanted it. The Heron was a hull of rot and a list that had doubled, and nothing on her could be called finished or even certain. A punt could be finished in a morning and proved by afternoon. She wanted one hull in Larkmere to come out sound.

She had her father's caulking iron from the yard and a wad of oakum teased out on her knee, and she had begun to rake the dead wax from the seams when his boots sounded on the shingle. He carried a block of wax under one arm and a pail of hot water in the other hand, and a petal sat on his hat brim.

"You're early."

"The wax won't set itself."

He put the block on the trestle. Wax lay under his thumbnails, pale in the grain of the skin, and smoke hung in his coat. He crouched beside her and ran a thumb along the starboard patch, and she caught herself watching it and returned to her seam.

"Whoever cut that had more nerve than skill," she said.

"That was me."

"It shows."

"It held for years."

She laid a strand of oakum along the seam, set the iron to it and drove it home with the mallet, and the wood took it with a dull, sound note. She shifted along the seam and did it again. He watched her for a while without being asked to, and then he cut a slice of wax from the block, warmed it in the pail and rolled it between his palms into a rope, and held out the end.

It was what an apprentice did. She took it. Neither of them said so.

They worked along the seams together in the thin sun with the blossom coming down on them, her hand driving the oakum and his laying the warm wax over it for her to press home, and the work ran faster than it would have alone. Her mallet found its rhythm. His wax met her hand as she reached for it. By the time the sun cleared the apple trees the whole starboard side was done and the patch lay sealed under a long clean bead.

"She wants to swell," she said. "A dry boat takes a day to close."

"We could wait a day."

"No."

She did not trust a seam she had not seen take water. It was the one rule she had carried out of every yard she had worked in, and she would not have broken it for a punt in front of him or anyone.

Together they turned the punt and slid her down the shingle. She floated light and level on the lake, no more than a hand's depth of freeboard, and rocked once as she took the water and settled.

Bryony stood at the edge with her boots in the lake and watched the bottom boards.

She watched them for a long time. Nothing rose through the boards. A single dark bead formed on the patched seam at the forward end and ran slowly aft along the grain.

"It's weeping," Ewan said.

"It's one bead. It'll close." She was already kneeling in the shallows with her pencil, ringing the place on the plank in a tight circle. "I want to see it close on the water."

"Then let's take her out."

She glanced up at him. He stood holding the oars.

"I've not been out on it in seven springs," she said, and was surprised to hear it come out of her, because it was true of the lake and not only of the punt.

"It hasn't changed."

"It always changes."

"Then you'll want to see it," Ewan said.

She climbed in and took the stern seat. He pushed the punt off and stepped aboard in a single motion, and the boat dipped and steadied beneath them, and he set the oars in their pins and settled on the middle thwart facing her. It was a narrow hull. When he leaned to the first stroke his knees rose on either side of hers and stopped there, and the wool of his trousers lay against the wool of hers.

She kept her face turned toward the shore. The bottom boards stayed dry under her boots.

The lake took them out in long easy strokes. His sleeves were pushed to the elbow and the old stings stood out pale along his forearms, and she could have named the year of each and did not. The shingle fell away, and the orchard rose behind it in a white cloud on the shoulder of the Rise, and the petals drifting off it fell on the water around them and rode there, pale and slowly turning, in the wake. The oars dipped and lifted. Water ran off the blades in glass threads. She had not known her shoulders were up around her ears until the lake let them down.

Nothing on land in Larkmere had been easy. Every lane held a face, and every door a conversation she had planned and not had. But the water asked her nothing. It lifted the boat or it did not, it held or it leaked, and she could read every word it said in the boards under her feet. Nothing in the long road to the coast had ever made her this light.

"There," Ewan said, and tipped his head toward the Far Shore.

The lime wood lay on its slope across the water, a long dark band under the sky with the new leaf beginning to haze the tops of the trees. A line of old stone wall ran down through it. She could see the roof of a hut among the trunks, no bigger than a thumbnail from here.

"When the flow comes you can smell it from the middle of the lake," he said. "Before you can see anything wrong with the trees. The air goes sweet. You get a taste of it on your lip. Then the sound starts, low, like the whole wood is thinking. Within a day the leaves are sticky to the touch and you can't put your hand on a trunk without a hundred of them walking up your wrist."

"And then?"

"Then I cut, whatever day it is, and the crates have to come across."

"The punt takes one hive."

"The punt takes one hive and me," he said. "It would take me all season to bring over what the Heron brings in a morning. Nothing else on Larkmere Water is broad enough for the crates."

"I know."

"I know you know."

She studied the Far Shore and measured it. It was farther than it had appeared from the shingle. A broad ferry with a keel scarfed new and a clean sheer would take the crossing in her stride. A broad ferry with rot in her frames would not take it at all. She was the only one in Larkmere who could finish the Heron, and work that nobody else could do was hard work to be late for.

"The wind's backing," she said.

He glanced round. A dark line had formed on the water to the west, a catspaw of wind creeping over the surface toward them.

"It does that at the middle," he said. "It'll be gone by the time we turn."

She watched the line. She did not like it, and the dark bead on her patch had not closed.

"Who taught you?" he said.

He asked it carefully, without looking at her, to the oars. It was the voice he had used in the hive yard to say that the bees were slow. He might have been asking her the name of a plank.

"What?"

"To test a seam on the water. To rake out the old wax before you lay the new. Who taught you?"

She knew what was under it. It was not a question about caulking. It was a door he was holding open a hand's width, to see what she would do.

"Abel Trent," she said. "On the coast."

She said it flatly and let it lie, and turned her face toward the Rise. She did not say what manner of man he was, or how long she had stood at his bench. She had never said any of it in Larkmere. It was a closed shed with a sound roof on it, and it could keep.

He did not ask for more. His oars kept their stroke, even and easy, and she heard him take in the answer and put it away and not turn it over again. It was the most generous course he could have taken.

The knee against hers did not shift. Neither of them shifted it. The boat rocked and the wool pressed and eased and pressed again, and she became aware, in a part of herself that she had no use for in a punt, of how long it had been since anyone had been so near her for no reason but the size of the boat.

A petal fell from the orchard wind, tipped, and settled in his hair above the left ear. It sat in the dark there, pale as a shaving. He did not know it was there. His hands were on the oars.

She could have reached it with two fingers. Her hands knew the distance. She kept them in her lap.

The dark line on the water had reached them and gone by, and there was only a cold breath of it, and a ripple along the strakes. The lake slapped at the hull. She glanced at the boards.

The bead had closed.

"It's tight," she said.

"Is it?"

"It's taken up."

He rested on the oars. "I'd have said it was tight an hour ago."

"I wouldn't." She shook her head slowly. "But it is now."

"Then she's yours to be proud of."

"She's a punt," said Bryony. But the word eased a knot in her middle, and she let it ease.

He turned the punt with two strokes and brought her about for the shore. The orchard was a white wall now, tilting gently as the boat swung, and blossom fell steadily from it onto the water. The oars knocked in the pins. She did not glance at his hair, and she glanced at it, and the petal was still there.

The bottom boards took the shingle with a long soft hiss. He shipped the oars and stepped over the side into the shallows, and held the gunwale steady for her. The lake ran off his boots. In the dark wet hair above his ear the petal still lay, pale against it, and he stood on the shore with the punt's rope in his fist and said nothing about it, and neither did she.
