The last wedge was struck at mid-morning, with the shed doors open to the wet smell the squall had left on the lake.

She knelt at the heel with the mallet. Since the thaw she had eased the old keel down on oak wedges, knocking each one back a quarter inch and listening to the wood, and the Heron had sat lower on her props with every blow, as a hull settles on a falling tide. Her shoulders still carried yesterday's pump. Both palms were raw where the handle had worked them. She had wrapped the left in a rag that morning and then torn the rag off, because a wrapped hand cannot tell what a mallet is doing.

The back of that hand, which she had caught herself studying since the quay, held nothing now but its scar.

She set the mallet to the wedge and struck.

It gave. For the space of a breath nothing else happened, and then the keel let go of the Heron with a long low groan that ran the whole length of the timber, from the stem end to the heel, the sound a tree makes when it comes down after the saw has finished. The oak dropped the last hand's depth onto the balks she had laid for it. The shed floor took the weight. Shavings leapt off the boards and settled. Dust hung in the light from the doors and turned slowly.

She stayed on her knees until the groan had gone out of the building.

It lay there in one piece, black and enormous, the oak her father had chosen and shaped and set down in this shed before she was old enough to hold a spokeshave steady. Seven springs on its back had not shifted it. Nothing had shifted it but rot. She laid her palm along the flank and the wood was cold and faintly damp, and under the damp she could smell oak, old oak, with the sourness of the heel coming up through it like bilge.

The heel was as she had known it would be. She drove the awl in at the after end and it sank to the handle with no more resistance than a thumb through bread. Brown crumb clung to the point. She tried again a hand further forward and the wood held, and a hand beyond that it held better. She sat back on her heels with the awl in her fist.

She had expected to feel differently. She had imagined this morning more than once, and in every version the keel lifted out and she was lighter for it, the way a boat rises when you take the ballast off. She was not lighter. A heavy weight of timber lay on the floor of her father's shed, and her work now began and not ended.

The shock of the drop had opened the scarf joint a finger's width at the lower face.

She saw it as she shifted her knees: a dark seam in the flank where the two lengths of the keel had been joined, a long sloped lap cut so that it would lock and bear. The iron that had drawn it tight was rust to the core. It had crumbled when the keel dropped, and the joint stood a little open, and in the gap, where no eye had been since the day the faces were cut and clamped, she saw a streak of grey.

Pencil.

She dropped onto her stomach in the shavings with her cheek on the floor and her eye to the gap. Grey light fell in, enough to read by if she held her head still. Pencil, flat-sided, a carpenter's, laid on the sloped face of the lower lap in a hand she would have known on the far side of the lake.

She did not touch it yet. She rose, fetched a thin ash wedge from her roll, set it in the joint and tapped it once with the heel of her hand. The two faces parted with a long dry sigh, the sigh of surfaces that had not seen the air since they were made, and the daylight reached in and lit the writing.

*Mind the heel. Marrick wax in every seam.*

She read it once. She read it again. The letters leaned a little, as his had always leaned, as if the words were walking into a wind. The M had its hooked leg. The stop after *heel* was a hard dot driven into the grain, where he had pressed too hard with the pencil's corner, the way he did when he was sure.

She sat down on the shed floor.

She did not decide to. Her legs let her down into the shavings and the mallet rolled off her knee. The shed was silent. Out on the lake a late gull complained, and the wet doors ticked as they dried.

He had known. He had known where this keel would fail first, because a boatwright knows where the weak place in his own work lies, and he had written the warning where only a hand that drew the scarf would ever find it. Which meant a Fairlie hand. Which meant that this line was not for her at all, not at the start. It was for whoever followed him and drew this joint in a failing hull, and found the writing, and learned. He had written it to whoever followed, and she was the one who had.

*Marrick wax in every seam.*

She had caulked the punt with Marrick wax, the day before the squall. Ewan had rolled the rope of it between his palms and held out the end, and she had taken it without a word. Her father's pencil had said it long before either of them was thought of as a pair. She had no way to know what he had meant by it. She knew only that it was a joiner's instruction, plain as a bevel, and that it was set down in the one place on the Heron no wind could reach and no town could read.

She put out her thumb and laid it on the pencil.

The grey lifted a little on the ball of it. She did not wipe it away.

"That'll be the heel gone," said a voice behind her.

She did not start. Her hand stayed where it was.

Ottoline stood at the stem end of the keel, upright as a post in her black coat, with her back bent and her chin level. Bryony had not heard the keys. The old woman had come in with a hand flat on the ring at her belt to keep it silent, and she let it fall now so the keys spoke once against her hip.

"It's soft at the after end," Bryony said. Her voice stayed level, which surprised her. "The rest will take a scarf."

"I've no doubt." Ottoline regarded the open joint and the grey streak in it, and her eyes rested there and turned elsewhere. She said nothing of it. She crossed the shavings with her stick and lowered herself onto an upturned bucket by the cradle, slowly, in sections, and the keys rang as she sat. "I'm not here about the heel."

"I've work."

"You've sat down in the middle of it. Stay sat." She settled her stick between her knees. "The jar you saw."

Bryony took her thumb off the pencil.

"I didn't ask after it. I asked what it was."

"That's asking after it." Ottoline's voice had the tone of a weather remark, flat and certain. "He told you it's not for eating. That's true as far as it goes, and it's the worst answer he could have given you, and he knows it. So I'll give you the rest."

"Don't tell me."

"I'm telling you."

The shed was quiet. A board settled on the Heron's cradle with a dry tick.

"It's the comb he took on the morning you left," Ottoline said. "He cut it before light, which is not an hour I'd choose for the work. He rendered nothing and blended nothing. He put it up in that jar as it was, sealed it with a disc of our wax, and tied the blue linen on himself, and set it on the top shelf of the Honey House turned so the blank side faces out. It has not been opened since. That is seven springs."

Bryony said nothing. She sat with her hands open on her knees, empty, wanting a handle.

"You know what a Marrick jar is. I'll say it once, plain, because nobody does and everybody thinks it has been done." Ottoline's stick tapped once on the floor. "The honey tastes of the keeper's truest feeling on the day he cut the comb. Not what he thought, not what he said. The truest part of him. It can't be faked or hidden or practiced, and it never fades, and he can't choose it. He cuts the comb and whatever he is that day goes in the jar. And a keeper can't taste his own. He knows what's in his jars only from the faces of the people who eat them."

"Then you know."

"I do not." The old woman's eyes did not leave her. "Nobody has tasted it. Not I, nor the boy. Not Ewan, who couldn't have told you what was in it if he'd swallowed the lot. I'm telling you that it exists, and when, and why it's shut. I'm not telling you what's in it, because nobody living knows."

The words fell into the shavings and lay there.

Bryony's mind did what it had always done with a hull it could not test. It turned the problem over and searched for the strain. A man cuts comb in the dark on the morning the woman he loved takes the thaw road, and seals it, and puts it where he never has to see it. What does a man feel on that morning? She did not have to guess long. She had a plank in her mind, sprung hard on a frame and clamped and left, and nobody had ever taken the clamps off to see whether it had kept the curve or cracked. She knew which she would bet on. A jar that stays shut for seven springs is not full of what a man wants kept.

"Why tell me?" she said.

"Because you walk round a shut door, and it grows with every lap." Ottoline closed her hands on the stick and rose, in sections, as she had sat. "And because you will be in this town until the Heron floats, and I won't have you hear it from a neighbor with a worse mouth. Nobody is making you taste it. That's the whole of it."

Boots sounded on the shingle outside, unhurried, a man's.

The old woman's chin lifted. She had known the sound, as she knew every step in Larkmere, and she did not so much as glance at the doors. "That'll be a message about wax," she said, to nobody. "Be gentle with the post."

She walked out past the keel with her stick and her keys and her bent back, and Bryony did not turn her head. She heard the keys go quiet at the threshold, and a low exchange, three words, four. She heard the old woman say, "Ewan," and heard him say, "Aunt." Then the keys faded along the shingle, and the shed was quiet again, and the doors let in the smell of rain and cut oak.

She did not get up.

His shadow reached the floor first. It fell across the shavings and across the long black flank of the keel and stopped there. He had come three steps into the shed, and he had stopped, because of how she was sitting, or what he could see of her face from there.

"Tobiah says the wax is rendered and cooled," Ewan said. "He wouldn't bring the cake down himself. I said I'd carry it." He stopped. She heard him not finish. "I've left it on the bench."

She said nothing.

She had her head bent. She knew that without looking, as one knows the pitch of a deck by the weight in the knees. She could feel his eyes on the crown of her head, on the sawdust in her hair, on the pencil behind her ear that she had not touched in an hour. Three steps of shavings lay between her and the door. She knew exactly how far three steps was. She did not lean toward him. She had wanted to the moment the shadow fell, and she kept her weight where it was, on her palms in the dust, and her back as straight as a plumb line, and her breath even.

He did not come closer. He did not ask. He stood where he was with the lake light behind him and the wax on the bench behind him and the whole of her silence in front of him, and he held still, the way a man holds still when a wild creature has landed near his hand and has not yet decided.

She put her thumb back on the pencil. The grey of her father's words lay under it, soft and flat, and it did not come away any more.

In the door Ewan did not move.
