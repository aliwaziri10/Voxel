She had the edge of the third plank in the vise when the light in the yard door changed.

Tobiah stood in it with the box in both arms.

He had not run this time. He had walked, and slowly, and it showed in how he held himself, upright and careful, as a man carries a full pail across a yard in a wind. The box was the brown of his own boots, soft at the corners, with the lid pressed down and a length of twine looped across it that had been untied and not cut. She could see the turns lying flat where the knot had come out of them.

She laid the plane down on its side. He had learned that from her, and she watched him see it.

"I read the first one," Tobiah said. "I couldn't read the rest."

He crossed the floor and held the box out, his arms straight, and she thought he would have held it so for as long as she liked. She took it. It was lighter than she had braced for. Every month of her coast was in there, and it weighed no more than the boots it had been made for.

"Sit," she said.

They sat on the long bench along the south wall, which she had scoured of dust on her first morning, with a hand's width of oak between the two of them and the sun from the door lying across their boots. She kept the box on her knees and her palms flat on the lid. She did not touch the twine. She did not ask what the first one said, though it sat in the yard with them as plainly as the cold stove.

He talked to the floor.

"It sounded like you."

She waited.

"I didn't think it would. I thought it'd be a person writing. A stranger being careful." He lifted his shoulders and let them fall. "But you were in it. You were in it talking, and there was a part about the cold, and I could hear you say it. The way you say a thing and then stop." His voice thinned. "I couldn't do the next one. I'd have had to hear you again."

She nodded, because she did not trust anything else she had. Under her palms the lid was warm from his arms. In seven springs she had not once pictured this: that he would hear her, and that hearing her would be the thing he could not bear. She had written for a boy who could read and had never thought of a boy who could listen.

"Bry," he said.

She turned her head.

He had not called her anything since she came back. It had been *you*, and it had been nothing at all, and once, across the Sheds, it had been a flat *her*. The word came out in the voice he had had at ten, with the same crack in the middle, and his ears went pink to the tips. It was not temper this time. He kept his eyes on the floor as though the boards might answer for him.

"Yes?"

"Nothing." He scrubbed a knuckle under his nose. "I wanted to see if it still worked."

"Does it?"

"Seems to."

She found that she had to look at the vise. The edge of the plank stood there under its rule of light, true to a hair, and she studied it for a long minute, and when she could trust her face she looked round again. He had gone on sitting. That was the whole of what he had come to do, and he was doing it.

"There's a cast hanging under the Honey House eaves," he said at last. "Ewan says leave it till evening. It'll go when it goes."

"They do."

"I've the second row to weigh." But he did not get up at once. He sat on, and she sat on, with her father's box between her palms and her brother's shoulder a hand's width from her own, and neither of them moved until the sun had crossed the pegs.

She slept that night on the loft floor by the stove with the box beside her head. Twice she woke and put out a hand to be sure of it, the way one checks a moored hull in weather, and each time it was there.

Abel went at first light.

She walked him to the last wall of the town, where the lane gave out and the thaw road began its long climb into the grey. She had not meant to walk so far. She had meant to say goodbye at the Sheds. But he had come out with the pack on his back and the ash stick in his fist and had set off up the shingle without waiting, and her feet had gone after him. At the wall she stopped.

She had never stopped here. Seven springs ago she had gone through without a pause, and had kept her eyes on the road, and had not turned round until the first bend.

Abel stood in the road with the light behind him and the white eyebrow catching it. The grey came down the hill in long bars. A rook shifted on the wall and settled again. He looked at her for a long while with his hands crossed on the stick, and the left one trembled a little, and he did not hide it.

"I'll be back for the launch," he said.

"I know."

"You'll tell me then."

"I know that too."

"Don't tell me before you know." The stick shifted in his grip. "A seam doesn't close because you've asked it to. It closes when it's taken up. I'd as soon have a true answer in the autumn as a fair one now."

He did not say any more. He lifted two fingers, which was the whole of his farewell, and set the ferrule on the road and went up. She watched the pack go small between the hedges. She watched him go round the bend, where she had once turned, and she waited to see whether her body would go after him up the hill or stay at the wall.

It stayed. She noticed that it stayed and did not like how well she knew why.

The hill took the rest of the grey. Below her the roofs lay in their rows, and beyond them the harbor and the long shape of the Net Sheds, and above them all the Rise, with the white ground of the orchard fallen to green. She turned and walked back down, and at the Sheds door she took the box off the floor.

She took it up the Rise at the end of the day.

She had meant to carry it to Tobiah and tell him to keep it, and he had said it was hers. So it was hers. It sat in her arms the whole way up the lane, with the evening light going long and gold across the walls and the elder smell lying thick in the dry air, and she knew with each step that she could not think with it near her and could not think without it. Both her hands wanted a task. She wanted them for the box and the box wanted nothing, and the question she could not carry sat on top.

The Honey House door stood open. He was inside with a lamp not yet lit and a frame of capped comb across the bench, cutting it with a long knife, and he looked up when her shadow came onto the floor.

He did not ask. He wiped his hands on a cloth and waited.

"Hold this," she said.

She held out the box.

"I need both hands to think."

He took it. He took it in both arms as Tobiah had, and she saw him understand what it was before he had it settled, and she saw his hands go careful and strange on the lid. He did not look at the twine. He set it on the bench beside the frame, squarely, and put his hands behind him, and the room filled up with the plain quiet of a man who has been handed what he was never going to be given.

She did not look at the top shelf. She knew what stood on it. A squat glass jar, with the blank side out. She kept her back to it as she kept her back to the window in a gale.

"Think, then," Ewan said.

She had meant to. She stood in the cool, wax-smelling room with her hands open and empty at her sides, and what she thought about was the old man at the bend with two fingers lifted, and the long slip at the coast running green into the sea, and the trembling hand. It was a master's yard. She meant to say it. She had it ready in her mouth, plain and square, and the man in front of her stood with his hands behind his back and his smoke-smelling coat and the wax under his nails, and waited, as he waited for everything.

She stepped in and put her forehead against his.

She had not decided to. It was the thing her body did when her head had run out of road. It was a plain lean, the way one leans a spar against a wall to ease the load off it, and his forehead was warm and dry and there, and it took the weight. He drew a breath. She felt it go in, and felt it stop.

Neither of them said a word.

His hand came up. It came slowly, as it had come up in the lane on the first day, to see whether it would be struck, and it settled along her jaw with the thumb below her ear. The palm was rough. It smelled of wax. She shut her eyes and she could feel her own pulse in the hollow his thumb lay over, and in the dark behind her lids the room was a warm blur of sweetness, and his breath came across her mouth and went away and came back.

An inch of air lay between her mouth and his. She could have crossed it in the time it took to say his name. It was a smaller gap than any she had ever fitted a plank across, and a plank would have gone in at once.

She meant to. She felt the whole of her turn toward it, the way a hull comes round on her helm and feels the water take her. She had her lip caught in her teeth. She had her hand halfway up to the front of his coat.

Over their heads the eaves began to sing.

It started as a thread. She had been hearing it for a long time without letting herself, a thin high sound in the stone and the old rafters, and now it thickened and rose, and the whole roof took it up. It was a sound with a body to it. It ran down the walls into the floor. She felt it in the soles of her boots and then in her teeth, a roar like a weir in spate, and his hand went hard at her jaw.

"Ewan," she said.

"That's them," he said against her mouth, and she felt him laugh, a short helpless breath of it. "That's them going."

The roof let go.

It came off the eaves in a single dark rush, a brown storm of it, pouring past the open door in a rope so thick it put the evening out. The air shook. The sound filled the room until there was no room, and from every hive on the Rise the yard answered, the whole terrace lifting into noise, a clamor of wings from the first row to the wall. The lamp rocked on its nail. The knife skittered on the bench. A grey handful of dust came down off the rafters onto her hair, and she was laughing, and she could not stop, and neither could he.

They came apart the way a plank comes off the steam box, all at once and shaking. She had her hands over her mouth. He was bent half over with one fist braced on the bench and the other still held out, open, in the air where her face had been, and his shoulders were going. Tears ran into the creases at the corners of her eyes.

"It's the roof," he got out. "It's been the roof the whole afternoon."

"You said evening."

"I said it'd go when it went."

"You might have said it'd go then."

She went through the door ahead of him with her breath in rags. He snatched up the skep from the bench, and she thought he would run, and he did not. He stopped on the sill with the straw basket in his hand.

The yard was a gold roar. The swarm hung in the air above it, a great turning cloud shot through with light, and it drifted over the wall and the hawthorn and settled, all at once, as a squall settles when the wind has gone round. It gathered on a low bough of the old apple tree at the corner of the yard. In the time it takes to draw one breath it hung there, dark, heavy and enormous, a furled sail on its yard, with a murmur coming off it that sank and sank.

He took a step toward it with the skep.

She put her hand flat on his chest and held him where he was.

Under her palm his heart was going like a rope paid out too fast through the hand. She felt it through the coat, through the shirt, through the rough warmth of him, and she did not take her hand away. He did not go on. The swarm hung in the apple tree and the light went red across it, and the yard grew quiet one hive at a time.
