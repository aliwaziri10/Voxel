By noon Bryony had the shape of the garboard on a batten. She had taken it from the last sound length of the old plank, laying the batten along the lap and marking each curve with the divider, and the marks ran fair, and she did not trust them yet. A plank that read true on a stick had fooled better builders than her. The only test was to bend a real board round the real frames and see what it did, and the real boards were still in the lane on a cart.

The landward door darkened.

Tobiah ducked the lintel without being told it was low. He carried two oak planks across one shoulder with his free hand steadying the front end, and his step landed heel first, as their father's had. He set the planks down along the wall and walked out again without a glance at the hull or at her. He came back with two more.

He did not speak. She had told Ewan she did not need him to, and it was simple to say that on a hill in the sun. It was harder in a shed, with her brother, who was taller now than their father had ever been, carrying their father's oak past her as though she were a mooring post.

She watched the grain go by. Quartersawn, tight, the rays flecked silver on the face. A careful hand had chosen these boards and stacked them dry for years, and she would have given a good deal to know who.

On the last trip he laid the planks down and straightened, and she said, "Tobiah."

It was all she had. She had carried that single word for seven springs, and it was shorter than the rest of what she had planned.

He stopped in the doorway with his back to her. The daylight wrapped his shoulders and lay on the floor at her feet.

"You didn't write," he said.

"I wrote every month."

"I didn't read them."

He ducked the lintel and was gone.

She listened for the whistle. He had never been able to lie without it, not since he was small enough to be carried, and the lane outside held only the hiss of the lake on the shingle and the crunch of his boots going away. No whistle sounded. The points of the divider had pricked a row of white dents in her palm, and she had not known she was gripping it that hard.

She laid the batten against the cradle and drew the next line in a single pull. It ran fair, which was no help at all.

The afternoon went to the planks. She lifted each one onto the trestles and sighted down its length with one eye shut, looking for the twist that would show only when the steam reached it, and she turned the best two so the heart side would face the water. It was clean work and it asked nothing of her but attention, and she gave it all her attention so that none of it was left over.

Ottoline sent for her at dusk by the simple method of coming herself. She waited in the landward door with her keys quiet at her belt.

"Supper," she said.

"I've oat cakes."

"You've oat cakes and a draft. Supper."

She did not leave the doorway. Bryony had never in her life won a waiting match against her, and she put down the divider.

They climbed the Rise at the older woman's pace, and Bryony shortened her stride to it and did not remark on that. The Marrick kitchen had not changed. The table still carried the long scar of a hot pan, the flagstones still sloped toward the stove, and the smell of the place, bread and woodsmoke and wet wool drying, took her by the collar before she was through the door.

Four cups stood on the table. Tobiah sat at the far end with his eyes in his bowl. Ewan was at the stove with the kettle, and he lifted his head when she entered and lowered it again. Ottoline pointed her chin at the chair across from his, and Bryony took it.

There was soup first, plain, with no remark. Then Ottoline set a board on the table with a loaf and a knife and a wedge of cheese, and beside the loaf four round glass jars with cloth tops tied down with string. Each had a paper strip hanging from its neck. She stood a spoon in every jar and sat.

"Bread," she said. "A spoon from each."

"She doesn't have to," said Tobiah, to his bowl.

"Nobody is making her. She'll want the cheese," Ottoline answered.

The strips were in Ewan's hand. Bryony knew it from the downstrokes, and she read the first to give her eyes work. Clover, dry and bright.

"I'll eat," she said.

She cut a slice of the loaf, spread a spoonful on it and ate. It was honey, and then it was not. It was a boat riding at her mooring on the first dry afternoon, the line slack and nobody watching it, a weight that settled and wanted no tending. She swallowed. Across the table Ewan ate his own bread with the same honey on it, and his face stayed the face of a man eating honey. He tasted none of it. He never had. He lowered the bread and watched hers.

She did not look at him. She could feel where he sat as she could feel the lee of a wall. She read the second strip. Heather, wind from the north.

That one sat low. It dropped into her chest like ballast stowed under a floor, heavy and deep, with no part of it she could shift, and it kept her upright all the same. It was grief. For whom, she could not tell, and she did not ask.

Tobiah's spoon had gone still in his bowl. Ottoline cut more bread.

"Third," she said.

Dandelion, a high wind. Bryony tasted it and her mouth opened a little on no word at all. It was the instant a grounded hull lifts off the bar on the rising water, when the whole keel wakes under your feet.

"The pear tree," Ottoline told the cheese. "He brought the swarm down in a gale and lost none."

"I'd have thought," said Bryony, and found she had no end for it.

The fourth strip said apple, warm rain. She ate it slowly. It was the ache in the forearms after a full day at the drawknife, warm and clean, the tiredness of a day that had used all of her and left nothing over to carry home. She knew that ache. She had climbed the coast road with it every evening at the yard, and he had climbed this hill with it in another trade, and she was tasting it as she would have tasted her own.

The jars stood in a row before her, each with its leaning strip, and it dawned on her slowly and then all at once that a shelf of them waited in the stone house across the yard, row on row, a flower and a weather on every strip and a day of him behind each, years of Ewan Marrick's days set out in order for anyone with a spoon. Ottoline had sat her down to read them.

She had braced for the bead on her wrist, the old honey he had dabbed there in the first fine week. It was not among the four. She could not have told whether what moved in her at not finding it was relief.

His hand lay round his cup across the table, the old stings pale along the forearm, steady. Her own hands lay in her lap with no tool in them, and they knew it. They wanted a handle, a rail, the rim of a bowl. They wanted the cup, if she was honest, and that was worse than wanting nothing.

She raised her eyes to anywhere else.

The kitchen door stood open on the yard. Beyond it the Honey House door stood open too, with the lamp Tobiah had left burning on the bench, and from where she sat the lamplight reached the shelves. It lit the rows of jars with their written sides turned to the room. It lit the top shelf, and a squat glass jar of dark honey on it, sealed with a disc of Marrick wax, a strip of blue linen tied round its neck, turned so that the blank side faced out.

Her eyes stayed on it. It had no flower and no weather. Of every jar in that room it alone had nothing to say.

"What's that?" she asked.

Tobiah's spoon stopped against the rim of his bowl. Ottoline did not look up from the loaf, and her knife did not finish the slice.

Ewan's hand tightened round the cup.

"It's not for eating," he said.

The stove ticked. No one in the kitchen drew breath.
