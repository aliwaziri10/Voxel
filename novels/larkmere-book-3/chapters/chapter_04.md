Ottoline had made it an order at breakfast, in the voice she kept for weather. The first blocks of wax would go down to the Net Sheds this morning, and Tobiah would carry the second. Ewan had heard a remark about rain. Tobiah had heard an order, said "Fine" to his porridge, and now walked two paces behind him on the lane with a block hugged to his ribs and his mouth shut.

It was a loud silence. Ewan read it as he read a hive that had lost its queen, by a note that ran too even, and he left it alone. Meltwater braided in the ruts. The hedges were bare except for catkins and the first green at the tips of the hawthorn, and rooks argued in the elms above the harbor roofs. Sleet had fallen in the night and quit before dawn, and the light it left was thin and low. The two blocks in the basket on his back were the color of straw and smelled of last year's lime and old smoke, and they warmed slowly along his spine.

He had decided on politeness. He had settled it in the dark, as a cluster settles on a new queen, all at once and without asking anyone. He would say hello. He would set the wax where she told him, ask nothing, and climb the Rise again. He was sure of one morning's worth.

"You've got your polite face on," Tobiah said. It was the first thing he had said since the gate.

"I haven't a polite face."

"You wear it for the neighbors when they buy the clover. It's worse than the other one."

Ewan walked on. A boy of seventeen who whistled when he lied could still read a man's back.

At the bend the lane opened on Larkmere Water, and the Far Shore lay across it with the lime wood grey on its slope. His far hives sat under those bare branches, wrapped against the cold, and the hut beside them. The limes would open when the sun and the wet agreed. He had known them early and he had known them late, and no year had once consulted him. When the flow arrived the supers filled faster than one man could carry them, and the comb was cut that week, and whatever he was on the day of the cutting passed into the jar and up the Rise to the neighbors' tables. Then the crates had to cross the water, and one boat on Larkmere Water was broad enough to carry them, and she stood in the Net Sheds on her cradle with her heel gone soft.

The lantern hung on the seaward wall, its one green pane clouded with soot. It wanted wiping before dusk. He took his eyes off it and put them on the door.

A tune came out of the shed, low and flat, laid along the knock of a mallet. It broke off the moment his shadow crossed the floor.

"Hello, Bryony," he said.

The Heron filled the shed, a broad wooden ferry with a wheelhouse, built by Bryony's father. Two oak props stood wedged under her stern where there had been none the day before. Bryony stood beside them with the mallet in her left hand and the pale scar across the back of it, the flat pencil behind her ear, sawdust thick in her hair.

"Morning." She did not set the mallet down.

"You've the coast still in your hair," he said.

Politeness had lasted two words.

Her free hand rose toward her head and stopped short of it. "It's sawdust."

"Coast sawdust. It's paler."

"It's oak."

"Pale oak, then."

A twitch crossed her mouth and was put away fast. He knew a hive that had decided not to sting by the way it held still.

Boots scraped on the floor. Tobiah set his block on the bench and took his hands off it quickly, as if the wax had been hot. His ears had turned pink to the tips.

"Bryony," he said. He gave the name nothing to hold onto.

"Tobiah." She said it slowly, one hand held open, the way a person walks up to a hive after a long absence. His eyes dropped to the mallet and stayed there.

She crossed to the bench, took the nearest block and pressed her thumbnail into it. The nail drew down the face and the wax rolled up in a clean curl, with no grit and no drag. She held the block under her nose for the length of a breath.

"It's clean," she said.

"Through cloth twice," said Ewan, "and left to set slow."

"I can tell." Her thumb passed over the curl again.

It was only wax. It kept nothing of the hand that had cast it. He had always counted that a mercy in the trade, and he counted it again now, watching her thumb.

"I've hives to see to," Tobiah said, and whistled two notes through his teeth. He had never learned to lie with his mouth shut. He crossed the sill into the thin light with his shoulders set, and nobody stopped him.

Bryony watched the open door.

"He's taller than Father was," she said, to the lane.

"He ducks the lintel at the Honey House."

"Does he."

"Twice a week he forgets."

She nodded once and turned to the hull. The door stayed open on the lane, and neither of them closed it.

"The garboard's gone along the lap," she said. "I can't lift it and hold the bar. It wants two."

"Tell me where."

She showed him a stretch of planking low on the starboard side, where the seams ran dark and wept. A long iron bar leaned against the cradle, taller than she was, with a flattened foot. She hooked the foot behind the plank's lap and settled her hands on the bar at the height of her chest.

"Take the far end, past my hands. Lean when I say. Not before."

"Not before."

"Before and you'll take the sound wood with the rotten."

He had to stand behind her to reach. When he did the shed shrank to the width of the cradle and the back of her coat. He took the bar past her hands with his arms either side of hers, and her back met his chest through canvas and smoke-stiff wool, two coats laid flat against each other.

Her head fitted under his jaw. It fitted exactly, to the hair's breadth, at the height he had spent seven springs not measuring, and the measure had not changed. Her hair smelled of oak and the road. He stopped breathing so as not to stir it.

"Ready?" she said, to the plank.

"Yes."

"Lean."

He leaned. The bar bent a hair against the plank and nothing gave. A half note started in her throat, caught, and cut off.

"Again. Harder."

He gave it his weight. Sleet flung a handful at the roof and was gone. The fastenings let go one after another with a dry squeal, rust sifting down onto the floor, and the lap parted along its length with a long tearing sound, like capped comb pulled from its bar. The plank peeled outward and its last length snapped. Brown wood crumbled out of the seam, wet as tea leaves, and a smell followed it, cold and sour, of water kept in the dark.

Where the plank had been, the heel of the keel dropped. It dropped the width of a thumb onto the nearer prop with a knock he took in his own ribs.

Her hands closed on the bar. His closed beyond them. The prop took the weight, and the heel settled and stayed.

"Don't let go," she said.

"I haven't."

"Then don't." She slipped out under his arm, and the cold of the shed rushed into the place where she had been. She knelt at the prop with a mallet and a wedge and drove the wedge home, three blows, and knocked it with her knuckle, and listened. She drove a second wedge on the other side. She knocked that too. Then she set both palms flat on the prop and shoved with her whole weight, and the hull did not stir.

Out past the water doors the rotten ice at the lake's edge ground against the shingle. Ewan waited alone with the bar in his fists and nothing on the end of it, and counted the wedges she tested, and she tested the first one again.

"Now," she said.

He let go. His hands were slow to learn it.

She crouched at the opening and put her thumb to the frame ends behind where the plank had been. The wood there was black at the feet. Her mouth pressed flat.

"It's gone further than the keel," she said.

"How much further?"

"I'll know when I'm in it." She dug at the black with the awl, and it crumbled and clogged the point. "I need a flat blade, thin. Mine's too thick."

She stood, stepped in close, and reached round his hip without asking. Her fingers closed on the hive tool in his back pocket and drew it out. It was the oldest habit either of them had, and she did it the way she always had, quick and sure, and her knuckles pressed once against his hip through the wool.

He did not move. He had no other plan.

"It'll do," she said, and went down on one knee with his hive tool and scraped the black from the frame feet in long clean pulls. It was made for lifting propolis from a hive lid. It lifted the dead wood out of the Heron as well.

He stood with his hands still holding the shape of the bar. The basket still hung from his shoulders with a second block in it, and his arms had nothing to do. He set the basket on the bench.

"The limes will open," he said. "Not on any date. When the weather says. The crates have to cross the water, and the Heron is the only boat that can carry them."

"I know what she's for."

"I won't know until the hives tell me."

"Then they'll tell you." She did not look up from the frame. The awl sank to its ferrule, drew out, and sank again an inch along. "I'll be done when she's done."

It was the answer he had walked down the lane for. It was also every morning between now and the lime flow: the basket on his shoulders, the lane at this hour, this door standing open. A seam wants wax and a hull has more seams than a man can count, and he had meant to bring the first load and send the boy with the rest. He had meant to say that he understood.

He heard himself say the other word.

"Good."
