The swarm hummed against his hip all the way up the Rise, a low, close drone that carried through the straw and into his ribs, and Ewan kept his stride soft for it. A swarm that has settled wants nothing said to it. It wants a steady arm and a short road. He gave it both. His hands held the skep as they would have held it on any afternoon of any thaw, firm at the rim and easy underneath, and the whole way up he knew they were steady only because the bees needed them to be.

Under the steadiness they were not his. They had been at her collar. The smell of her coat had stayed in the creases of his fingers: wet wool, cold iron, the clean dust of cut oak, and under all of it, faint as pollen on a sill, the lake. He did not wipe them on his coat.

He had counted the bees into the skep as he always did, by sound, the way a man counts children in the next room by the noise they make. When the hum settled it came out one short. He had left a bee on her sleeve. He had seen it start toward her wrist and he had waited in the lane with the skep on his hip and watched it, and she had watched it, and neither of them had done anything about it at all. Then, because standing there any longer would have been a decision, he had turned and climbed.

One short. It would find its way home or it would not.

For seven springs he had practiced not hoping. It was a skill like any other, learned the way a hive learns to hold against robbers, by keeping the entrance small. One narrow door, and nothing came in to carry off what was stored. He had been skilled at it. The thaw came and the willow opened and the first bees flew out to it, spring on spring, and not once had he walked down to the lane to see who was on it. One swarm in the lane, and the narrow door stood wide.

The Rise was beginning its year. Hawthorn showed green at the tips along the hedge. The willow at the turn of the path was loud with early workers, bent to the catkins in the thin sun as if nothing in Larkmere had changed. The hives sat in their winter wraps along the terrace, and what came out of them was the low, drowsy sulk of a cold colony starting to stir.

Tobiah was at the gate of the hive yard with a hive tool in his fist and no work for it. He had been on the terrace wall long enough to see the whole lane. His veil hung off one shoulder, cut for a larger boy, as every veil he owned had been, and he grew faster than Ewan could cut cloth.

"That was Bryony," he said.

It was not a question. It was the flat voice he used for weather that had already done its damage. Pink had climbed both his ears.

"They landed on her," Ewan said. "I took them off."

"I saw what you took off."

Tobiah's eyes stayed on the skep. He did not whistle. He only did that when he lied, and he was not lying, which was worse. Ewan walked past him through the gate, and Tobiah fell in at his back, and the house had chosen a side without anyone saying so. It was not hers.

Ottoline stood in the door of the Honey House with the ring of keys at her belt and her hands folded in front of her. She wore the face of a woman who had been standing there all afternoon for no reason whatever. Her back was bent with age and her voice was not. It came across the yard upright as a stalk of mullein.

"Kettle's hot," she said.

"Aunt."

"It's no good letting a swarm sit in the light. Set it in the kitchen out of the draft."

He set it on the flagstones by the stove with a cloth over its mouth, where it hummed to itself in the dark, and Tobiah leaned in the doorway, and Ottoline poured tea into three cups. Ewan did not touch his. His hands wanted work and the cup was not it.

"Who wrote to her?"

Ottoline stirred honey into her own cup and tapped the spoon twice on the rim.

"The Heron's keel is soft at the heel," she said. "The Heron is Fairlie built. There are not many ways to read that."

"You might have said."

"You might have asked, any spring of the seven."

He waited for the next part, the part where she explained herself. It did not come. Ottoline drank her tea. Under the cloth by the stove the swarm had picked up his tone and pitched its own a half step higher, a thin, wary whine. He made himself breathe out. The whine eased.

He turned her answer over, hunting for the flaw in it, the way he turned a frame to the light to find a cracked cell, and there was none. A ferry with a soft keel wants the hands that made her. There was nobody else to send for. He had known that for seven springs and had refused to know it, and now an old woman with a ring of keys had said it over a teapot and the knowing sat in the kitchen like a third cup.

He could not be angry. That was the trouble. He had stood in front of plenty of weather in his life and could generally find the front it came from. This one had come from the single person who had stayed, who had raised him, and who had waited seven springs for him to ask. There was nowhere to put it.

"Does she know where the kitchen is?" Tobiah said from the doorway.

"The loft over the Net Sheds is aired," said Ottoline, to nobody, in the voice she used for the likelihood of rain. "The stove draws. There are blankets."

"You aired the loft."

"It wanted airing."

"The swarm will want a hive," he said, and left them to the tea.

The empty hive stood in the second row, scoured with thyme and waiting since autumn. He propped a board against its mouth and carried the skep over and shook it once, hard, so that the bees fell out in a soft brown rope and began to walk up the board and into the dark. It was the oldest work he knew. His hands knew every motion of it and still shook, a fine tremor in the knuckles that began the moment the skep was empty and there was nothing left in them to hold. He closed them. He opened them. Tobiah had followed him out and waited at the end of the row, watching the bees go in.

"Is she staying?"

Ewan watched the last of them cross the board. He counted them in again, by ear, and again he came out one short.

"She's here for the Heron."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's what I know."

Tobiah turned the hive tool over in his fist once and put it in his belt, and he stalked up the path toward the Honey House without another word, boots hard on the wet ground, and Ewan lingered in the yard with the bees going in.

The Honey House was cool and stone-floored and smelled of wax and old summers. The jars stood in their rows along the shelves, each with a paper strip tied at the neck, each written by hand with the flower and the weather of the day it was cut: clover, dry and bright; heather, wind from the north; lime, thunder at dusk. Every jar in the room was a day. The town came up the Rise to buy a mild afternoon or a hard frost, and they tasted what he had been, and he had never once tasted it himself. His own spoon found nothing in any jar but honey. That was the law of it. A beekeeper learned what he had put into the comb only from other people's faces.

He waited in the middle of the floor with the smell of her coat still in his hands.

The top shelf held one jar that was not like the others. A squat glass jar of dark honey, sealed with a disc of Marrick wax, a strip of blue linen tied round the neck, turned so the blank side faced out. The side that faced the room had no flower on it and no weather. He had turned it that way himself, and in all the springs since he had never turned it back.

He did not know what was in it. He had arranged his life so he would not have to.

He lifted one hand toward the shelf. It rose an inch and the fingers began to shake again, and he lowered it to his side and stood looking at the blank glass.

Behind him a board creaked. He did not turn. He knew the tread of that step, and the careful silence of a boy trying not to be heard, and he knew that Tobiah stood in the doorway with his veil hanging off his shoulder and his ears gone pink to the tips, watching Ewan not touch the jar.
