Bryony woke in the loft window with her neck set at an angle no joiner would have passed, and the first act of her day was to decide that nothing had happened in the night.

The stove had burned down to a grey crust behind its door. The green square from the lantern had gone off the boards, and the lake beyond the glass was white under the early light with a skin of mist on it. A curl of oak lay open in her palm, her father's hand running along it in grey lead. Her fingers closed on it, and she stood up one joint at a time, and found that the collar of her coat held a thread of smoke that was not the stove's.

She brushed at the collar and stopped brushing. Turning it up against her jaw, she climbed down the stair, and the third tread complained, and she told it to mind its own business.

She was brisk. Brisk was a way of working she knew as well as a plane stroke. She washed her face at the water door, ate the end of yesterday's bread standing, tied the paring into her tool roll beside the letter without opening either, and had the first plank of new oak up on the trestles before the sun cleared the Rise.

It was her father's oak, sticked and turned every autumn and dry to the heart, and it took a line cleanly. She laid a batten along the course the garboard had to follow from the stem to the sternpost, pinned it to the frames, and drew the curve onto the plank in one long pull. Then she drew it again from the sternpost end. The two lines parted by a hair's width at the middle. She scraped both off with the edge of a chisel and drew a third from the stem, slowly, and a fourth from the stern, until they lay on each other and she could not tell which was which.

The shed stayed quiet. She let it. Blossom drifted in through the water doors on the morning wind, pink at the edges, and settled on the shavings and on the black flank of the old keel where it lay on its balks.

Keys rang on the shingle, loud this time, one deliberate step to a note. Yesterday the old woman had muffled them with her palm to arrive unannounced. Today she wished to be heard coming, and Bryony set her pencil to the line again and pressed it into the grain and did not turn round.

Ottoline crossed the threshold on her stick and did not sit. She had a way of standing that meant what she had to say would be short and would not be improved by argument. Her glance climbed the stair to the loft, rested on the stovepipe, the last warmth still wavering the air above it, and dropped to the plank on the trestles.

"The stove's warm," she observed.

"It draws."

"I said it did."

That was the whole of what either of them offered on the subject of the loft.

Ottoline planted the stick. "I wrote it."

Bryony did not stop. The line ran on to its end. "Wrote what."

"The letter. It's in that roll." Ottoline nodded at the canvas on the bench. "A person who will not answer a letter keeps it in whatever she carries everywhere."

Bryony laid the pencil on the plank, parallel to the line, exactly square. It was the most careful act of her morning.

"I knew it wasn't him," Bryony told the plank.

"It was not. I saw to that." The old woman's knuckles whitened on the stick and eased again. "He knows now. He worked it out at the swarm, and he has not spoken one word to me about it, which is how I know he is furious. I have never been so well served. He pours my tea before I reach for the pot."

"Why?"

"Because he would have forbidden it." There was no apology in the voice. It was the voice that had told her about the jar, flat and certain and delivered whether it was wanted or not. "He'd have stood in the lane with his hat in his hands and told me you had a life on the coast and nobody had the right to call you out of it, and he would have meant every word. He would have been right. He is nearly always right, and it has bought him nothing."

"So you wrote behind him."

"I wrote what was so. The Heron's keel is soft at the heel. The Heron is Fairlie built. I wrote it four times, and the first three had the word home in them, and I burned each one in the stove."

"You burned them."

"The stove draws."

The corner of Bryony's mouth twitched, and she put a stop to it. She could see it all the same: the old woman at a table with a pen and a sheet of paper, burning the word home out of her letter one draft at a time. It was a tender picture. Bryony was too angry for tenderness, and set it down as she would set down a hot iron.

She had it by heart already. She had read it once at her bench on the coast and it had not needed a second reading: two sentences in an upright hand, and under them a title where a name belonged. The keeper of the Net Sheds. No word of home. No word of him. She had gone over the white paper beneath the two lines more often than she had gone over the lines.

"You used my father's boat."

"Yes."

Ottoline did not drop her eyes and did not soften it. That was the worst of her. She never dressed a fact for company.

Heat climbed Bryony's throat and sat behind her teeth. She took up the pencil, because a hand with nothing in it gives the game away, and turned to the water doors so the old woman should not watch her face do whatever it was going to do.

Along the shore a man was crossing the shingle with a long board on his shoulder.

He had walked out of the Fairlie yard, where the door stood open as it had stood since the first oak was hauled out of it, and he walked toward the sheds along the line of the water with the board riding level and his hand steadying the forward end. His sleeves were pushed to the elbow. Even at that distance she could find the stings on his left forearm, white and pink and scattered, and the new welt above the wrist. He kept the board true without a glance at it, as a man does who has learned its heft by carrying it.

He did not turn his head toward the sheds. She could tell, because a man who is not looking walks with his whole back. Blossom fell across the shingle in a thin slant and caught in his hair, and he did not brush it out. The line of his shoulders under the load ran in one long fall from neck to elbow, and she traced it before she could stop herself, down to the bunched sleeve at his arm. She knew how warm the back of that neck ran at the end of a day's work.

Her fist closed on the pencil until it broke with a dry click, and the stub bit into her palm.

She turned her face to the plank on the trestles, to the two lines laid as one and the grey scar her broken lead had dragged across the new wood. She would have to scrape the whole stretch again.

"That'll be the pencil," Ottoline remarked. She had not once turned her head to the doors, and she knew exactly what Bryony had seen there.

"It was a poor pencil."

"It was a sound pencil." The old woman let that stand for the space of a breath. "Seven springs is long enough. For both of you."

"It wasn't yours to say."

"No. It was mine to write." The stick shifted on the boards. "I counted them, one for each year. I am not young enough to waste another."

"You could have asked me to come."

"You would have said no."

"And if the keel had been sound?"

"Then I would have written about the roof." Ottoline's mouth thinned. "The boat is soft at the heel, and that is a fact, and I did not invent it. The rest is meddling. I do not pretend otherwise."

Bryony opened her mouth and shut it again. It was true, and she hated it for being true. She had read the letter once and left it by the oil can and walked round it for the whole dark of the year, and if it had asked her for anything but a keel she would have burned it with the shavings.

That was what she could not forgive. The two lines had been cut to her measure, as a plank is cut to a frame, and they had fitted. Ottoline had not lied to her. Ottoline had read her correctly, and used what she read.

She took the letter out of the roll, opened it on the bench and flattened it with her palm, though the paper had gone soft at every fold and would hardly lie flat. Its two sentences ran in that upright hand. The Heron's keel is soft at the heel. The Heron is Fairlie built. A grey smudge from her palm marked the margin.

Two warnings, one in ink on a folded sheet and one in lead on the face of a joint no eye had seen. Both of them were about the heel. Until now she had not noticed it.

She thought of the coast yard. She could not help it, any more than she could help the weather. At the coast yard nobody had ever used a boat on her. Her bench stood as she had left it, with the oil can beside it, and her master would have called what Ottoline had done a fair stroke of work and told her so, for Abel Trent had a measure for everything and the measure for this was fair. He had also walked past that sheet of paper every morning of the dark of the year and never once asked what was in it. The road to the coast lay dry and open under its drift of blossom, and she could be on it before the petals had finished falling.

She took up the roll and put the strap across her shoulder.

Ottoline did not stir. "The road's dry. I'll not stand in it."

"I can see that."

"If you go, I'll think none the worse of you. The Heron will be in the shed in the morning, and so will I."

She gave it flat, with no more weight than a forecast, and it was worse than a plea. Bryony waited with the strap biting across her collarbone and the page open on the bench behind her, and glanced at the keel on its balks, black and heavy, the oak her father had chosen. It was soft at the heel. That was not Ottoline's doing. Ottoline had only said so.

She lifted the strap off her shoulder and hung the roll back on its nail. The smoke at her collar rose with the movement, faint and stale and not her own, and she held still in it for the length of a breath.

"I'll finish her," she said.

"That is all I asked."

"It isn't. But it's all I'll give."

"Then it's what I'll take." Ottoline turned for the doors. At the threshold she stopped, her back bent and her chin level, and for a moment she might have added a word. She did not. She lifted a hand to the ring at her belt and let the keys speak once against her hip, and she crossed the shingle in the blossom and the light.

Bryony took up a chisel and worked the ruined stretch, long strokes with the grain, each one lifting a grey curl of lead and wood. She kept at it until the keys had thinned along the shore and were gone.

When she glanced at the doors again, a board stood against the jamb, leaned square, its end resting on a folded sack so the shingle should not mark it. She had not heard him come. She had not heard him go.

Bryony laid the chisel down. The letter lay open on the bench as she had left it, and she picked it up, and the paper lay soft as cloth against her palm.
