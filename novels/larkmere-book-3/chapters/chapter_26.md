The second row was crowded before the dew was off it.

He had known it would be. The flow had run on through the dry mornings and the supers filled faster than he could sort them, and every hive on the terrace held more bees than it had cells for. They hung out of the entrances in beards. They walked over the landing boards and over one another, rose into the air and dropped back, with the restless, sweet sound of a colony that has begun to talk about leaving. He went down the row with his palm on each roof. Low, then lower, then at the sixth hive a thin edge at the top of the note, which he marked and walked on from. Swarm weather gives no notice. It gives hints and a clear sky.

Tobiah's veil hung on the gate post.

It hung by one corner, as it did every morning when the boy took it off to wash at the pump, a long grey ruin of netting that had been Ewan's before it was his. But the pump handle was dry. The scales hung from their hook with nothing on them, and the second row's supers stood unweighed on the stand.

Ewan stayed a moment with his hand on the post. He had slept badly. At the end of the evening a fiddle had stopped and his hand had stayed where it was, and she had let it, and then she had said goodnight at the Sheds door with the roll still across her back. All the way there he had watched her not say a sentence. He did not know what it was. He knew only that it had been a whole one, and that she had put it away the way a bee puts away a load it has decided not to bring home.

The door of the Honey House stood open.

He saw the boy from the end of the yard. Tobiah sat on the stone step with his knees drawn up and his boots in the dust and a brown boot-box on his thighs, tied with twine, the lid gone soft at the corners from seven springs of being pushed under a bed. He held it with both arms round it as he held a frame of brood he had been told to carry and had not been told he could put down. His ears were their ordinary color. Ewan took that for a mercy and a bad sign.

He did not hurry. A beekeeper learns to cross a yard so that nothing in it lifts. He crossed the last of the grass slowly, with his hands open and low, and lowered himself onto the step at the boy's left, a hand's width off, and set his palm flat on the stone beside him. It was already warm. The sun had been on it since it rose.

Neither spoke.

The hum came up from the terrace behind them and settled over the yard, the same note it had held all week, thickening. Elder had come into flower along the lane wall, and the smell of it lay on the air, sweet and a little sour, and under that the cooler smell of wax from the open door at their backs. He did not turn round. He had never sat with his back to that door and felt nothing, and he did not now. The top shelf kept its place behind him as a held breath keeps its place in a chest.

"I never opened them," Tobiah said.

He said it to the lake. He had not turned his head.

"I know."

"You've never asked."

"No."

"Why not?"

It was a fair question, and Ewan found that he had no answer made. He had not asked because it was not his to ask. He had not wanted to hear the answer, whichever it was. And the box under the boy's bed was the way a house learns to walk round a loose board, until the board is part of the house.

"Why not?" he said instead, and turned it gently back. "Why have you not opened them?"

Tobiah's arms tightened on the box.

"If I opened them she'd be gone for real."

He said it flatly, laying it down in a row with his other words. Ewan had heard him say hard things since he was ten, and the boy had never once said one that came as straight from the middle of him as this.

He had no reply. Anything he said would have been either a comfort that was not true or a truth that would sting, and in either case it would have been a lid. So he did what he knew. He stayed where he was. He kept his hand on the stone, and he let the boy hear him not leave.

A while passed. A swift went over, high and screaming, and the hum behind them rose and fell.

"As long as they're shut she's still writing them," Tobiah said. "Do you see? She's still on her way. There's one coming. There's always one more coming. If I read them all, that's the last of it. It's finished. And she's only a person who wrote to me once."

"She's still here."

"For now."

That was the truth, and it sat on the step beside them like a third person, and Ewan did not dispute it. He looked down the Rise to find a place for his eyes.

From the Honey House step the whole of the lower town lay open to him in the clear morning, the roofs and the cobbles and the long grey tongue of the quay, the water behind it going from pewter to a pale blue as the light climbed. West of the harbor the shore bent. At the end of the bend, small as a hive door, stood the open door of the Fairlie yard, and in it a figure bent over a board.

He had not looked for her. He had looked down the hill, and the hill had put her where she was.

She had her sleeves pushed to the elbow. At this distance he could see no more than the pale flash of her forearms going forward and drawing back, steady and long, the whole of the stroke in the shoulder and none of it in the wrist. He knew those forearms. He knew the underside of them, where the skin was the color of cream and the sun had never found it, and the line of the tendon that stood up when she gripped. The knowledge came to him whole and from a long way off, and he pressed his hand flat against the stone until the grit marked the heel of it.

He wanted her, here on the step, with a boy beside him holding a box of her letters. It sat under his grief like a stove banked under a cold room. He could not tell the two apart any more, or tell which of them was keeping him still.

"You're looking at her," Tobiah said.

"I'm looking at the lake."

"The lake's the other way."

Ewan said nothing. After a moment he took his eyes off the yard door.

"She talks to you," Tobiah said. "You never talk to her. Not about anything. You hand her wax and she hands you a plank, and everybody in the house knows. Ottoline knows. The man by the bollards knows." His voice had gone low and bitter and almost steady. "I can tell from the jars. I could smell her on that pan before she'd even been up the hill. Everyone's had a spoon of you. All of them. Ottoline, me, Bryony, the whole quay. Everyone's tasted you but you."

The words went in as a sting goes in, narrow and exact. Ewan did not move.

"Open yours," said Tobiah.

He did not have to say which. They both knew what stood on the top shelf of the Honey House with the door open behind them: a squat glass jar of dark honey, sealed with a disc of Marrick wax, a strip of blue linen tied round the neck, turned so the blank side faced out. It had stood there while the boy grew a hand taller. It had stood there through every winter of her absence, and it had never once been wrong about anything, because it had never once been tasted.

"It's not that simple," Ewan said.

"Why not?"

"It is not a letter. I can't open it and read it." He heard his own voice come out even and a little dry, the voice he used on a hive he did not like the sound of. "I can't taste it. You know that. None of us can taste our own. I would have to put a spoon in somebody's mouth and stand and watch their face, and know what it was from what their face did."

"So?"

"So it was cut on that morning."

He had not said that aloud to anyone. Ottoline knew, and had told it to Bryony in a shed, and he had never been told what she said. The boy only looked at him, with plain curiosity, for the first time in a long while.

"I don't know what I put in it," Ewan said. "I've been afraid for seven springs that I do. And that whoever tastes it will know it before I do."

It was as much as he could manage. He had meant it to be less. He sat with his hand on the stone and heard it settle in the bright air above the yard. A hive on the third row set up a thin high note and held it, and let it go.

Tobiah looked at him a long moment. His ears had begun to color at the rims.

"You coward," he said.

He did not say it with heat. He said it as a man says the name of a plant he has been walking past for a long time without looking at. It was not an insult. It was a diagnosis, and Ewan took it as one.

He had no answer. A bee does not argue with the weather. He sat with the word on him as the warmth was on the stone, and he knew it was true, and it did not make him smaller. It made him exactly the size he was.

The boy looked down again. He had a way of looking at things he was afraid of, with his head bent and his shoulders square, and he turned the box a little on his thighs so that the knot faced him. It was a plain knot. It was the one Ewan had taught him in his first summer for tying a super down against the wind, a turn and a turn and a tuck, and it had never once been undone in all that time.

Ewan knew how to undo it. He had guided the boy's hands through it the first summer, and shown him the way back.

He did not say so.

Down the hill, small and exact in the doorway of her father's yard, Bryony straightened, rolled her shoulders and bent again. Her stroke went on. The hum thickened round the hives. The elder smell came and went on the breath of the lane, and the whole Rise leaned toward noon with every hive on it deciding.

Tobiah put out his thumb and laid it on the twine, at the knot, and held it there.

He did not pull.
