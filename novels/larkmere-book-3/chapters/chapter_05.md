Rain blew in off the lake before light and settled in for the day. It sounded on the long roof of the Net Sheds, flat and even, and the damp drew the smell out of the Heron, a broad wooden ferry with a wheelhouse, built by her father. She smelled of oak and old pitch, and under them of the sour cold of wood that had stood wet far longer than anyone had known.

Bryony knelt in the gap where the garboard had been and set the awl to the frame feet in turn. The point sank to the ferrule at every foot, forward to the turn of the bilge and aft toward the sternpost. The black rose out of each foot and up into the frame, two hands high at the worst and higher forward, and at the forward end she did not need the awl. Her fingertips sank in alone. The strake above the garboard took the point at its lower edge as readily as the frames had.

She sat back on her heels. The list she had made the first morning, garboards sprung and stem scarfed back and every seam opened to the pitch, had covered half the work. She reckoned it again from the stem and the total grew, and she stopped.

Her tool roll held what a boatwright takes on the road: the awl, a mallet, a bevel gauge, two chisels a finger too thick for this, a spokeshave, a plane the length of her forearm, the drawknife. It had no adze. A frame foot wants an adze, and so does every scarf she meant to cut, and a ferry this broad wants oak that has dried under a roof for years. Green oak would shrink in her seams and let the lake into the new work by the next thaw. The nearest seasoned oak she trusted stood in the coast yard, and she had walked away from that.

Her father's yard held both, or had when she left.

His hive tool was where she had dropped it the evening before, on the second tread of the stage ladder. It was thinner than either chisel and she was not finished with it. She put it in her coat, took the mallet, and let herself out.

The shore path led past the water doors and out onto the quay, and nobody stood on it. The harbor lanterns hung unlit on their posts. Rain dimpled the harbor, and the old ice at the lake's edge had turned the color of wet slate and ground at the stones with every swell. The wet had worked the sawdust in her hair to paste. Shutters were closed along Lantern Street, chimneys smoked, and no door opened to see who walked past.

Beyond the quay the path narrowed to a rut between the shingle and a bank of rust-brown bracken, and she kept her eyes on her boots. Seven springs of frost and thaw take a roof apart one nail at a time. She had told herself so on the thaw road and again the whole way down the shore, in the voice she used to read a cut list: boards curled, the ridge opened, rain on the floor, the oak gone to punk. If the yard had failed she had no adze and no seasoned stock, and a Heron with her keel soft at the heel, and a boatwright standing over her with a roll of tools that could not finish the work.

The yard door was weathered and swollen in its frame, set in the end of a long low shed whose slip doors faced the water. A hasp crossed the post, and the padlock on it had rusted red to the shackle. She kept her eyes level.

She slid the flat foot of the hive tool under the hasp plate where it met the post, held it steady in her left hand with the scar pale across the back of it, and tapped the other end with the mallet. A split post would be hers to mend, and she would mend it, but not today. The screws had rusted into the wood and let go in turn with a squeal, and the plate lifted away whole with the screws still in it. She set it on the sill with the padlock so it could be fitted again where it belonged.

The door dragged on its sill. She put her shoulder to it, and it gave with a groan, and the smell of the yard met her.

Old oak first, the dry sweetish smell of timber given years to let go of its water. Pitch under that. Dust, and cold, and nothing more. No mildew, no sour wet, no rot. She had breathed rot all morning, and her nose knew its absence the way her hand would have known a missing tool in the roll.

She had not stood on this sill since the morning she took the thaw road. The light from the doorway cut a bar across a floor furred with dust, and her boot printed the first mark in it.

She crossed the floor slowly and the dust took her prints. The long bench stood under the shuttered window with the vise at one end, its jaws shut on nothing, and the dust lay across it thick enough to write in. On the racks the planes sat in their slots, the chisels in their row, the saws hung by their handles. A dull brown bloom covered the steel, nothing a stone would not lift, and nothing had pitted. At the far wall planks were stacked on stickers to the rafters, layer on layer.

She drew a board out by its end, sighted down it, and let it drop against the stack. It rang. She drew another from lower in the pile and let that drop too, because one board proved nothing about a stack, and it rang as well.

Her father's try plane sat in its slot on the rack. She lifted it out, leaving a clean shape in the dust, and turned the sole to the light from the doorway. The iron showed at the mouth with a notch in its edge the width of a barley grain, and she knew the line it cut. She had followed that line along every frame in the Heron, from stem to sternpost. She held the plane out from habit, toward the far side of the bench. The far side held the cold.

She set the plane down in its own outline, square, and at last tipped her head back.

The rafters were bare and grey. The planking over them was dark from the eave to the ridge, and she searched the whole slope for a water stain and found none. A stain on a board has a shape, a tide mark with a darker rim, and this planking was clean. The rain overhead made one sound. A roof that has failed makes two, the rain on it and the water in the room.

She searched the slope again, because one pass proves little, and found the pale boards. They showed here and there among the weathered ones, new timber the color of tallow. In the oldest patch, down by the eave and silvered nearly to the wood around it, three nails had skewed out of line and broken through the sides of the rafters, and one had curled over on itself where it emerged and never been cut off, the work of a hand that had not yet learned the heft of a hammer. The patch above it showed one point. The patch above that showed none, and in the newest, up at the ridge, each board met its rafter square and tight.

Roofs do not stay shut on their own. Every spring of the seven a ladder had stood against this wall and a hammer had climbed it, and the planking had been renewed and nailed hard down against the thaw while she planed other men's strakes at the coast, and she had walked the shore this morning expecting standing water on the floor and had reached this door without once raising her head to the planking that sheltered her timber and her father's tools.

She reached for a word and found only trade words. Sound. Tight. They fit a roof. They did not fit whoever had kept it, and she stopped searching.

Boots crunched on the shingle outside. The hinge she had freed cried out, and yellow light swung in across the floor and stopped at her feet.

She did not turn.

"The door's open," Ewan said.

"I opened it."

"I know. I saw it from the shore path." He stayed on the sill. Water ran off the brim of his hat and dripped onto the hasp plate she had set there, and the tin lantern in his hand hung low, so that the light lay across the floor and picked out her prints as far as the bench.

"You're letting the wet in," she said, to the rafters.

"It's dry in here."

"I know it is."

He stepped in then, and woodsmoke rode in with him over the smell of the oak. He set the lantern at the end of the bench and swept a place for it with the edge of his hand, and his thumb pressed down in the dust beside it and left a whorl with every ridge clear, wax packed under the nail. She kept her eyes on it until he straightened.

"Who's been mending the roof?" she said.

"Tobiah and I."

The rain filled the quiet after it.

"Tobiah goes up," Ewan said. "I hold the ladder and have opinions."

She thought of the bent nail by the eave, and of the boy who had driven it, and kept that to herself.

Above the bench her father's adze hung on its peg with the edge to the wall, the ash of the handle worn dark where a hand had sat. She reached for it. Without being asked Ewan lifted the lantern and held it up beside the peg, and her breath smoked in the light. When her fingers closed on the handle, the wood on the lantern's side was warm.

She took it down and turned it to the flame and tried the edge twice, once across the ball of her thumb and once against the end grain of the bench, and it wanted a stone. When she glanced up he was holding the lantern steady at arm's length and was not watching the adze. He held the look a moment past polite, and she turned hers to the edge again.

She opened her mouth.

"It wants a stone," she said.

"Yes," said Ewan. He let that be the whole answer.

She took the hive tool out of her coat and put it on the bench, handle toward him, and turned away to the racks. Behind her she heard him take it up, and the soft scrape of it sliding into the old worn place in his back pocket.

"I'll leave you the lantern," he said at the door.

"I didn't ask for it."

"No."

He pulled the door to behind him and left it a hand's width open on the weather. His boots ground over the shingle until the downpour covered them.

She kept the adze in her fist. The warmth drained out of the handle from the lantern side first, then from the rest. Overhead the rain drummed on the planking, steady, and not a drop reached the floor.
