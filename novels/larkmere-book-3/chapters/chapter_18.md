The hives told him before the sky did.

He was strapping the last roof on the terrace when the note changed. All afternoon it had run low and even, the sound of a yard at work, and now it drew in on itself, higher and tighter, the whole yard deciding at once to stay home. Foragers dropped onto the landing boards in ones and twos and walked straight in without a rest. He cut every entrance down to a finger's width, laid a flat stone on each lid and hauled the strap over it, and by the end his forearms ran with old stings and new rain.

Over the Far Shore the cloud that had stayed away all day had stopped staying. It had turned the yellow-grey of old comb, and under it the lake was white at the edges. The orchard shivered from the crowns down. Blossom lifted off the boughs in sheets, and none of it fell.

The house behind him had its shutters barred and its stove banked. He had seen to that first. Then he put on his coat, which she had handed back at noon with the collar turned out and no word spoken about it, and lifted the taper from its hook and the orchard way down, because the lantern would not light itself.

On the shingle the wind laid the flame flat along the wick before he reached the wall. He turned his back to the lake, shut his coat round the taper and tried again, and again. The fourth try caught. He cupped the flame to the little iron door, worked it inside and shut the catch with a thumb that shook, and the green panel lit thin and steady behind its glass. Then the wind found the lantern, and it began to swing on its hook. The green light slid out across the stones and in again, out and in, the way a forager swings before a hive it has lost.

The side door of the Net Sheds stood a hand's width open on its latch, with a thread of lamplight lying across the shingle from it. He ought to have knocked. He shouldered the door and was inside.

The noise stopped him. Rain struck the roof at the seaward end like a fist on a kettle, and at the landward end the wind pushed on the wall and the wall pushed back, so that the whole long shed breathed. Under all of it ran a tick, slow and dry.

Bryony stood beside the Heron's cradle with her palm flat on the third frame and her head turned, one ear to the hull. Her lamp hung from a nail on a prop. She was not working. Her boots were planted wide in the shavings, and she wore the face of a keeper at a hive wall in the cold, ear to the wood, reading a note he could not hear.

"Door," she said, without turning.

He pulled it to behind him and dropped the latch.

Her ear stayed where it was. "She's talking."

"What does she say?"

"I don't know yet."

The tick ran again from the stern forward, the way a knock passes down a row of hives when the first lid is rapped. The shed met a gust broadside. The tick answered a hair louder. Her lips worked once, and he understood that she was counting gaps.

She pulled a straightedge from the trestle and laid it across the frames amidships, and kept her lamp low behind it. He saw no light under the edge at the second frame, none at the third. At the fourth there was a thread no wider than a hair, where an hour ago, by her face, there had been none.

"Is it failing?"

"It's a hull that has never had weather on her." She gave it flat, like a measure. "Nothing wrong with her has been proved. Nothing right has either."

He had learned in these weeks that she would not say a hull would hold until she had watched it hold. He had not known until now what the refusing cost her.

"Tell me what to do with my hands," he said.

She did, in short words and an order he would never have chosen. He knocked wedges home under the seaward props with the mallet she handed him, four on a side, and she followed down the row with her palm, testing each, and rapped the cradle once at the bow. Then the roof gave over the seaward valley. A braid of water as thick as his finger dropped out of the dark onto the new garboard, onto the wax in the lap, and she used a word the Sheds had heard in their day and that Ottoline would never have allowed under her keys. He hauled a sack from the rafter pile and threw it over the new planking. She had a bucket under the braid before he straightened.

"The wax is holding," he said.

"The wax is wax." She did not look up. "Ask me again when it has been wet a week."

Lightning flashed white through every crack in the boards, and the thunder was already on top of it. Under the thunder he heard another sound, out on the harbor, wooden and final, like a hive lid flung against stone. She was at the foot of the stair as the light faded.

"The loft window. I want to see the wall." She was already on the first tread.

She lifted her tool roll from the trestle by its thong as she passed and carried it up with her, the way a keeper carries the one frame of brood out of a flooded hive. He followed. The stove ticked in its corner. The roof had drenched the cot an hour ago, and its blankets lay in a wet heap against the chimney. The window rattled in its casing, and the blossom had pasted a skin over the glass. She wiped a patch clear with her sleeve.

The next flash showed them the harbor in one piece, white as a plate. The lake stood up against the quay in ridges. At the foot of the steps a rowing boat that had lain moored since autumn hung by its painter from a single post, bows to the sky, and then the painter parted. The boat rode a wave up the shingle and dropped onto the stones, and the sound reached them a breath after: a crack, and a long wet splintering.

She spoke to the glass. "That was somebody's boat."

"The man with the nails. It was his."

She did not answer. Her hand lay flat on the sill, and he watched her fingers read the wood as they had read the frame. Each ridge on the lake rode a little higher up the shingle than the last.

"If that comes over the stones, it's under the cradle."

"It has never come over the stones in my life."

"Then it has never had a reason."

Lightning again, and in its light her face had lost the yellow of the lamp. Under the set of her jaw he saw a woman working a sum she did not want the answer to. He opened his mouth to tell her she would hold, which he did not know and she would not have forgiven, and shut it.

The next sound was not thunder. It broke from the landward end of the loft, a crack so near that the floor jumped under his boots, and the whole long room lurched like a hive knocked off its stand. Grit filled the air. Boards split with a roar, and the black wet arm of the shore willow thrust through the gable above the stair head with the rain driven in behind it.

He caught her sleeve and drew her clear of the window, and she let him. The limb struck the head of the flight and drove on down, and the flight fell with it, tread after tread, in one long groan that ended on the shed floor. Then there was only the wind pouring in at the hole, and the rain, and the stove ticking on.

She was on her knees at the edge while the dust still hung. He crossed to her and knelt, and put out a hand, and did not use it. Below them lay a drop of more than a man's height to the shavings. Her lamp still burned on its nail, swinging. The limb lay across the trestles beyond the Heron's stern, and its broken end stopped a forearm's length short of the transom. The sack he had thrown over the planking had held, and the water still dropped onto it in its steady braid.

"A forearm," she said, to the dark below, and then, as she would give a measure, "Ewan."

She leaned out over the edge as far as the boards would take her. He put his hand into the back of her belt and hung on, and she did not shake it off. After a long time she settled on her heels.

"The sack held. The props stood. The cradle stood." She listed it flat. "It never reached far enough to tell me anything."

"There's tarred line in the wedge box," he said. "We could go down on that."

"Onto a floor of splinters and broken stair, in the dark, in this." She wiped the wet from her face with her wrist. "Morning."

"Morning," he agreed.

Neither of them glanced at the other while they agreed to it.

The gable hole let the storm in over the landward third of the loft. Rain drove across the boards in sheets, ran to the gap where the stair had been and dripped through it in a ragged chorus. They withdrew to the lake end, where the stove stood. One blanket hung dry over the stove rail, grey and scorched at the hem, and he lifted it down.

"There's only one." She nodded at the blanket.

"It's a wide one." He offered a corner with the care he gave a frame full of bees. She accepted it, shook it once to see what lived in it, and sat with her back to the wedge box. He sat beside her and drew the blanket across the two of them.

His shoulder lay against hers through two layers of wet wool, and his knee against her knee, and neither of them did anything about it. The iron ticked at their feet. The loft smelled of pitch and scorched wool and the smoke in his coat, and the air that blew in at the gable tasted of cold iron and crushed petals. Her tool roll leaned on the wedge box by her hip, tied, with the pocket behind it pressed to her side and everything the pocket kept. He kept his eyes on the stove. So did she.

He noticed the breathing a while after it had begun. Hers drew as his drew. He tried to break it, drew early, held late, and in three breaths they had found each other again, slow and level and in step, and he let them.

"Did the straps hold?" she asked the stove.

"The stones will have."

"What do they do, in this?"

"Sit on the honey and keep their heads down. Nobody leaves in weather."

"Sensible."

"Mostly."

She breathed out through her nose, which in her was a laugh, and the blanket stirred. Thunder rolled again, further off, and she did not flinch at it, and neither did he. The lake had a roar of its own that ran on under the rain and the thunder, a sound with no gaps in it. He thought of the hives on the terrace under their stones, every colony on the terrace clustered round its stores with the whole winter's patience in it, and found that the thought had steadied his hands. In the firelight he saw that her hands, in her lap under the wool, had stopped holding on to each other.

When the wind fell for a moment he thought of the tarred line in the wedge box behind his head, and let the thought go by like a bee that has not decided. She mentioned nothing. Neither of them spoke of morning again.

"My father built this loft for Ottoline's winters," she said.

"I know."

It was not what she had meant by it, and they both let it stand.

"He built the stair too," she said, after a while.

"I'd noticed it was missing."

The breath left her nose again, longer this time. She let her head tip to the wedge box. The shoulder at his did not draw away, and he did not ask it to.

Morning would come with the light through the gable hole and the gap where the stair had been, and she would be on the floor below with a straightedge before he had his boots on. He knew it as he knew frost. He wanted the night longer, and he did not say that either.

The thunder walked off up the lake, grumbling, and the rain thinned from a drumming to a patter. On the shingle the surge ebbed stone by stone. Below them the braid of water on the sack thinned to a thread and then to single drops, slow as a hive in the first cold. The wind dropped to a breath. Blossom stuck to the patch of glass she had wiped clear, and beyond it, outside, the lantern had stopped swinging. It hung straight on its hook and burned on the seaward wall, green and steady, the only light left on the shore.
