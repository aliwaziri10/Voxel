The Rise had not been quiet since the storm.

The sound came down the hill all morning on the wind, a low surge that rose and fell like surf on a far shore, and it filled the Net Sheds until she stopped hearing it and then, every so often, heard it all at once. She was bent over the edge of the second plank with the plane, taking it down to the line, when a shadow crossed the doorway and the light changed on the shavings.

Without looking up she knew how he stood in a door by now: weight on one leg, one shoulder to the frame, the way of a man who has learned that entering a room fast frightens whatever is in it.

He had gone down the tarred line ahead of her at first light and held the end taut while she followed, and neither of them had said a word about the blanket. She had folded it over the stove rail herself.

"I've a swarm in the orchard," Ewan said.

"I heard."

"You'd hear it from the next valley."

"I've been hearing it since dawn. I took it for the lake." She drew the plane the length of the edge, and the shaving curled up and fell. "Is there a reason you're telling me?"

"I need a pair of hands."

"You have a boy for hands."

"He's on the far terrace, and three more hives up there are muttering. Every hive on the Rise has a mind to leave, and the storm's loosed the lot at once." He shifted the dome of straw on his arm. "It's a bough job. One of us holds the skep and one of us cuts."

"I don't know bees."

"You know how to hold a plank steady while it's sawn."

She laid the plane on its side and looked at him then. He had the skep under one arm, a dome of straw the color of old rope, its rim worn pale where his hands went. A pruning knife hung from his belt, and the hive tool rode in his back pocket. There was straw on his coat and a petal in his hair, and he was not smiling, which meant he was.

Above the stair gap the gable wore a sheet of tarred canvas, nailed in rows that began crooked and straightened as they climbed. The nails had come out of the quay man's tin. She had not asked who. The long ladder from the yard leaned in the gap where the stair had been, the willow limb lay sawn into stove lengths along the wall, and the stair could wait on the hull.

"I have a bench that needs me," she said.

"You'll have it back by noon."

"I'm to hold a basket in an apple tree."

"It's a skep."

"It's a basket."

"It's a skep," he said, gravely, "and it has opinions about being called a basket."

She took her coat off the nail.

The hill met them with its noise. Over the terrace the air was thick and bright with moving specks, running up and across and back the way a flood runs when a sluice is thrown open, and the roar of it had weight, so that she walked into it with her shoulder down. Hive roofs stood open or knocked askew. Straps hung loose. She kept her hands in her pockets and her eyes on his back.

"They don't care about me," she said.

"They don't care about anyone. They've one idea, and it isn't you." He did not turn. "A swarm goes when it goes. You can read the signs, and you can have your skep ready, and you can't call one back. All you can do is be standing under it when it falls."

She thought of the thaw road, and put the thought away at once, as she would put a chisel back in its rack, edge down.

The orchard was late into its blossom. Petals lay thick under the trees and came down yet in the wind, loose and pale, onto the grass and onto her sleeves. In the old tree at the end of the row, the one with the leaning trunk, a dark mass hung from a low bough the size of a pitch bucket, shifting slowly on itself, its surface crawling. It hung there with perfect patience, and it hummed.

"Well," she said. "That's a lot of basket."

"It's a middling one." He dragged a short ladder out of the grass, set it against the trunk and tested it with his boot. "Hold the skep under the bough, rim up and level, the way you'd hold a plank for the saw. When I cut, the whole bough drops. Don't catch it. Let it fall in."

"And then?"

"Then stand still."

"I've done that."

"I know," he said. "I was there."

It was not much of a joke, and it loosened a knot in her chest all the same. She took the skep from him. It was lighter than it looked, springy as a coiled hawser, and it smelled of old smoke and dry grass. She held it up under the bough with both hands on the rim and her elbows locked, and watched him go up the ladder.

He did not hurry. He stopped on the second rung and stood a while with the knife held low at his side, not touching the branch, and he talked. She could barely hear it. It was not words she could follow, only a low run of sound, the voice he used for animals he was fond of: easy, easy now, nobody's hurrying you, there you are. The mass on the bough shivered and went on shivering. A long loop of it sagged and swung back.

"They're settling," he said, quietly. "Hear it? The note's dropping. They've stopped arguing about where."

"I hear a great many bees."

"They're only on the branch."

"That reassures nobody."

He laughed under his breath, and the knife went in. It was quick. She had not seen him decide. There was one clean stroke through the green wood, and the bough dropped through her hands into the skep with a soft heavy sound, like a wet fender dropped on a quay.

For a breath nothing happened. Then they poured.

They came over the rim in a brown flood, over her fingers and her wrists and the cuffs of her coat, a warm moving weight that spilled and went on spilling. The knife went into his belt unlooked at, and his hands came down over hers on the rim, one over each of her own, and the skep took the weight and held. She felt the whole of it through his palms.

"Stand still," Ewan said. "As you did in the lane."

She stood.

She waited for the fear. She had expected it to come the way a gust comes onto a lake, from far off and then all at once, and it did not come. In the lane she had stood with her knees shaking and her hat full of bees and her heart going hard, and she had told herself that a swarm was weather and weather did not choose a person. Now they walked over her knuckles and the old drawknife scar, over his hand lying on hers, and they weighed no more than shavings blown along a bench. She could feel each one, and could not have told the first from the last.

His thumb lay against the scar. It had settled there when the weight came down, across the white line on the back of her left hand, and the bees went over both and neither of them moved it. She knew the thumb. She had known it before she had a word for it. A hand that steady could have set a seam in a gale.

She looked up. His eyes were on her face and not on the bees, and he watched it as she watched a plank take its bend on the steam box, for the first sign of a crack. When he found none, the line of his shoulders came down by a quarter inch and stayed down. A petal turned past his shoulder in the light.

"All right?" he asked.

"All right."

"Not afraid."

"No." She found, a little to her surprise, that it was true. "I thought I would be."

"You were in the lane."

"I was." She watched a bee walk the whole of his thumbnail, turn at the end of it, and walk back.

He said nothing to that. His thumb on her scar did not shift. It pressed, the faintest weight, the way she laid a pencil to a line she did not mean to cut past.

She understood, standing there with the roar of the Rise in her ears and every bee in the orchard gone quiet on her hands, that she trusted him. The deciding had not been hers. Since the thaw she had been trusting him in small ways without noticing, as one trusts a floor, and now she trusted his hands with every living creature in that skep and with her own bare hand under his in a cloud that could have taken her apart.

It had the feel of an old joint taking up its load.

"You see," he said, very low, "that's all of it. You stay where you are and they sort themselves out." He was watching the rim. "A few go in at once. The rest stand about and ask each other. The ones at the back always think they've been left behind."

"And have they?"

"Never. They follow the scent in." His mouth moved. "I'd have told you in the lane, if I'd had the time."

"You told me in the lane to stand still."

"I'd have told you the rest."

He glanced up the hill, where the terrace lay hidden behind the blossom and the noise was thinning. "It's the flow," he said. "It has come on early, and they're filling faster than I can empty them. Every hive up there wants more room than I have to give. I'll be cutting comb soon, ready or not."

She knew what a full comb asked of him. She said nothing, and her hands stayed where they were under his.

The roar from the Rise was dying, a hill's worth of weather going over. In the skep the hum had dropped to a steady purr, round and low, with one thin high note running through it that she supposed was important. The weight on her hands lightened as the bees walked in off the rim. The backs of her knuckles showed again, one at a time, pale and bare. Last, only the scar was covered, and the bees across it went on in twos and threes, the straggling end of the crowd.

Neither of them spoke. Neither of them lifted a hand.

One bee remained. It crossed her scar, slowly, and stepped onto his thumb, and over his knuckle and down onto the pale worn straw of the rim. It stopped there, cleaned its face with its front legs, and walked over the lip and into the dark.

She watched it go in, and heard the hum close over it.
