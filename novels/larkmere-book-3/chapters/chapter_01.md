The thaw road had run with meltwater from the coast, and where it bent past the foot of Marrick Rise the ruts stood full to their lips. Bryony stopped on the crown of the lane, where the mud was thinnest, and let the town come up at her all at once.

Larkmere Water lay below, the color of an old chisel, rimmed with rotten grey ice where the shore still argued with the thaw. Rooks worked the bare hedges. Catkins hung from the willows at the water's edge, yellow against the wet black of the trunks. Past the quay the long timber roof of the Net Sheds ran down the shore like a hull turned over to dry, and the sight of it dropped into her chest and hung there, plumb and heavy, a bob on a line.

West of the quay, out of sight behind the sheds, was the yard. She did not look for it.

She hitched the pack higher. The flat pencil sat behind her ear where it always sat. Sawdust from the coast yard still clung in her hair, and the long walk had not shaken it loose. The tool roll, oiled and tied with its leather thong, knocked against her hip at every step, and across the back of her left hand the old drawknife scar had gone white with cold.

She had built her speeches on the road, as she built anything, from the keel up. First the greeting, level as a bench top. Then the business: she had come about the ferry. Then the matter of the yard, put so that nobody hearing it could guess how much hung on the answer. Seven springs had gone into the first sentence alone. She had said it to a hedge, to a gale, to the inside of her own hat.

She had last stood on this road going the other way, in the same thin light, with the ice letting go and her bag packed so tight the buckle strained. Hers had been the first boots of that year in its mud. She had not looked back, because a boatwright who stares at a cut line spoils the cut, and she had held to that for seven springs as she held to most of her rules, with both hands.

She was humming. She noticed only because the sound stopped. A bee had come down on the brim of that hat with no more noise than a dropped thread.

She did not turn her head. She watched it from the corner of one eye, a brown-gold speck walking the felt, and then a second landed beside it, and then so many that she gave up the tally.

They poured out of the air. The note of them climbed from a thread to a rope to a roar, and the roar came down through the crown of her hat and into her collarbone, a saw biting green timber. Weight gathered on her right shoulder, spread over the canvas of her pack, crept along the brim. Feet finer than hair pricked through the wool of her coat. Her calves began to ache from holding still, and a drop of meltwater ran from the hat brim down the side of her nose and hung off the tip.

Every sentence she had planed on the road went to shavings. The greeting, the business, the matter of the yard: gone, and nothing underneath. She had rehearsed his anger as she rehearsed a hard cut: where he would stand, how loud he would get, which of her answers would hold. In every version he had a voice to raise, and she had a plank to plane while he raised it. She had planned for his silence too. She had not planned for a swarm of strangers standing on her collar. Stillness was the whole job now. A step or a word would throw them off her in one roar, and the bees would take with them any hope of arriving quietly.

The lane stayed empty. No cart on it, no walker, no one from the quay come up for kindling. Larkmere kept to its chimneys in a thaw, and she wondered, with her stomach turning over, how many windows below had a clear view of a woman standing in the road with a swarm on her shoulder.

Boots came down the lane at a run, slapping through standing water.

She did not turn. She watched the roof of the Net Sheds and breathed through her teeth.

"Don't move. They're only deciding."

The voice had dropped since she last heard it, and worn smooth, like a tool handle. It still came in at its own pace, as though no hurry had ever mastered it. She would have known it with her eyes shut. With her eyes open she could not look at it.

Ewan stopped two paces off. His breath came hard, and the smell of him reached her before anything else: wood smoke, warm wax, wet wool. He carried a coiled straw skep in the crook of one arm and a soft brush in his other hand. A folded veil hung from his belt, unused. The haft of a hive tool stuck out of his back pocket. His sleeves were shoved to the elbow, and on his left forearm lay the old stings, white and pink and scattered, a pattern she knew as well as the knots in her own bench.

She was counting them before she could stop herself. Every old one was in its place. One was new, a pink welt above the wrist bone, still angry at the edges, and the sight of it hit her harder than the swarm had. Seven springs of stings, and she had missed every one of them arriving.

He did not look at her face. He studied the bees with the level attention of a man reading a sky that meant weather.

He eased the skep under her right shoulder, mouth up, and held it there the way she would hold a bailer under a weeping seam. His free hand rose to her collar. It stopped an inch off the wool and stayed, while the bees on her shoulder made up their minds about it. Then the brush touched down, and the heel of his palm after it, in a long sweep from the nape of her coat toward the open skep. He drew it again, and again, slow as the tide drawing off shingle.

Her pulse knocked in her throat where his knuckle passed. She was certain he could see it. If he did, he left it alone.

Then a bee that had missed the sweep walked in under her collar.

The itch came cold-footed at the base of her neck, between the wool and her skin, and every muscle she owned wanted to fling it out. She locked her jaw. Ewan read it in her shoulders before she made a sound. His hand came round behind her, flat and slow, and stopped with the fingers spread a hair's breadth from her nape, near enough that she caught the warmth of them in the hollow where the bee was walking. He tilted the brush and drew the bristles once along the inside of her collar. The bee climbed on as if offered a ladder. His knuckle grazed her neck on the way out, once, and the touch ran down her spine to her heels and stayed there.

He carried the bee to the skep and said nothing.

He blew across the cluster, one long patient breath, and it reached her too, smoke and wax and the cold iron smell of the road on him. The bees on her collar turned from it and walked into the straw.

She studied the roof of the Net Sheds as if she had been hired to price it. She did not look at his mouth, which was a hand's width from her ear and said nothing at all. She noted the sag in the ridge above the third shed, the patch of new boards near the seaward end, and the way a tongue of ice had slid off the eaves and lay in the mud like a dropped plane blade. Her brother had come up only to her ribs the morning she took this road the other way. He would be taller than she was now. He would not know her on this lane.

The letter had reached the coast yard in the dark of the year. She had read it once, standing at her bench, and left it by the oil can. Each morning she shifted it aside to reach her plane and each evening she set it back square. Nobody at the yard asked what it said. When the ice began to go she packed the tool roll. That was all the reasoning she had ever managed, and it would not have filled a page.

The hat went next. Ewan lifted it off by the crown, slowly, as a man lifts a lid from a hot pot, and tipped it over the skep. A drift of bees slid from the felt into the straw with a sound like rain beginning. Cold air found the sawdust in her hair. He had to have seen it. Seven springs on, he still said nothing, and turned to the pack, flicking stragglers off the canvas strap by strap with the brush.

The roar thinned to a hum. The hum broke into single wings, and then there were only a few, loose in the air, tapping at her sleeve and at the skep's rim, deciding what to do about the rest of the afternoon.

Her legs shook from the knee down. She locked them. A pack strap had worn a groove into her shoulder and she kept that to herself too.

When the last bee was in, Ewan turned the skep mouth down onto a folded cloth he pulled from his coat, and it sat there humming to itself on the road. His hands were steady. She watched him set the brush on the cloth and take his fingers off it, and the moment the brush was free of him, a tremor ran through his knuckles, quick, and gone when he closed them. He wiped them down his trousers. Then he held out her hat.

She took it by the crown, the way he had given it. A thumbprint of wax shone on the felt brim. She did not wipe it off.

He took in her face then, and the look ran the length of her and came back: the pack, the tool roll, the hair, the hands. It rested on the pencil behind her ear, and stayed there a breath longer than a pencil called for. The stub was chewed flat at one end. He had told her once she would end by eating the whole plank, and the toothmarks were still there.

She opened her mouth and found the first sentence gone entirely. What came out instead was the last of them, the one she had meant to keep for the end.

"I was sent for," she said. It came out level as a bench top, the only plank of her planning that had survived. "By the keeper of the Net Sheds."

Nothing in his face moved for the length of a breath, and then all of it did. The skin around his eyes drew tight. His glance flicked to the Net Sheds and back to her. He ran the words once through his head, she could see it, as a man runs a rope through his hands to find where it frays. His fingers closed on the empty air where the brush had been.

He had not known.

She had no tool for a man who was not angry. This was not anger. This was a man who has crossed the same deck for seven springs and feels, in one step, a board give. Whoever had written to her had written round him, and he was only now learning how wide the gap in the planking ran. He did not ask who. He did not ask when. He stood straight in the mud with wax under his thumbnails and the swarm humming at his feet, and she saw him decide not to say the first thing that came to him.

She hated that she knew the look. She hated more that she had missed it.

The wind came up off the water and turned the catkins over. Meltwater ticked from the hedge. Neither of them filled the silence. The skep hummed on, and the light along the lake slid from pewter to a thinner grey, the shortening light of a thaw afternoon with the sun gone behind the Rise.

On her left cuff, where the scar began, one bee had stayed behind.

It reached her before she saw it, a tick of feet through the wool. It started up her sleeve toward her wrist, unhurried, and she watched it go. Ewan watched it too.

Neither of them moved.
