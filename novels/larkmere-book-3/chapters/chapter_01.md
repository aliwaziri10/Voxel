The thaw road was a slurry of mud and meltwater that pulled at her boots with every step. Bryony stopped where the lane curved past the foot of Marrick Rise, the air tasting of wet stone and the first sharp scent of waking earth. Below her, Larkmere lay huddled against the shore. Larkmere Water was a sheet of bruised pewter, the ice gone grey and rotten at the edges where the lake began to eat the winter away. She stood still, her breath a pale ghost in the chill, and traced the long, low roof of the Net Sheds. The sight of them was like a plumb line dropped into her chest, pulling her center true and heavy.

She shifted the weight of her pack. A flat carpenter's pencil was tucked behind her ear, and a few stray grains of sawdust clung to her hair, remnants of the coast that she had not yet brushed away. Her canvas tool roll, oiled and tied with a leather thong, pressed against her hip. On the back of her left hand, the pale scar from a drawknife caught the thin light. She had spent the long walk from the coast carving out the words she would say. She had planed them smooth, bevelled the edges, and fitted them together like a tight scarf joint. She knew exactly how to announce her return, how to keep her voice as level as a tabletop, and how to ask for her father's yard without sounding as if she expected it to be open.

The first bee arrived without a sound. It was a golden speck that drifted through the damp air and landed on the brim of her hat. Bryony froze. She did not blink. Then came another, then ten, then a hundred. They descended in a humming cloud, a sudden shift in the wind that brought a roar of wings. The swarm dropped out of the air, settling in a heavy, vibrating mass across her right shoulder, the canvas of her pack, and the felt of her hat. 

The humming was a physical thing, a vibration that seeped through her wool coat and into her skin. She felt the tiny, prickling legs of them, the weight of a thousand bodies deciding if she was a stone or a flower. Her planned sentences vanished. The carefully joined words splintered and drifted away like scrap wood in a tide. She forgot the coast, forgot the road, and forgot how to breathe. The only thing that existed was the roar in her ears and the terrifying, electric stillness of her own body. If she moved, she would be a target. If she spoke, she would break the spell. She stood as rigid as a stempost, her heart thumping against her ribs like a loose nail in a gale.

A shout broke the silence, distant but urgent. Someone was running down from the Rise, the sound of boots splashing through the mud growing louder. Bryony did not turn her head. She could not. 

"Don't move. They're only deciding."

The voice was deeper than she remembered, but it had the same steady cadence, a slow current that promised safety. It was Ewan. He skidded to a halt beside her, the smell of smoke and beeswax cutting through the damp scent of the road. 

Bryony shifted her gaze slightly, her neck stiff. Ewan stood close, his chest heaving from the run. He wore a heavy coat that smelled of the hives, and a folded bee veil was tucked into his belt. In his back pocket, the handle of a hive tool peeked out, and his thumbnails were stained dark with wax. His forearms were mapped with old stings, small, faded marks he ignored as he stepped into her space. He held a woven straw skep in one hand and a soft bee brush in the other.

He did not look at her face at first. His eyes were on the swarm, studying the way they clustered on her shoulder. He moved with a patience that felt like a slow tide, his motions fluid and sure. He reached out, his hand hovering an inch from her collar. 

Bryony felt the heat of him before he touched her. The air between them tightened, a tension as sharp as a fresh saw blade. When his fingers finally brushed the wool of her coat to sweep a stray bee toward the skep, the contact sent a jolt through her that made her pulse leap in her throat. She could see the steady rise and fall of his chest, the way his shoulder nearly brushed hers. He was so close she could smell the sweetness of the honey that lived in the creases of his skin.

He began to work, his voice a low murmur that he directed at the bees, not at her. He coaxed them with a gentle, rhythmic brushing, his breath warm against her cheek. He used his bare hands to guide the cluster, his fingers deft and sure. Every time he moved, his arm grazed hers, a fleeting touch that felt like a spark hitting dry tinder. 

Bryony stared straight ahead at the grey water of the lake, but her entire world had shrunk to the space between them. She was acutely aware of the distance to his mouth, the line of his jaw, the way his brow furrowed in concentration. She forced herself not to look, gripping the straps of her pack until her knuckles went white. She felt the thrum of the bees and the thrum of her own blood, two different rhythms colliding in the silence of the road.

He worked in a focused quiet, his movements precise. He did not ask where she had been. He did not ask why she had come back after seven springs. He treated her as if she were simply a piece of the landscape, a convenient perch for a wandering swarm. The restraint in him was a physical wall, as solid as a heartwood beam. 

Slowly, the weight on her shoulder lightened. The roar dimmed to a hum, and then to a whisper. Ewan eased the last of the cluster into the skep, his hand lingering for a second too long near the nape of her neck. He stepped back, the sudden gap between them feeling like a cold draft.

He tightened the lid of the skep and finally looked at her. His eyes were the colour of deep water, steady and searching. He did not smile, but there was a softening in the corners of his gaze, a flicker of something that looked like a held breath. He stood there, the skep cradled in his arm, the smoke of the hives still clinging to him, waiting for her to speak.

The silence stretched, filling with the sound of meltwater dripping from the hedges. Bryony felt the ghost of his touch on her collar, a warmth that refused to fade. She cleared her throat, her voice sounding rough and foreign in her own ears.

"I was sent for," she said, her voice small against the wide expanse of the lake. "By the keeper of the Net Sheds."

The change in his face was immediate. The stillness vanished, replaced by a sharp, sudden tension. His grip tightened on the skep, and his eyes narrowed, searching her face for a lie.

"The keeper," he repeated. He sounded as if the word were a stone in his mouth. "I did not know."

Bryony watched the ripple of his expression, the way his jaw set into a hard, straight line. It was a look she remembered from the year before she left, the way he became a closed door when the world pushed too hard. She shifted her weight, her boots squelching in the mud, and the movement felt clumsy, like a boat with a fouled rudder. The silence returned, but it was no longer the quiet of the bees. It was a heavy, expectant thing that pressed against her ribs.

"She wrote to me," Bryony added. She did not say who she meant, and she did not say how long the letter had sat on her desk at the coast before she finally packed her bags. "She said the ferry needs work."

Ewan did not move. He stood with the skep held against his hip, his gaze fixed on her. The wind shifted, blowing a strand of hair across her face, and she reached up to tuck it back, her fingers brushing the flat pencil behind her ear. She saw his eyes follow the movement, a quick, sharp glance that took in the sawdust in her hair and the scar on her hand. He looked at her as if she were a piece of timber he was trying to grade, searching for the knots and the shakes that might make her fail.

"Seven springs," he said. The words were quiet, but they had the weight of a falling mast. "You were sent for after seven springs."

Bryony felt the sting of it. She had prepared for his anger, for a shout or a cold dismissal, but this quiet observation was worse. It was a measurement of the gap between them, a distance that could not be bridged with a few well chosen sentences. She wanted to tell him that she had thought of him every time she planed a board, that the memory of his voice had been a steady line in her mind while she learned the trade. Instead, she tightened her grip on the straps of her pack.

"The ferry is an old boat, Ewan. She cannot wait forever."

He let out a short, dry breath that was not quite a laugh. He turned his head toward the town, looking down at the Net Sheds where the ferry sat on her cradle. The light was failing, the gold of the afternoon turning to a thin, watery pewter that matched the lake. 

"She has waited," he murmured. 

He looked back at her, and for a moment, the hardness in his eyes flickered. The heat that had existed between them while he brushed the bees away returned, a low, humming tension that made the air feel thick. Bryony did not look at his mouth, though she could feel the pull of it, a magnetic draw that made her pulse thrum in her throat. She focused on the way his thumbnails were stained with wax, on the rough wool of his coat. 

He stepped closer, not enough to touch her, but enough that she could feel the radiation of his warmth. The smell of smoke and honey enveloped her, a scent that belonged to the Rise and the meadows, a scent that had lived in her dreams for years. He was a constant, a fixed point in a world that had shifted and slid beneath her feet. 

"You look the same," he said.

"I don't," she replied, her voice regaining some of its bluntness. "I am a boatwright now."

"I can see that." His gaze drifted to the canvas tool roll at her hip. "You brought your tools."

"I did not come here to visit."

Ewan shifted the skep. The movement was slow, deliberate. He seemed to be weighing her, deciding if she was a guest or an intruder in the town she had once called home. The tension between them was like a rope stretched too tight, vibrating with a frequency that only they could hear. 

"Ottoline does not usually send for people from the coast," he said. "She prefers to handle things within the town."

"Then she must have known the ferry was in a bad way."

"She knows many things," Ewan replied. There was a note of dry humor in his voice, a ghost of the man he had been. "But she did not tell me you were coming."

Bryony felt a sudden, sharp ache in her chest, a feeling like a grain of sand caught under a plane. The fact that he had been kept in the dark felt like a deliberate act, a way to protect him or a way to test her. She wondered if he had spent the last seven springs waiting for a letter that never came, or if he had simply closed the door and locked it.

"I only arrived an hour ago," she said.

"The road is a mess. You are lucky you did not sink into a ditch."

"I have walked worse," she said, and it was true. The roads at the coast were often nothing but salt and shale, shifting beneath her feet while she carried heavy loads of seasoned oak. 

Ewan looked at her again, and this time his gaze lingered on her eyes. The search was deeper now, looking past the boatwright and the tools to the girl who had left on the thaw road. Bryony felt herself slipping, the rigid posture of the stempost softening. She wanted to reach out and touch the rough fabric of his sleeve, to see if he was as solid as he looked.

He noticed the shift. His eyes darkened, and he took a half step toward her, the skep momentarily forgotten. The air between them became a vacuum, pulling them together with a force that made her breath hitch. She could see the fine lines around his eyes, the way his skin was weathered by the sun and the wind of the Rise. He was a man grown, his shoulders broader, his presence more grounded.

The silence was no longer a wall; it was a bridge. Bryony felt her heart hammering against her ribs, a frantic rhythm that matched the memory of the swarm. She did not move, fearing that any gesture would break the fragile connection. She could feel the heat of his breath, a warm current in the chill air, and the scent of honey seemed to thicken, wrapping around her like a shroud.

Just as he seemed about to say something, a sudden gust of wind swept down from the Rise, carrying the scent of damp pine and old snow. It whipped her hair across her eyes and chilled the skin of her neck. The spell snapped. Ewan blinked, and the distance returned, as if a wedge had been driven between them.

He stepped back, his expression returning to that guarded, steady mask. 

"You should get to the sheds," he said. "The light is almost gone, and the mud only gets deeper after dusk."

"I can find my way," Bryony said, though the words felt hollow.

"I know you can. But the harbor lanterns are being lit, and the quay folk will be heading in. It is better to be inside before the frost sets in."

He began to turn away, the skep cradled in his arm. He did not offer to walk her down the road. He did not ask her to stay for tea or tell her where she could find a bed. He simply began to retreat back toward the Rise, his boots splashing through the slurry of the lane.

Bryony stood still, watching him go. He moved with a steady, patient stride, a man who knew exactly where he belonged and how to get there. She felt a sudden, piercing loneliness, a realization that while she had spent seven years learning how to build boats that could weather any storm, she had no idea how to navigate the waters between her and the man walking away.

She adjusted the straps of her pack, the weight of her tools feeling heavier than they had before. She looked down at the town, at the long roof of the Net Sheds and the grey, rotten ice of the lake. Everything looked the same, and yet everything had changed. The town was a map she knew by heart, but the landmarks had shifted.

As she started down the slope, her boots pulling at the mud, she felt a light, tickling sensation on her wrist. She stopped and looked down.

A single bee, a golden stray from the swarm, had landed on the wool of her sleeve. It did not fly away. Instead, it began to walk, its tiny legs moving with slow, deliberate precision up the fabric toward her skin. Bryony froze, her breath held in her lungs, watching the small creature approach the pale scar on her hand. She did not move, and above her, on the road back to the Rise, Ewan stopped and turned to look back at her. 

Neither of them moved.
