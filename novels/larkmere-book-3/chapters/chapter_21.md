She had the second plank on the trestles and the spokeshave in both hands, bringing the lap down to its line, when Ottoline came along the shingle in the heat of the afternoon carrying a jar the way a person carries a full bucket up a ladder. She walked into the Net Sheds without being asked and stood the jar on the bench beside the plane.

It was a plain round jar with a cloth top tied down with string. A horn spoon hung from the neck on a loop of the same string, and a paper strip was knotted under it. Bryony knew the hand on the strip. It was square and upright, every letter set down with care, the hand of a man who had never once been in a hurry.

"I didn't send for honey," she said.

"No."

"I've a plank half faired on the trestles."

"It will keep." Ottoline stood with her hands folded over the ring of keys, which for once made no sound, and gave the jar the look she gave a sky she had already read. Then she said one word, in the voice of a woman remarking that it looked like rain.

"Eat."

She went out into the glare, bent in the back and upright in everything else, and the shingle took the sound of her away.

Bryony went back to the plank. The jar sat on the bench with its strip turned toward her, and she read it once and then tried not to: clover and hawthorn, and under that, dry, a wind from the south. She had tasted four jars at Ottoline's table, and she knew what a jar could do to a person. Those had been old days. This one had been cut with her in the valley.

She worked. The lap came down a shaving at a time, and the shavings curled off the iron and fell, and she ran the spokeshave over the same stretch of it a second time before she saw what she was doing. A strake taken too thin is a strake spoiled. She laid the tool down on the trestle and wiped her hands on her coat and went to the bench.

The knot gave to a thumbnail, as a hitch will if you know which end to ask. She lifted the cloth and filled the spoon and held it level at her mouth for a moment, as she held a feeler over a lap while she waited for the light to tell her. Then she ate it.

Sweetness first, thin and bright, clover and a thread of hawthorn sharp as a splinter. She had been ready for sweetness. Then it opened, and she was not ready for the rest.

Longing came first, and she knew it for longing at once, since it could not have been anything else: a pull, steady and unhurried, like a line paid out over a gunwale with a weight still fast to the far end of it and no hand anywhere near the knife. It had been going on a long time. It did not hurry and it did not ease. And under it, lower and closer, lay a second thing that took her longer to name, because she had not thought to look for it: a plain gladness, warm at the back of her throat, like a stove catching in a cold shed at first light, when the iron begins to give back the night.

The first spoon she had taken after the thaw had been all stillness. This one had been moving the whole time.

She sat down on the stack of oak by the wall, which took her weight without comment, and held the edge of the top board in both hands while the taste ran down and settled. The shed was very quiet. Dust turned in the light from the door.

He had told her the weather. In all the weeks since the thaw he had told her the weather, and when a board would hold, and where the tarred line was kept, and she had said back what the work needed, and they had built a perfectly good way of standing in a shed together that left out the only matter in it. And all that while this had been going on in him, a line running out, a stove catching, and he had stood in his Honey House with a knife in his hand and put it into a jar for anyone with a spoon. It had told her more in one mouthful than he had said since the thaw, or in the seven springs before it.

The thought came late, with a cold edge, like a squall line crossing the lake from a quarter she had not been watching. By now half the quay had a jar of this cut on its table. The quiet on the shingle that morning, which she had taken for the old verdict, had been a different quiet: a town that had put a spoon in its mouth and then looked at her and found nothing left to say. She had walked about Larkmere for weeks pretending she did not know what he felt, and she had been the last person in the valley to hold that opinion.

She tried, briefly, to un-know it, as one tries to un-hear a note. The taste stayed where it was.

"Ottoline," she said to the empty shed, in a voice that could not decide which way to go.

She got up and went back to the plank and stood over it with the spokeshave in her hand for as long as it takes the light to cross a board, and put the tool down again without touching the wood. The light turned long and gold across the shingle. The midges came up off the water in a haze. When she looked round, a thin green light lay across the doorsill, and the lantern on the seaward wall was lit. The taper had made no sound that she heard, and she did not ask who had held it. She had stopped asking about the lantern a good while ago, and the habit had gone the way of her other arguments, a plank at a time.

She picked up the jar by its neck, the way she would carry a mallet she did not mean to use, and went out and up the Rise.

The lane was dry and the dust lay soft on it. Dandelion clocks stood along the wall gone to bare heads. The swifts were going over the roofs in screaming loops, and the air coming off the water already had a cold edge in it, even in the long evening. Below her the kitchen lamps were coming on along the harbor one after another, and in every window, if she had looked, there would be a jar on the table. She did not look. She kept her eyes on the path and on the lamp at the top of it.

The door of the Honey House stood open on lamplight. He was at the bench with a pen and a stack of paper strips and a long row of jars with their cloth tops tied down, and he was writing. His sleeves were rolled. The lamp lay along his left forearm where the old stings stood out white, and she knew each of them, as she knew every knot in her own bench. His coat hung on its nail by the door, and she could smell the smoke in it from the step.

Beyond him the lamp reached the shelves. On the top shelf the squat glass jar of dark honey sat sealed with its disc of Marrick wax, a strip of blue linen tied round the neck, turned so the blank side faced the room. She looked at it once and not again.

He looked up. He saw the jar before he saw her face. She watched him take in the horn spoon on its loop of string and work out whose hand had put it there, and the pen stopped.

Neither of them spoke. She crossed the stone floor and set the jar on the bench harder than she had meant to. It struck the wood and rocked on its base, and he put out a hand to stop it and flattened his palm on the cloth top, and her own hand came down over his before the jar had settled. She had not decided to.

Her face, she found, was an inch from his throat. She could see the pulse going in it under the skin, quick and hard, and smell smoke and wax and clover at his collar. His hand was warm under hers and it did not move. Her thumb had found the inside of his wrist on its own, the place where the pulse ran nearest the surface, and it lay there.

The lamp hissed. Outside, the last of the swifts went over the roof, and the hum from the terraces thinned to a murmur and then to a few late voices. He did not ask what it had tasted of. She did not say. The pulse under her thumb ran quick and a little ragged, and she counted it, and counted it again, as she tried any joint she meant to trust, and it came out the same both times.
