The cold woke her before the light did. It rose through the floor of the main shed and settled at the base of her spine, and Bryony sat for a moment with her coat across her knees and her pack under her head, learning the place by its smell: pitch and wet oak, and under them the flat mineral breath of the lake pushing in around the water doors. The collar of the coat, where it lay against her chin, still held woodsmoke and warm wax. She turned it down and kept her fingers off it.

She had come in at dusk by that door, which had been left on its latch. Before that she had stood on the shore stones until the cold reached her boots, watching a green light burn on the seaward wall of the sheds, a starboard light on a wall that faced no channel. Then she had walked in and found the cradle and sat down at its foot to rest her legs, and the dark had done the rest.

A thread of grey lay along the doors' seams. She stood, and her knees gave a full account of the road. She raked her fingers through her hair, found the coast yard's sawdust still in it, and left it there. Thaw water ticked from the eaves and ran in the gutters, and up in the roof beams a sparrow complained about it.

The Heron filled the shed. A broad wooden ferry with a wheelhouse, built by her father, she rested on her cradle as she had rested since the ice closed the lake, bow to the water doors and stern to the landward wall, so near the roof beams that her rail brushed the cobwebs. The sole boards had been lifted and stacked against the wheelhouse bulkhead, the way a hull is aired for the winter.

Bryony began where she always began, at the stem, with her palms. The rail there was worn pale and dished along its top by years of hands steadying themselves, and her own hand settled into the dish without being asked.

She worked aft along the starboard side with her hands flat and her thumbs leading, and now and then she rapped a plank with a knuckle and listened. Forward the planks rang. The rivets sat tight in their seats, and where rust had bled from a rove it had run down, dried and gone no further. Then she reached the garboards, and those answered with a flat knock and no ring in it. The seams ran dark and damp along their whole run, and the oldest still wept a thread of brown where the thaw had found the pitch. Seepage had been finding its way to the keel.

She climbed the stage ladder and dropped inside. The frames were bare in the bilge, each one cut true, and on every one lay the ridge her father's plane iron had left, a raised line where a nick in the blade had cut the same groove at the same height, frame after frame, from stem to sternpost. She laid her palm along the nearest. She took it away again and wiped it on her coat, as if the ridge had left a mark.

The keel was the rest of the business. She climbed out, crouched, and shuffled in beneath the turn of the bilge between two cradle bearers, to where the long backbone of the boat ran overhead. Forward it was dry and ringed with old pitch, and her thumbnail stopped dead against it. Aft, at the heel, where the timber met the sternpost, the same thumb sank to the first joint.

She unrolled the tool roll on the floor and drew out the awl. The scar across the back of her left hand pulled tight as her fingers closed. She set the point to the heel and leaned, and it slid in to the ferrule with the push of one finger.

She drew it out and tried a hand's breadth forward. The oak there smelled of oak. The point skated, bit the depth of a shaving, and stopped. She tried the heel again, a finger's width from the first hole, and the awl sank again, and she tried a third place and a fourth and had the same answer from each. Dark crumbs drifted down onto the floor. She brushed them off her wrist.

The whole length would have to come out. A new scarf at the heel would bury the rot and keep it warm. She laid the work out in her head from the first fastening to the last, garboards sprung, stem scarfed back, sternpost lifted off its tenon, every seam opened to the pitch and closed again, and the list ran long. She laid it out a second time in case she had it wrong, and it did not run shorter.

Low in her throat a tune ran on beside the work, as it did at any bench, and she did not hear it.

Keys rang at the landward door before anything else did.

The lock turned. The hinge complained, and a bar of daylight lay across the floor and stopped short of the cradle. Ottoline entered at her own pace, which was the only pace she had, with the ring of keys swinging at her belt and the left pocket of her coat hanging heavy. Her back was bent, and her chin stayed level above it.

"Frost's gone off the lane," she said, to the shed in general.

Bryony slid out from under the hull on her back, awl in her fist, and sat up. "Morning."

"You slept on my floor."

"I slept on the floor."

"There's a loft." Ottoline pointed her chin at the roof. "I aired it."

"No one told me."

"I'm telling you." Her eyes dropped to the awl, and then to the dark crumbs in the dust beneath the timber. "Well?"

"The keel comes out."

"All of it?"

"All of it."

Ottoline considered the crumbs a while longer. She did not ask whether Bryony was sure. She nodded once, as she might nod at a sky.

"Then it comes out. Hawthorn's greening on the Rise. Blossom's next, then swarm, then the limes, and the limes have never waited on anyone's pride. I want the Heron in the water before they flower."

"She'll be in the water when she's sound."

"I said before."

Bryony kept her eyes on the holes. It was a fair hit, and she let it land. She set the point to a fresh place and leaned on it, and the wood gave way.

"It said yes the first time," Ottoline said.

"It'll say it again."

"You're humming."

"I'm not."

"You were."

Bryony shut her mouth on the last note and kept at the awl.

Ottoline crossed to the hull and laid her palm flat on the planking above Bryony's head, near the garboard.

"Fairlie keel, Marrick comb," she said.

She said it flatly, like a measure read off a mark. "Your father cut that keel himself, and her seams were waxed from the Rise. That's how she was made, and that's how she'll be mended."

Bryony glanced up at the heel she had spent the dawn condemning. Her thumb lay flat on the timber beside the holes, longer than the work needed.

"Ewan will bring the wax down."

The thumb lifted. It rose to the pencil behind her ear, found it there, and stayed a breath.

"Wax is wax," she said.

"Not in that hull."

Bryony turned that over. The wax would reach her in his hands, and the hull would take it, and there was no honest argument against either.

"Come up," Ottoline said, "and see what I've aired."

A steep stair climbed the landward wall to the loft, its rail rubbed pale by hands, and Ottoline took it at her own pace, the keys ringing on every tread and falling silent each time she rested. Bryony followed with her pack on one shoulder.

The loft ran the width of the end bay under the roof, and it smelled of a room that had been open to the wind. A squat iron stove sat laid with kindling, cold. A cot was made up tight under a grey blanket folded square at its foot. One window faced the lake, and the glass had been washed; the streaks of the cloth were still in it.

She stopped in the doorway. She had meant to study the Heron, say what it needed in plain words, and be back on the thaw road before the ice had finished going. She had not planned for a washed window. She stood with the strap cutting into her shoulder and did not know where to put her hands.

Ottoline crossed to the window and shoved up the sash with the heel of her hand. Cold air pushed in, and the lake with it, laid out flat as a planed board under a skin of mist, a rim of old ice crowding the shore and the Far Shore rising dark behind. Past the frame, a long reach beyond the sill, an iron lantern hung from a bracket on the seaward wall. It was out now, the wick burned down, its one pane of green glass clouded with soot.

"It burns every dusk," Ottoline said, and brought the sash down with the same hand.

Bryony said nothing. The green pane held the first grey of the morning and gave none of it back.

Ottoline worked a key free of the ring with her thumbnail, a long iron key worn bright along the shank, and laid it on the sill. "Landward door and this room. The water door doesn't lock."

Bryony studied the key, and the hand above it, knotted and spotted and as steady as a vise. Then she took the key. It was cold, and heavier than its size allowed, and she put it away in her coat before she could think what taking it said.

Down on the shed floor, Ottoline cleared a space on the long bench with the flat of her hand, pushing the shavings aside. From the deep left pocket she drew a plain round jar with a cloth top tied down with string, and from the right a spoon with a horn bowl. A paper strip hung from the string, written by hand: lime, fog on the water. Bryony knew the hand before she read it. The downstrokes leaned one way, like a hedge on an open shore.

"Last autumn's," Ottoline said. "Eat."

"I've eaten."

"When?"

"On the road."

"Eat. It tastes of the day it was cut. You know that. Eat it anyway."

She knew the rule as every child in Larkmere knew it. She had not tasted Marrick honey in seven springs. It was honey. She had come for the Heron, and a spoonful was what an old woman pressed on a guest at dawn, and she could eat it and say so and the keel would be no harder for it.

She untied the string and folded back the cloth. The honey lay dark in the jar, and when she dipped the spoon it rose slowly, with a drag. She ate.

It was sweet first, a late-year sweetness that lay down on her tongue like pitch warming in a pot. She had time to think that it was only honey. Then, at the back of her tongue, a stillness gathered under the sweetness and held, the way a latch holds with a thumb laid on it, pressed down and not lifted.

She did not know which day it had been cut. She knew whose day it was.

"It's honey," she said.

"It is," said Ottoline, in the voice she had used for the frost, and she crossed to the landward door with her keys ringing at her belt. The door closed behind her, and the ring receded up the shore, thinning.

Bryony stood with the spoon in her hand.

She knew exactly what his wrist weighed. She had held it once across her palm to prise a splinter of oak from the ball of his thumb, and it had weighed what seasoned oak weighs, more than it promised. Her palm remembered it now, the way a bevel gauge keeps its angle.

Yesterday in the lane his glance had rested on the pencil behind her ear a breath longer than a pencil needed. The pencil had stayed warm against her skin all night.

She had come for the Heron. She said it to herself, plain.

She set the spoon down across the lid of the jar. Then she picked up the awl, because the timber was soft whatever the honey said, and dropped to her knees and shuffled in beneath it again, and the taste stayed at the back of her tongue, waiting on its latch.
