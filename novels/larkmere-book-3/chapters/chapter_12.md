Ewan woke in the dark with nothing in his head at all, and found it worse than the far hives had been.

The house lay quiet around him. He rose and crossed to the window to see what the night would say for itself. The orchard stood pale under the dark, every bough thick with blossom, and below the last row the lake held a few stars and one green point of light. The lantern. The iron lantern with its one green glass panel on the seaward wall of the Net Sheds. He had lit it himself at dusk and had no business noticing that it was burning. It burned.

Under it, in the loft window of the Net Sheds, a second light showed, low and yellow. A stove, not a lamp. A stove at this hour meant a person awake.

He stood at the glass a long while. The hives were on the terrace behind the house and the lake was in front of it, and he had not been confused on this point for most of his life. He told himself he would see to the wraps on the terrace. He pulled on his boots and took the orchard way down instead, and found the story unconvincing even for himself.

The grass was wet to the knee. Petals clung to his trousers and rode in his boot-tops, and the blossom breathed out a cold sweetness so thick that he walked through it. The hive tool rode in his back pocket, where it always rode. He left the smoker on its nail. Nothing he carried would be any use where he was going, and he could not say where that was.

On the shingle the lake turned over on the stones with a private sound. The lantern laid a path of light on the water that ended at his boots. The long roof of the Net Sheds ran black under the stars, and the side door stood on its latch, as if the shed had expected company.

Inside it smelled of oak and old pitch and cold water. The Heron's keel lay on its balks where it had dropped that morning, black and heavy, and he crossed the floor past it with a hand out for the dark. He had stood in that door at midday with the wax on the bench behind him and had not found a way in. She had sat in the shavings with her head bent over the open joint, and he had known better than to speak, and had spoken anyway, about wax. He walked round the keel now as he would round a hive in the cold, softly, with nothing in his hands.

The loft stair rose steep at the far end, built for a person carrying a load. The third tread complained. He had known it would; he had known every board of this stair once. He stopped with his weight half on it and listened. Above him the stove ticked. Nothing else.

He climbed the rest. The loft opened round his head, one long room under the roof beams, the cot along the far wall with its blankets squared, the stove ticking in the corner, and the window over the lake with the lantern's green falling through its one pane to lie across the boards in a square, the color of a lime leaf held up to the sun.

Bryony sat in the window with her back against the frame and her knees drawn up. Her boots stood in a pair by the cot. The canvas roll rested on the sill, still tied, the thong in its two turns. She had not touched a tool. He could not remember ever having seen her sit with her work put by, and he did not know what to do with the sight, so he did nothing, which was what his hands knew best.

She did not turn her head.

"I heard the third stair," she said.

"It complained to me as well."

"It always did."

There was no hum in the loft. She hummed over a plank and she hummed over a problem, and the silence where the three notes belonged was the loudest sound under the roof.

"You're not working," he said.

"No."

"I thought you might be."

"I thought about it."

Her fingers turned a pale curl of oak over and over in that light. He crossed the boards and stopped an arm's length off. It was a paring the length of her hand, shaved thin from the lap face of a joint, and a line of grey pencil ran along it in a leaning hand. He knew the hand. He did not read it. It was not his to read.

"It's only a bit of pencil," she said.

"Yes."

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to."

Outside, the lake shifted along the stones. Blossom drifted in at the cracked pane with the cold, one petal turning down through the light and settling on the boards. Neither of them stepped across the square of light.

She shook once.

It began in her shoulders without a sound, a single hard shudder that ran the length of her the way a gust runs through a hedge in blossom, every petal letting go at once. The curl of oak rattled in her fingers. She shut her mouth on it and held on, and the second one did not come, but he saw that it was in her, waiting.

He did not decide. He crossed the last of the floor and knelt in the green, and she leaned, or he did, and by then the leaning was already done.

His arms closed round her. One arm ran along her ribs. The other he laid flat between her shoulder blades, over the wool, where it rose and fell with her. She let him. That was all of it: she let him. Her forehead found the hollow under his jaw and stayed there, and her breath ran hot and broken against his collar, and her pulse struck against his throat, quick and light as a bee against glass. He held still so as not to lose it. The hive tool in his back pocket dug into his own spine, and he ignored it.

Her hair smelled of pitch and lake water and the plain wood dust she carried everywhere. She drew the smoke from his coat in, long, once, and let it out against his neck.

Neither of them said a word. There was no word in the loft that would not have cost more than it bought.

He thought of everything he had not asked her at the top of the thaw road. She had been so careful of his hives. He had been so careful of her work. Each of them had held the other's life at arm's length like a full frame that must not be tipped, and neither had said the short sentence that would have meant tipping it. He knew now what he had not known on the road: that they had both chosen their work, and that neither had asked the other for the one request the work left no space for. He could not have said it aloud to save his life. One wrong word and she would be on the thaw road by morning with her roll tied. He knew it the way he knew rain, not from the sky but from the sound of the hives.

So he asked nothing now, either.

It took a long while. The stove ticked. The square of light stayed at his knee. Her pulse lost its flutter and slowed to the pace of a person coming down from a height, and her breath lengthened and evened, slow as sap in the first warm week. He stayed until his own knee ached, and a while after.

When her weight had gone heavy against him, he drew back a quarter inch at a time and laid her head to the window frame with his hand cupped behind it so the wood would not wake her. She did not wake. The curl of oak lay open in her palm. He did not take it and he did not touch it. He stood, and his knee cracked like a dry twig, and she did not stir.

At the stair head he stopped. The stove glowed red at its door-slot, and the lantern's light crossed her hands. He had come down the Rise with no plan, and he was leaving with none, and for once the lack no longer troubled him. The third stair complained under his boot. He let it.

The whole stair creaked under him all the way down, and over his head the stove glowed on.
