The wind swung south in the night, and by noon the whole Rise smelled of wet earth and the lake.

Ewan stood in the hive yard with the wind pushing his coat against his back and listened to the ice. It had talked since dawn. A long groan rose from the foot of the hill, then a pause, then a crack like a brood frame snapping in the cold, and under every crack ran a deeper note that climbed through the soles of his boots. The bees knew it too. They sat on the landing boards with their heads turned toward the water and did not fly.

He had told himself he would stay on the Rise. Every year the town gathered on the quay the night the lake opened, and every year he had waited at the rear of it with his hands in his pockets and said little to anyone. This year the quay held people he had no wish to count. He scalded the tin pails. He sorted frames. He mended a veil that did not need mending.

When the light turned the color of weak clover honey the first true boom rolled up the hill, and every bee on every board lifted her head at once. He put the veil down and took his coat.

Larkmere had gathered at the water before him. The whole town lined the quay with coats buttoned to the chin, children up on shoulders, old men planted on the bollards, and nobody spoke above a murmur. The lake lay before them under one sheet of grey, veined black from shore to shore like a frame of old comb, and the veins widened as he watched.

He did not search for her. He found her at once.

She stood at the far end by the last iron ring, hands pushed into her coat, the flat pencil behind her ear, and a space lay around her about an arm wide. Nobody had decided on it. It lay on the cobbles as a ring of bare ground lies around a warm stone after frost. He threaded the crowd toward her. Several people nodded to him as he passed, and he nodded back, and none of the nods lasted long.

Halfway down the quay a man by the bollards said it to his boots.

"Has a nerve, coming back."

He said it low. He did not say it low enough. A woman near him put a hand on her child's head, and a murmur ran down the row like wind through willow, neither agreeing nor taking it back.

Ewan had no memory of crossing the cobbles. One moment he was among them with his hands in his pockets, and the next he was out in the open with his back to Bryony and his face to the man at the bollards, and the murmur stopped as though a lid had gone on it. He had nothing planned. He had no word ready. He only held his ground, which was what he did best, and let the man see him do it.

The man found his boots again.

In all his life Ewan had stood a step behind the crowd. Now the quay knew which side of the lane he stood on, and so did he, and nobody had asked him to choose.

Behind him Bryony had gone still. He knew she had noticed the way you know a front is passing: a change in the air at his back, a drop in the pressure of the evening.

"I can stand by myself," she said, low.

"I know."

"Then move."

"In a minute."

She said nothing more. He stayed where he was until the talk along the quay started up again in scraps, about the ice, which was all anyone had wanted to talk about in the first place. Then he stepped aside and took the place at her left against the iron rail, and their shoulders met through two sleeves, and neither of them shifted away.

"That was public," she said to the lake.

"I didn't plan it."

"I know you didn't. You never plan the ones that cost."

He had no answer to that, and she did not wait for one.

Then the lake broke.

It broke with a boom that took the quay up through its stones, a sound like a door the size of the sky slammed once on the south wind, and the whole grey sheet shuddered and split from the far shore to the steps. The crowd drew one breath together. Black water showed in the cracks, and the cracks ran and joined, and the slabs wheeled apart with a long grinding that rose to a roar and fell back to a hiss. Spray flew. A slab as broad as the hive terrace reared on its edge, hung there, and fell over flat with a slap that threw water across the lowest steps and sent a row of children shrieking back.

"That's the lake open," Bryony said quietly.

"It is."

"I know what that means."

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say the limes don't wait."

"I'll be done when she's done," she said.

He nodded. The slabs turned slowly toward the harbor mouth, grey and stately, each carrying its own weather of mist, and the last light from the west lay on them. On the largest a gull perched alone with its feathers puffed and rotated by degrees to survey the town, with the expression of a man who has been kept waiting.

"He thinks he's been robbed," Bryony said under her breath.

"That's Ottoline at the kitchen window."

It was out before he had weighed it. A sound escaped her, short and surprised, through her nose. The gull turned one more degree and fixed a yellow eye on the whole of Larkmere and found it wanting, and that did it.

Ewan's laugh started low in his ribs where he could not stop it, and a moment later it was in her shoulder too, shaking through two sleeves into his. She put the back of her scarred hand against her mouth and laughed behind it, helplessly, with her eyes shut, as she used to when a joke was too fine to be let out. It was the first time since the lane. He had not known how much of the lane's silence he had been carrying under his breastbone until it shook loose.

"Pale," she gasped, "for lake ice."

"It's grey."

"It's slate."

"Pale slate, then," said Ewan, and that finished them both.

"I forgot you did that," she said, when she could.

"Did what?"

"Made me laugh at the wrong moment."

"It's the ice."

"It's not the ice." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her scarred hand and faced the water again, and he knew better than to say more.

Heads turned along the quay, a few in surprise and the rest not. The man at the bollards studied the two of them the way a man studies weather he does not trust. Ewan wiped his eyes with his thumb and found the laugh already settling into a quieter one, and he did not mind that either.

The harbor lanterns woke as the light drained. A quay man worked along the posts with a taper, and the flame jumped from wick to wick, one gold light after another down the length of the quay, and the moving ice took each of them and carried it off across the water in pieces.

Down the shore the Net Sheds waited dark, and on their seaward wall hung the iron lantern with one green glass panel, unlit. It was always lit last.

He had told himself for seven springs that it was for the Heron. A ferry shut in a shed wants a lamp at her door, and a wall that faced no channel was as fair a place for it as any. He had said it to himself at every taper, and he had nearly come to believe it.

"There's a wick I should see to," he said.

She had been watching the ice. Her eyes swung round to him and rested a moment, and turned back to the water. "Go on."

She did not ask what wick. He left her at the rail with the quay at her back.

At the last harbor lantern he lit a taper and cupped the flame against the south wind, and walked the shingle with his hand held out before him like a man carrying a swarm. The ice grumbled and shouldered at the beach. The air tasted of cold iron and thawing earth. He reached the wall of the Net Sheds and stretched up on his toes, and his hands did what they knew, without him: the finger under the little iron door, the taper to the wick, the cuff drawn across the clouded panel. The flame lifted thin and yellow. The glass filled with green from the inside out.

The light fell down the wall and ran out across the broken ice in a long green road, and for a breath the whole slow drift of slabs turned the color of deep water in summer.

He remained under it with smoke on his hands and the taper burning down toward his fingers. It was for the Heron. A boat wants a light. He had said it so often that it held as well as any story holds when nobody leans on it.

He pinched the taper out and walked back along the shingle.

She was not watching the ice any more. She stood at the rail where he had left her, with the green light lying on the water at her feet, and she was watching the lantern. The quay had begun to thin. Mothers were gathering children. The last of the slabs had gone out of the lantern's reach and into the dark.

He took his place at her left. Their shoulders did not touch this time.

"That was lit late," she said.

"It's always last."

"Who keeps it lit?"

"Ottoline."

It left his mouth easily. That frightened him more than anything the lake had done. In years no word of his had run so smooth and been so false. A jar could not do it. A jar held the day as it was and gave it back whole to anyone with a spoon, and no jar of his had ever said anything but the truth, because it could not be made to. His mouth had no such law. He had faced the whole of Larkmere that evening and what he had said was a lie, and the green glass burned above it where anyone could see.

"Ottoline," Bryony said. She said it down toward the water, level, with no weight on it, as she might have read out the weather.

He waited. She studied the glass for a long moment.

"She wipes it well," she said.

"She does."

That made two. She nodded, once, and turned and climbed the shingle toward the green light and the loft, and she did not once glance at his hands.
