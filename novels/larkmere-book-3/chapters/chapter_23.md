By midmorning she had stopped telling herself what the blanket meant.

It lay folded on the long bench under the lantern, grey, with the scorched hem turned inward, and she had left it there. Ewan had gone up the Rise at sunrise with a raised hand and a face that said nothing at all, and she had let him go. The flow did not wait for anyone's feelings. Neither did oak. The third plank lay on the trestles with its steam-bent curve cooling into the set she wanted, and she had the straightedge on it, and the straightedge told her what it always told her, that she was a hair off at the heel and the hair was hers.

She was easing it with the plane when the light changed in the landward door.

Nobody stood there. The change was farther off, on the hill. From the doorway the thaw road showed above the roofs as a pale thread laid across the green, and a small dark shape was coming down it. She watched the way she would watch a cloud with a front in it. The shape was a man. He carried a pack high on his back and a stick in his right hand, and he did not walk like a man on a road. He walked like a man sounding a channel, one measured step and then the stick, one step and then the stick, as though the hill were water of uncertain depth and he did not intend to be caught out by it.

The plane went down on the bench. She did not remember setting it there.

There was a seam in her chest that had been tight since the afternoon a letter came folded small into her tool roll, and it was not tight now. It was open, the way a seam is open when the wax has not yet gone in, and everything on the other side of it showed. She knew that walk. She had followed it up a slip every morning for years.

She ran.

She ran through the shed door and along the lane with her boots slapping the wet stones, past the doors, and up where the lane turned into the road. The pencil slid out from behind her ear and she let it go. A woman at a gate lifted her head from a basin of washing and watched her pass and said nothing. The road climbed. The air got thin and cold in her mouth, and the cold smelled of tar and sheep, and the man on the hill had stopped, and he was leaning on his stick, and he was waiting.

She came the last forty strides as a hull comes down a slip, with no brake in it.

"Mind," said Abel Trent. "You'll have me over."

He did not move. She hit him, and he took her in with one arm, and the stick fell in the road. His other arm came round and closed on her back, and she was held against a coat that smelled of tar and wet wool and the cold of a long walk, by hands like spades. Nobody had held her like that in seven springs. She had not known how much of it she had forgotten.

"There," he said, to the top of her head. "There. You're riding light. I could carry you down the hill on one arm."

"You'd drop me."

"I'd drop the pack first."

He held her at arm's length then and looked her over, the way he had looked at every hull that ever came off his slip, with the white eyebrow lifting by degrees as though it were reading a gauge. She had sawdust in her hair. She did not brush it out. There was a new line at the corner of each of his eyes, and the stoop in his shoulders was a hand's breadth deeper than it had been, and she looked at those and said nothing about them, and he did the same.

"You'll do," he said.

"You said you'd write when you set out."

"I wrote I was coming. Here I am." He bent, with a grunt, and took up the stick. It was ash, grown straight and cured by use, the foot of it iron-shod and worn bright on one side. A man carried a stick like that when he no longer needed it and kept it for pointing. "Where's your ferry?"

"In the shed. Come and see."

"I'll see her when I've got my breath. Walk."

They went down together, with the pack riding on his back and neither willing to say why he had come. She did not ask. In all her time at his bench she had never once got more out of Abel Trent by asking. The hill dropped away ahead of them to the grey slate roofs and the quay, and the lake laid out beyond like a plank of pewter. He looked at it a long moment. He put the stick down in front of him and went on.

And there, in the middle of the glad uncomplicated warmth of walking beside him, she found it. It was not a thought. It was a pressure under the breastbone, the first trace of a draft in a shed that had been shut tight. The coast yard walked beside her, in a coat, with a stick. It was his stoop and his pack and the long slip going down to the water. It was everything she had ever wanted from a day's work, and it was an hour behind the hill, and he had walked all that way, and he had not said why.

She was gladder to see him than she could say. She was afraid, and it was the same thing she was glad of.

"You're quiet," said Abel.

"You're not saying anything either."

"I'm walking. It's a skill." He did not turn his head. "Is the garboard in?"

"The first of it. Two more to hang."

"Fair enough."

At the bottom of the lane the road turned along the harbor, and they came out onto the quay in the flat light, and Ewan was there.

He was crouched at the foot of a bollard with a bee sheet spread beside him on the stones and a skep tilted on its edge against the post, and the post was furred from base to cap with a living brown cluster that shifted and hummed and poured, a pour of bees as thick as a man's forearm. He had his sleeves rolled. He had smoke on his coat and a hive tool in his back pocket and a flat scrap of comb in the heel of his hand, and he had not noticed them. He was talking to the swarm in the low voice a shipwright keeps for a plank he is coaxing round a frame.

She stopped. Abel stopped beside her, because she had.

"Mind the sheet," said Ewan, not turning. "They're on the move."

His voice was as dry and as level as ever, and she felt her mouth move.

He turned then. He took in the pack and the stick and the white eyebrow in one glance, and she watched him understand. He got up off his heels without hurry. He laid the scrap of comb on the sheet, and he came across the stones, and he stopped a pace from her with the sheet and the bees at his back.

"Abel Trent," she said. "Ewan Marrick."

"Marrick," said Abel.

"Sir."

"You handle your swarms the way I'd handle a hull on a falling tide," said Abel. "Slow, and not looking at the water."

Ewan's eyes went to hers and back. "They go quicker if you don't hurry them."

Abel lifted a hand and set it flat against nothing, in the air, as if measuring a transom. He looked at Ewan, who stood with his hands loose at his sides and wax under his nails and the smoke still on him, and then he looked at her, and she knew he had read all of it from the one glance, as he read a hull from the cradle, the fairness and the fault in it together. Nobody spoke. A single bee left the post and crossed the gap from one man to the other and went on.

Ewan's hand came to the small of her back.

He did not look at it. He did not seem to know he had done it. It simply arrived, flat and warm through her shirt, the way a hand goes to a rail when the deck moves, and it stayed there, and she leaned back into it by an inch before she had thought, so that her weight settled, and for the length of one breath her whole body knew where it was. She heard him breathe in. She heard him not breathe out.

Abel said nothing at all about it. He turned his stick in his hand and looked at the lake.

"Show me your ferry," he said.

They walked the shingle to the Net Sheds with Ewan a half pace behind her and the stick ticking on the stones ahead, and nobody spoke of the hand, which had gone from her back at the first step as quietly as it had come. She could still feel where it had been. She felt it all along the shore.

The Net Sheds stood open. Abel paused on the sill with the stick planted and his pack at his feet, and took in the whole of the big shed as he took in anything, from the roof inward, with a long slow look that began at the rafters and ended at the floor. The props. The cradle. The frames, standing ribbed in the dusk like a boat on the stocks with her skin not yet on. The pale new planks stacked against the wall, and the old ones, grey with years, stacked beside them. The steam box with its copper boiler cold. The loft above, and the long yard ladder leaning in the stair gap.

"Good light to the stern," he said. "Plenty of room to swing a plank."

"There's room."

He grunted, and went in.

Ewan did not follow. He waited just inside the door with his back against the jamb, and the light lay across his boots. Abel crossed the floor with the stick tapping and his pack left behind him on the sill like a lashed load, and he stopped at the Heron's flank and put the back of one hand against her hull and stood.

He did not hurry. He never had. He went along her as he had once gone along every plank on his own slip, the whole length, bow to stern, with his head tilted a little and his eyes half closed and the white eyebrow gone low. He crouched under the turn of the bilge. He sighted along a strake with one eye shut, and she watched him find the thread of light between the fifth and sixth laps and say nothing and move on. At the stern, where the new garboard ran in, he halted.

She had set that plank with her own hands, in the dark, with a man holding the other end. She had steamed it and bent it and cooled it on the frame in one unbroken hold, and she did not know whether it was good. She had never known, in her life, whether a thing she had made was good until a person she trusted had put a hand on it.

Abel put his hand on it.

He laid it flat, spade of a palm and thick spread fingers, on the new garboard, and ran it forward. The hand moved along the plank as slowly as a plane taking its last shaving. It stopped at the lap, and went on. At the heel, where the old keel was soft and the new oak lay against it, the hand slowed and rested and pressed, hard, with his whole arm behind it.

"Fair curve," said Abel Trent. "And sound in the heel."

She let out a breath she had held since the thaw road.

He did not take his hand from the wood. He did not look at her. He turned his head, with his palm still flat upon the plank, and his eyes went across the shed, past the frames and the props and the cold boiler, to the man who stood against the door with the light behind him and the smoke on his coat. They stayed there. The old man's face did not change. His hand did not move.
