Ewan woke before light with the far hives in his head and could not shake them out.

They had wintered alone on the far slope with nobody's hand on a lid. Every year brought one morning when he could not wait on the limes, or the weather, or his own sense, and this was the morning. He shouldered the smoker and a bundle of spare frames in sacking and walked down through the orchard in the grey.

The apple rows were already humming. It was a thin early sound, the foragers working cold blossom before the sun could warm it, and he loved it better than any other he knew. It meant the year had made up its mind. Petals lay in drifts in the wet grass and stuck to his boots, and along the shingle a fresh fall of them rode the lake's edge in a pale scum, rocking.

Bryony was at the punt.

She knelt at the waterline with the canvas roll open on a stone beside her and one palm flat on the bottom boards. The punt floated at the end of her rope. She glanced at his boots first and then up the rest of him, and then back to the boards.

"You're early," he said.

"I put her in the lake at dusk and tied her short." She lifted her palm and showed him the damp stripe along it. "Nothing under the boards. Not a bead."

"Then I'll take her across."

"I know. I'm coming."

"It's the far hives. It's lids and frames and hours of staring at nothing."

"She's carried two of us out and back. I want her under a load, on a crossing."

"The load is me and a smoker."

"Then I'll see what she does with that." She was already rolling the canvas, drawing the thong tight in two turns. "You don't have to say yes."

"I wasn't going to say no."

He saw it land. She set the roll under the stern seat out of the wet and said nothing, and he found plenty to do with the sacking.

He stowed the smoker in the bows with the frames beside it. Under the forward seat the old pump was bolted where it had always been, its lever worn bright, and the tin scoop lay beside it. Bryony ran her eyes over both.

"You carry a pump in a punt."

"She's leaked longer than I've had her."

Her mouth began a smile and shut on it.

He pushed off and took the oars. The lake lay flat and clear as a pan of honey left to settle, with the orchard doubled in it, and the sun laid its first warmth along the back of his neck. She sat in the stern with her boots set wide to leave him room. Halfway across she set them again, closer, as if she had misjudged the punt, and he kept his eyes on the oar blades and rowed a stroke more evenly than he needed to.

"Blossom weather," he said. "Warm in the morning, wet by afternoon. It does what it likes."

"The sky's clear."

"It's blossom. It's always clear."

The Far Shore thickened as they neared it. The limes climbed in their long band up the slope with the new leaf beginning to haze the tops, and the old wall ran down through them, and along the wall ran the row of hives with the hut roof above. He ran the punt's nose onto the stones. She was out while he was still shipping the oars, hauling the rope up to a thorn root, and he noticed that. He had expected to be waiting on her.

He lifted the first lid with the hive tool, and the sound spilled out of the box at him.

It was warm and even, round at the bottom and threaded along the top with one thin high note. He laid his palm on the frame tops and the heat of the cluster climbed through his skin. The hive had wintered through. He waited with his eyes shut and his hand open, and did not know how hard he had been gripping the tool until he set it down and found the white line it had left across his fingers.

"Alive?" Bryony said from the wall.

"Alive." He lifted the next lid. "And heavy. She's eaten less than I feared."

"Is that a lot?"

"It's bread left in the cupboard at the end of winter."

He worked down the row, a lid and a frame and an ear laid to each. Every hive he opened was a debt struck off. When the limes broke into flower these would fill faster than anything on the Rise, because the limes flowed harder than any blossom in the district, and every full box would have to cross. The punt would carry one hive at a time and himself. He counted the boxes along the wall and counted the crossings in them, and stopped counting.

"One's on me," Bryony said.

A forager had landed on her cuff. She raised her arm and studied the bee against the light with her chin drawn in, the way he held a frame to the window to find the cracked cell.

"She wants the salt in the wool," he said.

"She can have it."

"She isn't asking."

The bee walked the length of the sleeve, thorough as a clerk, and lifted off. Bryony lowered her arm. She was nearly smiling, and she turned her face to the water so he would not see it finish.

Midway down the row the note changed.

He heard it before he knew he had heard it. The hum under his palm rose half a tone, and the high thread in it turned thin and hard, and on the landing boards the foragers began to arrive. They dropped out of the lime wood in dozens, in hundreds, and walked in without any of their usual patience. Along the whole row the same sound lifted, one note from every box at once, like a yard taking wing.

He straightened and turned west.

The hills had gone the color of pewter. Over the top of them a lower belly of cloud hung dark as a bruise, and under it the rain already trailed down the ridge in grey rags. The lake in front of it stayed perfectly flat. Not a catspaw touched it.

"The bees knew a quarter hour before I did," he said, "and the sky is still pretending."

Bryony had turned. She read the west faster than he had, and he watched her do the sum.

"How long?"

"Not long enough if we stand here."

He dropped the lids and left the frames on the wall; they could take rain. She was at the rope ahead of him, working the knot free of the thorn root with her fingers while he shoved the bows clear of the stones. The punt took them out on the long level reach with the hives roaring behind, and the wind had not yet arrived.

It arrived a third of the way over.

It hit as a single flat shove from the west, and it laid the lake down and then flung it up. The first wave slapped the punt on the quarter. The second slopped over the side. Under the oars the water had turned from clear to lead in the time it took him to breathe, and the rain hit behind it with a roar, so thick he could not see the Rise.

The punt had a hand's depth of side. The lake was now taller than a hand.

"Bail!" he shouted, and she was already on her knees in the bottom with the tin scoop.

He rowed into it two strokes more. The oars skipped on the chop and slewed in his hands, and a wave broke green over the bows and filled the forward boards to his boot tops. He knew then what he was rowing against. A punt that filled this far from either shore, on water that still carried the cold of the ice, would not float two people. A man's arms failed in that water before his sense did. And the season would go down with them: the punt was the only hull that could cross to the far hives until the Heron was whole, and the limes would not wait for either.

He dropped the oars along the bottom and dived for the pump.

The wind had the punt by the stern now and was shoving her east. He saw it a breath after he began: the same wind that was drowning them would carry them home, if they kept her afloat. He braced his boots against the ribs and threw his weight on the handle. Water spat from the spout in a thin gout and was torn off sideways by the gale. Bryony bailed behind him, scoop and heave, scoop and heave, with her hair plastered flat and sawdust long since gone to paste.

Another wave rolled over the side, higher than the last, and broke across her lap.

She surfaced spitting. Water ran off her chin. She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, put the scoop down, and laid her palm flat on the patched seam at the forward end. She kept it there while the lake poured in on every side.

"The seam's dry," she said.

He stared at her.

"It's dry. Look." She raised her palm and thrust it at him, streaming. "Every board on her is full of lake and that one's dry."

Then he lost hold of himself. The laugh started under his breastbone and burst out into the gale, and it was the wrong moment and it was entirely the right one, and she was laughing too, bent over the scoop with rain running into her open mouth. They shouted at each other and could not hear a word. He could not have said what was funny. Only that she had knelt in the lake to the knees with her patch holding and told him so, like a woman reporting one hive alive in a dead yard.

"It's pushing us the right way!" he shouted.

"It's drowning us the right way!"

The pump handle stopped at the bottom of its stroke.

He threw his weight on it and it did not give. He threw it again. Weed and a drift of petals had packed the throat, and the handle sat stiff as cold wax with the water rising over his ankles. He swore at it. Bryony let the scoop drop, crawled up the boards on her knees, and closed her hand over the handle under his.

Her fingers were cold as stones. The shape of them pressed into his palm, and under the heel of his hand ran the long raised ridge of the drawknife scar, which his hand remembered before he did. Neither of them spoke.

"On three," she said.

They hauled together. The handle tore up out of the throat with a suck and a rush, and the water poured from the spout in a rope. She did not take her hand away.

He did not ask her to. They worked it together, her palm under his, the handle rising and falling in the one stroke, down and up and down. Rain dripped from his hat brim and her hair. The lake shoved at the hull and he leaned into every roll without a word, and so did she, as if they had worked this handle together all their lives. Under the roar he heard another sound, low, a tune of three notes round and round. It took him a stroke or two to place it. She hummed when she worked. She had always hummed when she worked, and she did not know she was doing it.

He did not tell her.

A pale petal slid down his temple with the rain, from the dark wet hair above his ear, and settled on the back of her hand at the base of her thumb. He saw it there, pale on the old scar. His free hand lifted to his temple, found nothing, and understood. He put the hand back on the handle.

She was watching the petal too. She did not brush it off.

The next wave took the bows under and they both clung. When the bows rose, streaming, the rain was in her lashes in beads, and she lifted her eyes to his through them. She was a hand's breadth away. Water ran from her mouth to her chin. Her face kept nothing back at all, no guard on it and no plan, only the cold and the work and the look she had once turned on him across a shop floor when she did not know he was watching. His own name for her rose in his throat. It was not the careful one he had handed her in the lane like a full cup. It was the other, which he had not spoken in seven springs, and it rose whole.

A wave slapped the side and filled his mouth with lake.

He swallowed it. He swallowed the name with it, cold and entire, into the dark under his ribs, and worked the handle.

As it had risen, the wind dropped in a single breath. Then the rain thinned to a warm grey drizzle and a few drops that pocked the water, and ahead of them through the thinning veil he saw the Rise with its white orchard standing out of the wet like a lit lamp, and the quay beneath it, and the iron rings. The boat sat low and sodden, full to the thwarts. The seam at her bows stood dry.

He brought her in to the steps on the last of the swell. Bryony caught the ring and made the rope fast with two turns and a hitch, and climbed out onto the quay boards on her hands. He followed. His boots struck the planking and the wood stayed still beneath him. His knees began to shake and he locked them.

She stood dripping with her arms hanging, the flat pencil still behind her ear, which he could not account for. The petal was gone from her hand. Her breath was coming hard and she did not try to hide it. She met his eyes a long moment, and he had the sentence whole in his mouth, every word of it, the one he had carried the whole way across the lake and nearly spent in it.

"Ewan," she said.

"It'll clear by evening," he said.
