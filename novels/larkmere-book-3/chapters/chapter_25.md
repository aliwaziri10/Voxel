The new keel went home a little after noon, with eight people on the ropes and her own mallet calling the time.

She would not be hurried. The oak had come out of her father's stock in the Fairlie yard, the long baulk that had lain under the roof on three trestles with its ends tarred against checking, and she had been paring at it by lamp and by daylight since the thaw until the scarf closed on itself and would not pass a gleam. Now it hung under the Heron on slings and rollers, and the old one lay off to the side in the shavings, black and soft at the heel, finished. She went down the new one on her knees with the pot of Marrick wax and thumbed it into the seat from stem to stern. Mind the heel. At the heel she went over the wax a second time.

"Up a finger," she said.

The ropes creaked. Ewan knelt across the keel from her with the other mallet. He had his sleeves rolled and his hive tool in his back pocket, and the old stings on his forearm showed pale through the sawdust. He did not watch the timber. He watched her wrist, and struck when it fell, so that the two wedges took the load together and the oak rose level.

"Again."

They worked aft, wedge and wedge. The frames groaned as the weight came off the props and found the new wood. It was a low sound that ran up into her shins, and she held the others still with her raised hand until it had run out. At the sternpost she laid her cheek nearly to the floor and sighted along the joint. She drew the feeler down it. She crossed under the hull, with the whole ferry standing over her back, and drew it down the other side.

Nowhere would the cedar go in.

"She's down," said Bryony.

For a moment the people on the ropes only stood there, still hauling. A woman with her skirt knotted up let go first and blew on her palms. "We'll want lanterns tonight," the woman said, and went to see about it.

Abel Trent had watched the whole of it from an upturned crate by the water doors, with the ash stick across his knees. He had not offered a word or a hand. When the last rope was coiled he rose by stages, came down the shed and stood looking at the scarf.

"Who cut that?"

"You're looking at her."

"Hm." The white eyebrow stayed where it was. From Abel that was a great deal. "Walk me over to your father's shed. I want to sit where there's no audience."

The Fairlie yard smelled of linseed and dry larch. She swung both shutters back, and the light came in off the lake and lay across the bench, across the pegs and the gap on the pegs where the adze should hang. The adze was in her tool roll. She set the roll down and left the thong tied.

Abel did not sit. He went round the shed touching nothing, which was harder for him than the hill had been. He looked up at the roof, where the newer planking showed from underneath as pale strips among the dark, and he looked at the rack of seasoned stock, thinned now by one great length. He came to a stop at the slip door. The slip ran down green and short into the water. It would take a rowing boat, or a punt, or with luck and a high lake a hull of twice that.

"Your father built close to his means," he said.

"He built what the lake asked for."

"So he did." He turned the stick over and examined the iron foot of it. "My slip's long."

She knew how long. She had greased the ways of it on raw mornings with her fingers too cold to close, and once she had stood at the head of it and watched a hull she had planked from garboard to sheer slide down all that way and sit on the sea exactly on her marks. She picked a shaving off the bench and began to wind it round her finger.

"It'll be yours," said Abel Trent. "When I lay down my adze."

The shaving broke.

He did not look round to see how she had taken it. He held out his hands instead, the backs of them first and then the palms, in the flat light from the door. They were as broad as ever. The knuckles had thickened, and the smallest finger of the right no longer came straight, and while she watched, the left one trembled, very slightly, like the surface of a bucket when a cart goes by. He let it tremble. He made no move to hide it from her.

"I spoiled a scarf in the winter," he said. "Cut past my line. I've not done that since I had hair." He lowered his hands. "I'm not asking you to nurse me. I've no use for it. I want to know where the yard goes before these do. Your name on the transom of whatever comes off it. The tools, the stock, the slip, the lot. There's nobody else I'd leave it to, and I've looked. I looked hard, because I thought you might say no, and I wanted to be fair to the others."

"And were you?"

"They weren't good enough."

It was true. She could not find a rotten place in it anywhere, and she went over it as she had gone over the keel, from one end to the other and back down the far side. There was no trap in it. He had not said she owed him, though she did. He had walked over the hill on old legs to put a master's yard into her hands, the thing she had once wanted so badly that she had gone up the thaw road toward the mere chance of it, and the want had not rotted in the meantime. It was sound all through. It stood up in her now, tall and entire, and she saw the long shed with the doors open and smelled the salt in the timber and heard her own mallet going in it for the whole of her life.

Down the shore lay a ferry with a new keel under her and half her skin to hang.

"I need time," she said.

"Take it."

"I can't give you a day. I don't know the day."

"I didn't ask for a day." He settled the stick under his weight and moved toward the door. On the step he paused with the light on the white eyebrow and the water bright behind him. "At the launch," he said. "When she floats, you'll tell me. A boatwright ought to be able to answer with one hull off her hands."

"And if she doesn't float?"

"Then you've lied to eight people on ropes, and I've no use for you."

He went. She listened to the stick on the shingle until the lap of the water covered it, and then she untied the thong, took out the adze and hung it on its two pegs. She stood a moment. She took it down again and rolled it back with the others.

By dusk there were lanterns all along the quay.

They hung from the posts and the mooring rings and from a line strung between two oars lashed upright, paper ones and horn ones and the iron ones out of people's sheds, so that the whole harbor wall stood in a long yellow wash and the water under it carried the same again, shaking. Apples went round in a basket, and there was fish smoking over a pan. People who had not touched a rope were telling one another how heavy the keel had been. A fiddle had struck up at the far end by the steps, better than the last time it was tried, and the stones in front of it were filling.

She stood outside the light with her shoulder to a post. The roll was slung across her back. She had meant to leave it at the Sheds and had not been able to make her hand do it.

"A keel and no dancing," an old man said as he passed her with a cup. "Poor manners."

She would have answered, but the smoke reached her first, and it was not the smoke from the fish.

"Is the scarf still closed?" said Ewan.

"I looked at sunset."

"And since?"

"Twice."

"Then it can spare you." He had washed. His hair was wet at the ends, the folded veil was still in his belt because it had not occurred to him to take it out, and there was a rim of wax under each thumbnail that no washing would shift. He held out his hand, palm up, and waited. He had a great talent for waiting. She had never found the end of it.

"I dance badly," she said.

"I remember."

She put her hand in his.

His other hand came round to her waist, low, over the belt, and settled there, and the whole of her took its trim from it. She had thought she was ready. She had had that hand in the back of her belt over a long drop, and his arm round her while she shook, and none of it had warned her, because those had been for a purpose and this was for nothing at all. His fingers spread. Through the shirt she could count them.

They set off wrong. She went forward when he did, and their knees knocked, and she trod on his boot with her heel and then, correcting, with the other heel. A couple swung past and the woman's shawl slapped her across the mouth. The fiddle ran on ahead of them, quick and cheerful, and cared nothing for what she was doing.

"You're counting," he said.

"One of us has to."

"You're humming it as well. It's not this tune."

She shut her mouth. She had not known it was open. He steered her round the basket of apples, and she let him, and then did not let him, and drove them both against a post hard enough to set the lantern on it jumping. Under her palm his ribs gave a jerk. Her thumb had found the place low on his side where he could not bear to be touched, had gone to it without asking her, and he caught his breath and trapped her thumb there under his elbow.

"That's foul play," he said, close to her ear.

"It was an accident."

"It's an old accident."

He did not release the thumb. She could feel the heat of him through the shirt, as steady as the side of the steam box when the copper had been going all afternoon, and her whole forearm lay along it. The plank had taken a long while to cool, that other time. She had stood with his chest at her back and her arms burning and the oak fighting to be straight again, and neither of them able to shift a foot until it gave up and kept the shape. He drew her in out of the way of another couple, and when the couple had gone he did not let her out again.

His breath came down inside her collar. It stirred the short hairs there and the sawdust caught in them, and her neck prickled all the way to the shoulder, and her feet, left to themselves, began for the first time to go where his went.

She wanted to tell him.

It rose in her on a full phrase of the tune, and she had the words ready, plain ones, the only sort she had. Abel's offered me the yard. She would say it into his shirt, where she need not see his face. He would stop. Or he would not stop, which was worse. He would carry on turning her round the apples and say that it was a fine yard, he supposed, and that she would do well by it, and he would mean it, every word, with his hand exactly where it was.

That was how they had done it before. She had said what she was going to do, and he had let it be hers. She had stood on the thaw road with her whole chest waiting to be argued with and not known it was waiting. He had asked her for nothing. And she had asked for nothing either. She had not once said there might be a bench at the coast for her and a hill near it that would carry hives, or said come, or said wait. Each of them had left the other's work standing true and untouched, and thought it handsome conduct, and it was, and she had walked over the top of the hill with it.

Now there was a master's yard in her pocket with her name on the transom, and she was dancing on her father's quay with the first life she had had, which was warm and smelled of smoke and had just trodden on her foot. She was about to do it again. She could feel herself preparing the clean fair way of it, in which she would trouble nobody to move over.

"You've gone stiff," he said.

"Green timber."

His chin brushed her temple. Over the slope of his shoulder the lanterns swung round, the oars, the pan of fish, the steps, and then the dark end of the wall where the light gave out. A man sat there on a bollard. She could make out the stick planted upright before him and his two hands folded on the head of it. The rest was shadow, until a paper lantern turned on its string and showed her the white eyebrow, lifted high.

Abel did not nod. He looked at her, and at the hand on her waist, and at her, as he would look from a loaded gauge to the man who had set it.

The turn took him away. She came round with her pulse knocking in her throat. When the dark end of the wall swung into view once more he had not shifted, and the eyebrow had not come down.

"Ewan."

He drew his head back far enough to see her. The yellow light lay on one side of his face, and he waited, patient, dry, with a bee's old sting white on his jaw, ready for whatever she cared to hand him. She had it all there on her tongue. She looked at the open collar of his shirt.

"Your veil's coming out of your belt," she said.

"Is it," said Ewan. He did not look down to see. He watched her a moment longer, and then he laid his cheek against her hair, and his hand moved an inch round to the small of her back and drew her up against him, and they went on.

The tune quickened toward its end. People were clapping it home. She shut her eyes and lost the wall and the water and whoever sat beside it, and there were only his fingers, spread and warm, and his breath going in and out against her neck, and her own feet making a poor job of the last turns. If it lasted she need not do anything. She held on to the cloth at his side and willed the bow to keep sawing.

The fiddle stopped.

It stopped on a long high note that thinned out over Larkmere Water, and the couples round them broke apart laughing and fanning their faces and calling for cups. His hand stayed at her waist. She waited for it to lift. The quay emptied from the middle outward, and a lantern guttered on its oar, and down at the steps she heard the player let the bow knock once against the wood and lay it by.
