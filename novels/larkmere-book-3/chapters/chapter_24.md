He heard the stick before he saw the man.

It came up the Rise ahead of its owner, iron meeting stone, a pause, and iron again, as even as a forager walking a bar of sun. Ewan was bent over the third row with a lid in one hand and the smoker hanging from the other, and he did not straighten. He finished with the frames. The brood in the corner of this one lay in a patch the size of his palm, capped and orderly, and the hum under it had no drag in it at all. A colony at ease. Of everything he looked at that morning, it was the one thing that behaved.

"Steep," said a voice at the gate. "For a hill that is only a hill."

Abel Trent stood with both hands crossed on the head of the stick and his breath going short and slow. The pack was gone from his back. The white eyebrow was raised, the look of a man who has found a hill steeper than it showed from the bottom and means to say so.

"It has a path," Ewan said.

"It has a path that wants to be a different path." He looked along the terrace, the rows of hives in their weathered boards, the willow bent to its last catkins, the hawthorn gone over to green. "They told me the honey was up here."

"It is."

"I have a mind to buy a jar."

"I have a veil," Ewan said, "if you mean to come through the yard."

"I've had worse than a bee. I've had a boom across the ear." But he had come two paces through the gate, and when a worker lifted off the nearest landing board and made a slow, considering pass round his head, he stopped and held very still. The bee settled on the white eyebrow. It walked the length of it and stood there, washing its face with its forelegs.

Abel's eyes crossed with the effort of not looking at it.

"It likes you," Ewan said.

"It's a fair judge of timber."

"That's a flower. It thinks you're a flower."

"At my age I'll take it."

The bee lifted off after a while and went about the business it had come for, and the old man let out a breath and walked on, with the stick ticking and his chin held high, the way a man walks through a yard he has been told is all awake. Ewan followed him to the Honey House, and neither of them spoke of what had walked in at the Net Sheds door the day before, or the hand on the new plank, or the eyes that had come across a shed and stayed.

The Honey House was cool after the yard. Abel stopped on the sill, and his gaze went round the shelves as it had gone round the Sheds, from the beams down. He took in the long rows of plain jars with their paper strips, all turned to the room. He took in the top shelf. His eyes rested on the squat glass jar there with its blue linen and its disc of wax and its blank side outward, and they did not stay on it long. He asked nothing about it. Ewan, who had been braced for the question for years and had a short answer ready, found that he had nothing to do with it.

"I want what you cut last," Abel said. "Whatever's newest."

"The newest is spring. It's the cut from the clover and the hawthorn."

"Is it good?"

"I don't know." He took a jar from the end of the lower row and a horn spoon from its hook. "I never taste mine. It tastes of the man who cut it, on the day he cut it. You'll find that out."

"Will I," said Abel.

He undid the string with his big, flat fingers and lifted the cloth, and Ewan set the spoon in his hand and moved away to the stove, where there was nothing to do and he did it. It was not courtesy. A keeper does not watch a man taste. He had learned long ago what it costs to be looked at while a neighbor finds out who you were on the day you cut, and he had gone on learning it every spring since, from the backs of heads. He turned the kettle a quarter turn on the plate. Behind him a spoon went into a jar, and then there was no sound at all.

He counted the silence the way he counted a hive that had gone quiet, by what it did not hold. The stove ticked. Out in the yard the hum went on, content and indifferent. He gave it as long as he could, and then he turned round.

Abel stood with the spoon in his hand and his face as shut as a hive in the cold. Nothing moved in it. The white eyebrow lay level. The jaw was set. It was the face of a man who has been handed a hot dish and has resolved to feel nothing of it until he has put it down, and the care in it was so complete that Ewan read the whole of it at once, the way a keeper reads a hive wall that is too quiet, and knew better than any tongue could have told him what he had put in that jar.

Abel set the spoon on the bench, gently, as one lays down a full frame.

"Boy," he said.

It was said low and without anger. It was said the way a man speaks to a hive that has stood out all winter and come through.

"She won't stay small," Abel said. "You know that. I've known it since the first month I had her. A hand like that wants water. It isn't a thing a man can talk it out of, and I never tried." He turned his head, and the shut face met Ewan's across the cool stone. "I didn't come up this hill to take her off anyone."

There were a good many things Ewan could have said. They rose and queued like workers at a landing board with a fouled entrance. The one that got through was the dry one.

"I never asked her to stay."

"I know."

Two words. They fell between the jars as flat as a lid onto a super, and nothing in the old man's face made them either kinder or harder. Ewan stood in the sweet cold of the room with his hands at his sides and wax under his nails and knew that every beekeeper in Larkmere would have told him the honey could not lie, and that the man in front of him had just had a mouthful of it.

Abel capped the jar. He did it with great care, laying the cloth over the mouth and tying the string with two turns of his thick fingers, and when he had finished he slid the jar into the pocket of his coat, where it made a round weight against his hip.

"I'll take this one," he said.

"Take it."

"You'll want paying."

"Take it, and don't tell the town you've been given it. They'd all want one."

That got the corner of a mouth. It was not a smile. It was the place a smile would have gone, in a face that had found other uses for its muscles.

"I'll tell nobody," said Abel Trent, and went out into the light with the stick ticking on the flags.

Ewan did not follow him. He stood in the Honey House with the horn spoon lying on the bench where it had been set down, and after a while he picked it up, because it was there. It was cool and had a little honey still on the bowl of it, and he did not put it to his mouth. He went out into the yard and along the terrace to the wall at the end of the hives, where the Rise fell away, and he stood with his forearms on the warm stones and watched the old man go down.

The path took him in long careful switches. The ferrule struck, struck, struck. Below him the roofs of Larkmere lay in the clear dry light, and below them the harbor, and beyond the harbor the shore west of the quay where the Fairlie yard stood with its shutters back.

A figure sat on the step.

He knew her by the way she sat, with her shoulders up round her ears and her elbows on her knees. She had a plane iron across her lap and a stone in her hand, and the long strokes of her arm were slow and exact even at that distance. He could not see her face. He saw the old man come to the foot of the yard, and stop, and speak. He saw her head come up.

Then her head went back.

The sound did not carry. A hive-yard in the swarm season has a roar in it that swallows everything smaller, and the hum came up around him now like warm water. He saw only the long white line of her throat, and her shoulders let go of her ears, and the stone fall out of her hand onto the step. She put the back of her wrist against her eyes. She was laughing as he had not seen her laugh since the thaw, with her whole body and her mouth open, like a woman let out of a small room.

It was the easy way that struck him. It was a laugh that asked for nothing and cost her nothing. She had laughed for him at ice-out behind a scarred hand, quick and half stolen, and had put it away again. Nobody on that step had anything to sort out first. An old man had walked down a hill with a jar in his pocket and said one thing, and she was bent double and helpless on her father's step.

He hated it. He took the hatred out and looked at it plainly, as he would have looked at a sting that had gone in too deep to be scraped, and it was an honest one and did not improve with handling. He hated how little it took. He hated that he had not once been able to bring that sound out of her, in all the weeks of planks and oat cakes and weather, and that a man with a crushed ear had done it in the time it took to climb down a hill. Under the hatred, where it had always been, the other thing ran on without noise. He wanted her. He wanted the throat and the helpless shoulders and the stone gone out of her hand. He wanted to be what the next laugh was for.

His hands lay on the wall, sticky from the jar, and they were quite still. He kept them so.

She was a woman who ought to have the widest water there was. He had known it in the loft with her face against his throat, and he had known it on the thaw road seven springs back, and what he had never been able to settle was what a man did with the knowing. If he asked her to stay he would be putting a lid on her. If he did not, he would be watching her walk down a hill with an old man's stick going ahead of her, and holding his hands still to the end of it. Neither choice was a kindness. He was a man who had spent his life learning to tell a swarm from a robbing, and he could not tell this.

Down on the shore the old man had reached the step. She had caught her breath. He saw her get up and put her hand on his arm, and the two of them went in under the lintel of the yard together, and the shutter swung to behind them.

The step stood empty. The hives droned on. The light came across the Rise in its long afternoon bars and laid itself over the stone wall and over his hands, and a bee crossed his knuckles and went down to the clover.

He found he was still holding the spoon.
