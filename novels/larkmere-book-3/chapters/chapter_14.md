The wind blew warm off the orchard that morning and carried the blossom up the Rise in slow drifts, and every hive along the terrace answered it with a hum that Ewan could hear from the gate.

He worked down the first row with his palm laid flat on each roof, a habit older than his reading of them. The sound ran up through the wood into his wrist. Low and even meant a colony busy at its own affairs. Thin and high meant a queen gone missing, or a robber at the door. These were low, and under the low a note of gladness ran, the note a hive makes when the flow has begun and nobody has yet been asked to hurry. He stopped at the third row and rested his palm on the roof of the hive that had died in the winter. Its door was empty and swept. He took his hand away and walked on.

A smoker sat cold at the end of the row where he had left it. He did not light it. He checked the entrances with his thumb instead, clearing a dead bee here and a wax moth's thread there, and when the sun reached the terrace wall he heard the gate latch and knew the step.

Tobiah climbed the path with his hands drawn inside his sleeves and the veil dragging off his shoulders, a veil cut for a man a head taller. It had been Ewan's own, and the boy had worn it to the knees every season since and would not hear of another.

"You're late," Ewan said. "The supers on the second row want weighing."

"I'll weigh them."

"First I want the new cake of wax carried down to the Net Sheds. It's cooled in the Honey House. She'll want it for the garboard."

Tobiah halted on the path. His eyes slid past Ewan to the third row, to the empty hive, and returned flat.

"You carry it."

"I carried the last."

"Then you can carry this one."

"Tobiah."

"I'm not going down there."

Ewan set the hive tool on the nearest roof, because his hand had begun to close on it. He had kept his voice even since the squall, and before it. A beekeeper learns to keep a voice level. It is half the trade. He heard the level go out of it now like air out of a bellows.

"She is your sister."

"She was."

"She is your sister, and she has stood in that shed in the cold with your father's keel on the floor, and you will not walk forty steps along the shingle to hand her a block of wax."

"Forty steps and then what? She says thank you? She says it to you, not me." The boy's ears had gone pink at the rims, then pink to the tips, as they always did. He pulled his hands out of his sleeves. They were shaking, and he put them back. "You carry it. You like going."

"That is not the reason."

"Then what is?" Tobiah's voice cracked on the last word and he drove straight over the crack. "You tell everyone it's Ottoline who lights that lantern. You tell Bryony. Every dusk you go down the Rise with a taper in your coat and every dusk you say it's Ottoline's. I've watched you do it since I was ten, and it's a lie, and you tell it so easy. You lie about everything. You lie about her."

The words entered Ewan like a sting through a cuff, narrow and hot and exact, and for a moment he did not breathe. All along the terrace the hives kept humming. A forager dropped out of the blossom onto the nearest landing board and walked in.

He did not say it was not a lie. It was one. He had told it to her at the rail, with the green glass lit behind him and the lake clearing in broad grey slabs, and the jar on the top shelf of the Honey House had stood the whole while with its blank face turned to the room. A jar cannot say what is untrue. A mouth can, and his had.

Behind Tobiah's anger lay a fact Ewan had kept to himself for a long time. Under the boy's bed, pushed to the wall where the broom would not find it, sat a brown boot-box tied with twine. He had never needed to ask what was in it, because every post was delivered to the Marrick house. Every letter she had written her brother lay in that box, and the twine on it had never once been cut.

He had never said a word about it. He did not say one now.

"Go and weigh the supers," he said.

Tobiah stood another breath, with his chin up and his ears burning, and let go a whistle through his teeth, two notes, which meant nothing at all; it was only the noise a cornered boy makes. Then he turned and climbed the terrace, and the veil dragged after him in the grass.

Ewan stayed with the hives until his hands stopped.

The Honey House was cool and dim at the end of the yard, and it smelled as it always smelled, of wax and warm stone and a thousand mornings. He entered sideways out of habit, and the door closed behind him on the hum. Rendered cakes lay in rows on the low shelf under the north window, pale gold, each one cooled in its own pan and turned out clean. He took the newest in both hands and let its weight settle into his palms.

On the top shelf behind him, at the height of his shoulder blades, the Leaving Jar stood with the blank side out.

He kept his back to the shelf. He always did. He wrapped the cake in a clean cloth, tucked the corners under, and held it a moment against his coat, and the room kept its silence at his back as a hive keeps its cluster, close and dark and warm and not to be disturbed. Whatever it held, he had put it there on the morning she left. He had never tasted it, and nobody had, and it sat on the shelf and waited.

Out in the yard he found that the cuts on his thumbs from yesterday's cappings had opened again, and he wiped them on his coat.

The blossom was thickest along the lane. It piled on the cobbles in drifts that the wind lifted and laid down again, and it clung to his boots and to the cloth around the wax. He walked down past the closed shutters on Lantern Street with his head down, and nobody spoke to him, and he did not mind. The harbor lay bright. The quay held only the gulls at that hour. He took the shingle at the western end and walked along the water with the cake in the crook of his arm.

The Net Sheds stood ahead of him with their long roofs steaming in the sun, and on the seaward wall hung the iron lantern.

By day it was a plain lantern. Its one green pane was clouded with soot, and the wick had burned down nearly to the brass, and one corner of the glass still carried the clean arc his cuff had left there at the rail. Passing under it, he kept his eyes on the shingle. He would need to trim that wick before dusk. He knew already that he would go down with the taper, and he knew that he would say nothing, and that the boy was right.

The water doors stood open. He stopped outside them in the sun.

She was bent over a plank laid across the trestles with a plane in her hands, working the stroke long and low, and she was humming. He heard it before he saw her face: the tune of three notes, round and round. It stopped the moment her eyes lifted and found him at the door, and he saw her hear that it had stopped, and he did not say a word about it.

A new pencil sat behind her ear. The old keel rested on its balks in the shavings, black as a burnt comb. The board he had left against the jamb was gone from the doorframe and lay on the trestles beside her plank.

"Wax," he said, and held up the cloth.

"Put it on the bench."

He crossed the floor and set it down. She laid the plane on its side, dusted her palms together, and unwrapped the cloth with two fingers, and the pale cake lay in the light off the water with the clean shine of a cake newly turned from its pan. She bent over it. Her hair had sawdust in it again.

"Tobiah?" she said, without raising her head.

"He wouldn't come."

She was silent a moment. "No. He wouldn't."

"He's seventeen."

"He's seventeen and he hasn't forgotten anything." She said it evenly, to the wax. Then she lifted it from the bench and pressed her thumbnail into the corner of the cake, to feel what it would give. "It's soft."

"It's the weather. It'll firm in the shade."

"I need it firm."

"Then it will be."

"I need to see the underside." She turned the cake in her hands, and found she could not hold it and see it both, and without looking up she said, "Take the other end."

He took it.

The wax was warm from the walk. His palm took the near edge, and his fingers curled under, and she tilted the cake to the water light, and the two of them studied the underside, which was flat and clear and showed no seam or pit. She set her thumbnail against it again to try the surface. His own thumb lay at the corner. The nails met.

It was nothing, the edge of one horn against another. But all the stillness took him at once. His breath stopped. Heat ran up his arm from the thumb to the shoulder as if he had put his bare hand into a hive in July, and the whole of his attention sank into the wax and the two nails touching on it, and it did not come back. He knew what it was to hold still while a bee walked over his skin. He held still now, as he had held still in the lane. He could not have stirred if the shed had caught fire.

She did not take her thumb away.

Neither of them spoke. The lake sucked at the shingle outside. Blossom drifted in through the doors and settled on the floor. The block of wax lay in the span of their four hands, pale and smooth and a little too warm, and he watched her face and did not see it change, which meant that she was holding as still as he was. A long time passed, longer than any wax required. The light off the water shifted on the underside of the cake. He heard his own pulse in his thumb.

He noticed that he was holding his breath as he held it when a bee crawled onto his lip, and he let it out through his nose, slowly.

She drew her hand back first. She did it without hurry, as a woman lifts a hand from a stove lid she means to touch again.

"It's sound," she said. "It'll do for the garboard."

"I'll bring the next when the weather turns."

"Bring it before." She gave him the cloth and did not look at him. "I'll be at the yard door. The rest of the oak is in there."

She took the plane from the trestle and passed him into the sun. She did not ask what he had to say, and he did not offer it.

He stood alone in the shed with the cake of wax on the bench and the shape of her nail still bright on his thumb. After a while he folded the cloth, because there was nothing else to do with his hands, and followed her out.

She was crossing the shingle toward the Fairlie yard with the plane in her fist. The yard door stood open at the end of the beach, as it had stood since the first oak was carried out of it, and in the doorway, with the light behind him, there was a boy.

Ewan stopped where he was.

Tobiah had come by the back lane, which was the quicker way, and he must have run it. He was breathing hard and he was not wearing his veil. In his hands, held out in front of him as he would hold a frame he did not trust, he had a smoothing plane, an old one with a worn beech body and a brass lever cap that had lost its shine, and Ewan knew whose it had been before he saw the name cut into the toe.

Bryony halted six paces from the door.

Neither of them spoke. The wind laid the blossom across the shingle in a thin slant. Ewan did not step nearer. He stood on the shingle with the cloth in his hand, a man standing at the edge of a hive terrace with nothing to do but keep his hands to himself.

"Show me how," Tobiah said.

His voice was rough. His ears were pink to the tips, and he did not take his eyes off the plane.

She studied him a long moment, and her mouth shut and opened.

"Not like that," she said.

She crossed the six paces. She did not take the plane out of his hands. She put two fingers under his wrist and turned it, and shifted his other hand to the knob, and set the sole flat to the end of a Fairlie board that lay across the sawhorse in the doorway, and stepped back.

"Lean on the front. Then let it run."

Ewan did not move. He was a long way off, and had no business to be there at all.

Tobiah leaned. The plane caught, ran, and took off the first clean shaving from the Fairlie board, a single pale curl that rolled up out of the throat and fell at his feet in one piece, as cappings fall from a hot knife.
