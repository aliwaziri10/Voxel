The thaw road gave under Bryony's boots, mud and meltwater spattering the cuffs of her trousers as she walked above Larkmere. Winter had loosened its grip slowly, the ice on Larkmere Water crumbling at the edges into grey slush, the harbor lanterns still dark against the late afternoon light. She had not been back seven springs, not since she had packed her tool roll and caught the thaw road toward the coast, but the town still sat where she had left it, the long roof of the Net Sheds a familiar dark line against the sky.

Ahead of her, the Fairlie yard stood shuttered, its roof kept mended against the kind of weather that came off the lake without warning. The Heron rested on its cradle in the big shed, her oak hull quiet under the eaves. Bryony thought of the keel, soft at the heel, but she did not name it; she only thought the ferry, resting, waiting.

A sound behind her made her turn, and a small swarm dropped out of the early spring air, buzzing uncertainly. Bees settled on her shoulder, on her pack, the brim of her hat. She went still, every muscle settling into the quiet the moment demanded, and watched the cluster form. The bees chose her shoulder and did not sting; they only landed where they liked, ordinary bees on an ordinary afternoon.

Footsteps crunched above the slope from Marrick Rise, running light and fast. Ewan Marrick came into view, the skep under one arm, a bee brush in his hand, his coat smelling of smoke and the kind of old stings he ignored. He had not been expecting her; his face changed as he came closer, surprise and something like recognition warring before he caught himself.

"Don't move. They're only deciding." The words were flat, certain, and Bryony held herself perfectly still as Ewan set the skep down and came close, the woven straw brushing her elbow. He moved with the patience of someone who had spent years among hives, his hive tool in his back pocket, wax under his thumbnails, smoke lingering on his coat from the smoker he had tended that morning. He held out his bare hands, palm up, close to her face, and the bees drifted into the skep one by one, drawn by the stillness and the warmth of his hands.

When the last bee had gone, Ewan stood up and looked at her. His jaw tightened, a fleeting movement she had seen a hundred times when he opened a hive and found the queen was lost or the stores were low. "I did not know you were coming," he said.

"I was sent for by the keeper of the Net Sheds." Her voice was plain, the sentences she had planned on the road already dissolving into something else, something she had not accounted for.

The town lay below them, the Net Sheds lanterns dark, the Far Shore of the lake hazy in the distance. Lime trees stood on the Far Shore, and beyond them Ewan's far hives, the Honey House with its top shelf holding the Leaving Jar, though Bryony did not think of that now. She thought only of the bees in the skep, the way Ewan had moved without hesitation, his hands certain and close.

His hand came up, almost without thought, to the collar of her coat. His fingers brushed the bees that still shifted beneath the fabric, and Bryony felt her pulse jump in her throat. She did not look at his mouth. The space between them was not empty; it was filled with the small, precise things she had not said and the quiet that had brought her back to the road she had left seven springs ago.

A single bee walked up her sleeve, crossing the fabric toward her wrist, and neither of them moved.
