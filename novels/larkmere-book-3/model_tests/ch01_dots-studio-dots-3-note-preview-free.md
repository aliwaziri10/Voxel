The thaw road was a track of ruts and standing water, the last of the winter's ice pushed to the edges in grey slabs that caught the low light and threw it back dull and spent. Bryony walked it with her pack on her shoulders and her canvas tool roll thumped against her hip with every other step. The roll was oiled and tied with a leather thong, and the thong had worked loose on the road so that the handle of her drawknife showed through the canvas like a pale scar across the back of her left hand, the same scar that ran over the knuckles where the blade had once slipped.

She stopped where the road bent and the town lay below her, and she looked out over Larkmere Water.

The ice was gone grey and rotten at the edges, lifting in sheets where the wind got under it and the lake breathed beneath. The far shore was a dark line under the last light, and the long roof of the Net Sheds caught the sun and held it, a broad plank of seasoned timber left to dry in the open. The harbor lanterns were not lit yet; dusk was still a thin line on the far shore.

She stood there and let the quiet settle over her like sawdust. Her father's yard was down there to the west, shuttered for seven springs, its roof kept mended against the rain. She did not look at it. She looked at the sheds, at the water, at the line of the thaw road as it came down into the town. She had walked that road down to the town and then up again, seven springs ago, and she had not walked it since.

A sound came from above, small and busy, a hum against the wind.

She looked up.

A small cloud of bees, early and restless, dropping out of the air like a handful of dust thrown wide. They found her shoulders first, then her pack, then the brim of her hat. They walked on her sleeves and settled in the folds of her coat, small bodies moving in precise steps, the legs catching on the wool and pulling free again. The brim of her hat was heavy with them, a living fringe.

She went still.

Her heart beat once, hard, against her ribs, and then she held her breath. The hum grew louder, a single note held steady, the sound of a hive deciding where to go. She could feel the weight of them, light as ash but real, a living drapery over her shoulders and across the canvas of her pack. The bees on her hat cast small shadows on her face.

She had planned every sentence on the road. She had said them all in her head as she walked the ruts, each one weighed like a plank for fair curve and grain, and none of them survived the first bee. The first bee had landed on her sleeve and walked up toward her wrist and she had stopped speaking mid-word. The plan had died there, in the middle of a word she could not remember.

Footsteps came, quick and light on the mud. She did not turn. She knew the sound of that tread, the way it carried the weight of someone used to running uphill from the hives on the Rise. She knew it the way she knew the sound of a plank tapping true on a workbench.

Ewan came into sight, moving fast, the skep in one hand and a bee brush in the other. His coat was dark with smoke and the folded bee veil was tucked into his belt. Wax showed under his fingernails, pale and old, and his forearms carried the small white marks of stings he never spoke of. A hive tool hung from his back pocket, the handle worn smooth from years of opening and closing.

He stopped a pace away and looked at her. His face was the same, she thought, and then she did not think it. It was the face she remembered, but the light was different and so was she. The scar across his cheekbone from a bee years ago, the way his hair was cut short at the back and left longer at the front, the small line between his brows when he was working. The smell of smoke on his coat was the same.

"Don't move," he said. His voice was lower than she remembered, steady, the way it had been when he spoke to a frightened hive. "They're only deciding."

The words hung in the air between them, plain and quiet. He set the skep down on the ground and knelt. The bees on her shoulders stirred, restless, and he lifted his hands, bare, and let his breath move toward them. The smoke on his coat rose faintly in the cold air.

She watched his hands. They were the same hands that had worked the hives when she was there, that had touched her wrist once in the dark when the lantern in the Honey House had gone out. She looked at his mouth and then looked away, because the light caught the small scar there and she did not want to know how she remembered it. She did not want to know that she remembered the shape of it exactly, the way a boatwright remembers the curve of a garboard.

A bee walked up the sleeve of her coat, slow, legs moving in small precise steps. It reached the cuff and paused, wings beating against the wool, a small engine held in place by nothing. She could feel the tick of its legs on the fabric.

Ewan's hand came up, slow, and hovered near her collar. He was coaxing the bees, his fingers moving in small circles, his breath steady and warm. She could feel the warmth of it on her neck, a small cloud of breath and smoke. His hand brushed the bees on her shoulder, a light touch, the back of his knuckles grazing the wool. The bees did not mind. They moved on.

Her pulse was in her throat, a hard beat against the collar of her coat. She swallowed once and said nothing. She held her arms at her sides and let the bees walk. She did not look at his mouth. She looked at his hands, at the bees, at the skep on the ground.

He had said nothing else after the first line. He worked the bees with his hands and his breath, close to her face, and the space between them felt full of the hum of them, a single note that filled the lane and left no room for anything else. The lane was empty but for them. The town below was quiet. The lake lay grey and still under the last light.

She could feel the small movements of his fingers as he worked, the way he angled his hand to catch the light on the bees' wings, the way he held his breath for a moment and let it out slow. His hand was close enough that she could feel the heat of it on her cheek. She did not lean in. She held her place and let him come to her.

The bees on her hat had gone first, drawn by the skep. The ones on her pack were next, small bodies lifting and drifting. The ones on her shoulders held longest, clustered on the wool, and she could feel their legs moving against her coat like small pins.

She thought of the first hive she had ever opened, her father's hand over hers on the hive tool, the way the comb had smelled of wax and smoke. She thought of the way Ewan had looked at her the day she left, standing on the quay, his hands in his pockets and his mouth set. She did not think of that now. She thought of the road, the ruts, the plan of sentences that had died under the first bee.

Ewan's hand brushed her collar again, a small movement, and the bees on her shoulder shifted. He was steady. He did not speak. He let his breath carry them, one by one, toward the skep.

She watched the bees, counted them in the way she counted planks in a timber, and felt the quiet stretch. The skep sat at his feet, a woven basket, and the first of the bees were crawling into its mouth, their wings beating against the straw. One bee remained on her sleeve, walking slowly up toward her wrist.

She said, "I was sent for by the keeper of the Net Sheds."

The words came out flat, practical, the way she would say it to a man in a yard about a plank that had checked. She did not know if he would understand what she meant, only that it was the truest thing she had said since the bees had landed.

Ewan's hands did not stop moving. His face changed, a small shift around the eyes, the mouth set. He had not known.

The bees hummed between them, a single note, steady as a held breath.
