The lamp had not been lit. The wick stood dry in the glass, a black curl against the grey square of window. Kael lay on his back, the stick leaning on the wall where he could reach it, and said the numbers to the ceiling.

"Sixteen and a half. About thirty-three."

The lane outside was quiet. No carts, no voices, not even the rooster that usually announced the hour from the tanner's yard. He waited as if she might answer, the way they did at night when the bed held two bodies instead of one.

"Thirty-three is a figure. It is not a complaint."

He turned his head. The stick caught the pale light, its ferrule worn to a shine, the grain smoothed where his palm had settled night after night.

"Thirty-three is exactly what a man says when it is a complaint."

The stick did not reply. He addressed it anyway, his voice flat and dry.

"You are the only witness. You have seen me at my worst. You will say nothing in court."

A pause. He noticed the absurdity, then kept going.

"I have been talking to a stick. Before that I talked to a sock. Before that I talked to a window. This is how the Ashworths end. The window does not reply."

His hand moved toward the stick, fingers closing on air. He pulled it back. Moved again. Pulled back. On the third reach he took it, the wood warm against his calluses.

"I am not going to the anchor."

He said it to the ceiling, to the stick, to the empty pillow beside him.

"She asked. My not going is the only thing I can do for her this morning."

He repeated it, slower, the words falling like entries in a ledger.

"I am not going to the anchor. She asked. My not going is the only thing I can do for her this morning."

The second time he sounded like a man reading a ruling against himself.

He set the stick across his knees. The gauge-book lay on the table, its cover worn smooth at the corners. He rose, the left leg stiff and uncooperative, and crossed the room in three uneven strides. His thumb found the initials in the margin, I.V., faded to brown. He pressed until the ink warmed under his skin.

Then he turned back to the bed. His palm flattened on her side of the mattress, the linen cold. He held it there, the fibres catching on his calluses.

"Isolde."

Not a count. Not a figure. Just the name, quiet in the dark room.

Light came in a long grey bar across the floorboards, stretching toward the table. He did not need her to come back tonight. He said it to the empty air, to the stick, to the window that had already refused to answer.

"I do not need you to come back tonight. But I would like it known that the bed is the wrong size."

The lane stayed silent. The grey bar widened, pale dust motes drifting in the slant.

He sat at the table and opened the gauge-book. The needle stood at seventeen, fixed since Day 7. Sixteen and a half. About thirty-three. The arithmetic that governed everything and explained nothing. He traced the column with a fingertip, the paper rough under his nail.

The yard gate rattled.

Kael's head lifted. The sound came again, heavier this time, the clang of iron on iron. He set the gauge-book down and took up the stick, levering himself up with a breath that caught in his chest. The left leg held, trembling.

The gate stood at the end of the yard, barred from inside. Beyond it the lane was empty, grey cobblestones slick with last night's rain. But the pick stood there, a figure in Council grey, motionless as a boundary stone. Two men flanked the pick, unnamed, silent, their hands on the crossbar.

Joren and Corren held the gate on this side. Joren's scar caught the light as he leaned on his spear. Corren's face was blank, his jaw set.

"No," Joren said. One word. The fewest he could make it.

The pick did not speak. The two men did not speak. The gate did not move.

Kael descended the ladder to the yard, the stick tapping each rung. His boots hit stone. He stopped three paces from the gate.

"Joren and Corren."

He said it aloud, the way he always did before going anywhere. A rule. A habit. The names anchored him.

Joren glanced at him, once, then back at the pick. Corren did not look.

The pick raised a hand. A gesture, not a signal. The two men shifted weight but did not release the crossbar.

Kael watched the pick's face. Grey cloth, grey eyes, a mouth that had forgotten how to move. The order had run out at the end of the day before. Thirty days from the night of the petition. The custodian's pick had been named on Day 26. The formal demand read at the Lower Reach on Day 28. The attempt on Day 29. Today was Day 31. The order was ash.

The pick knew it. The two men knew it. Joren and Corren knew it. Kael knew it.

No one moved.

He turned from the gate and walked to the cellar door, the stick striking a rhythm on the stones. The boiling house yawned behind him, its vents breathing mineral air. The cellar stairs dropped into shadow.

Odo Marl stood at the bottom, broad shoulders folded against the damp. Tessa Rook crouched beside a coil of rope, chalk-line at her belt, checking a knot for the third time. They looked up when his shadow fell across the landing.

"No one goes below until Sol is back."

Kael's voice carried down the stair. Odo's jaw tightened. Tessa's fingers stilled on the rope.

"Myself included."

Odo nodded once. Tessa tied off the knot and clipped the rope to her belt. She did not ask why. She did not argue. The techs knew the plan. They knew the rig. They knew the pegs waited somewhere in the dark behind the iron door, the ledge peg and the table peg, same mold, placed by a hand that had meant to be found.

Kael did not tell them where the pegs sat. That was settled in ch.38, not here. He said only the cords.

"The cords stay coiled. The plate hums. Nobody pulls."

Odo grunted. Tessa stood, wiping chalk dust on her trousers.

Kael turned back up the stair. The cellar door yawned behind him, the passage mouth a black rectangle at the foot of the second stair. He had stood here before, in ch.26, with the plate warm under his palm and the line waking below. Today the plate hummed, a low vibration that travelled through stone and iron and into the soles of his boots. The attempt on Day 29 had woken it. It did not burn. Nothing was paid. The gauge still read seventeen.

He planted the stick against the wall beside the passage door. The ferrule found the groove worn by generations of boots. He set his feet on his side of the threshold, one boot heel touching the lintel stone, the other planted on the landing.

The hum rose through his legs, a resonance in the bone. The air in the passage moved, slow and warm, carrying the mineral tang. He held the door frame, fingers white on iron. His left leg screamed, a white-hot line from hip to knee. He did not shift. He did not step across.

The plate hummed. The line stayed awake. The stored seventeen waited in the dark.

He held the frame. The breath he held did not end.

Ansa came up the lane in the afternoon, a pot cradled in both hands. She moved slow, her brown knotted fingers white on the handle, steam rising thin and straight into the grey air. She did not look at the gate. She did not look at the pick. She walked past the boiling house yard and up the ladder to the room over the tar-shop, her boots silent on the rungs.

Kael was at the table, the gauge-book closed under his hand. He heard her before he saw her, the creak of the trapdoor, the thud of the pot on the hearth.

"She ate."

Ansa's voice was the same as it had been on Day 23, at the Lower Reach, when the public step had ended and the Reach had no handfast custom and her whole answer had been a single word. Eat.

Kael rose, the stick taking his weight. The left leg was worse today, the numbness climbing, the knee locking without consultation. He made the crossing in four steps.

"Thank you."

He did not ask more. He did not ask where, or when, or how much. He did not ask if the broth had been thick or thin, if the barley had been soft, if the fat had risen in yellow eyes. He knew Ansa's hand. He knew the count. He knew the habit.

Ansa nodded. She wiped her hands on her apron, the cloth grey with tar and time. She climbed back down the ladder without another word.

The pot sat on the hearth, steaming. Kael lifted the lid. Thick, grey-brown, barley and root and something that might have been dried mushroom. No meat. Meat cost years she did not have.

He drank from the pot, tilting it to his mouth. The heat spread through his chest, a small fire in the cold. He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and set the pot down.

The hum from the passage had not stopped. He could feel it in the floorboards, a tremor under his boots. The plate was awake. The line was drawing. The stored seventeen waited.

He returned to the table and sat. The stick leaned against the wall beside him, patient, warm, worn to the shape of a hand that was not his. The gauge-book lay closed. The lamp was still unlit. The grey bar of light had crossed the floor and climbed the opposite wall, narrowing as the afternoon wore on.

Dusk came grey and slow. The lane emptied of the last cart. The tanner's rooster crowed once, late and ragged, then fell silent.

Joren appeared at the trapdoor. He climbed down with the ease of a man who had not spent the day standing at a gate, his spear vertical, his scar pale in the fading light. Corren followed, two paces behind.

Kael did not rise. He watched them from the table.

"The cold has passed the elbow."

Joren's voice was quiet. Flat. The warden's tone.

Kael's fingers tightened on the gauge-book cover. The initials I.V. pressed into his palm.

He set the stick back against the wall. The ferrule found the groove. He sat at the table and waited for the morning.

The night deepened. The hum from the passage did not waver. Kael did not move from the table. The gauge-book stayed closed under his hand. The stick stayed warm beside him. When the grey bar of light returned across the floorboards, he was still there.
