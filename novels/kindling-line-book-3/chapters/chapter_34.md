The lamp had not been lit. The wick stood dry in the glass, a black curl against the grey square of window. Kael lay on his back with the wall at his shoulder, where he could reach it, and said the numbers to the ceiling.

"Sixteen and a half. About thirty-three."

The lane outside was quiet. No carts, no voices, not even the rooster that usually announced the hour from the tanner's yard. He waited as if she might answer, the way they did at night when the bed held two bodies instead of one.

"Thirty-three is a figure. It is not a complaint."

He turned his head. The plaster beside the bed was rubbed dark at the height of his hand, where he had hauled himself upright night after night.

"Thirty-three is exactly what a man says when it is a complaint."

The wall did not reply. He addressed it anyway, his voice flat and dry.

"You are the only witness. You have seen me at my worst. You will say nothing in court."

A pause. He noticed the absurdity, then kept going.

"I have been talking to a wall. Before that I talked to a sock. Before that I talked to a window. This is how the Ashworths end. The window does not reply."

His hand moved toward the plaster, fingers closing on air. He pulled it back. Moved again. Pulled back. On the third reach he took hold of it, the wall cold and grainy against his calluses.

"I am not going to the anchor."

He said it to the ceiling, to the wall, to the empty pillow beside him.

"She asked. My not going is the only thing I can do for her this morning."

He repeated it, slower, the words falling like entries in a ledger.

"I am not going to the anchor. She asked. My not going is the only thing I can do for her this morning."

The second time he sounded like a man reading a ruling against himself.

The gauge-book lay on the table, its cover worn smooth at the corners. He rose along the wall, the left leg stiff and uncooperative, and crossed the room in three uneven strides with one hand trailing the plaster. His thumb found the initials in the margin, I.V., faded to brown. He pressed until the ink warmed under his skin.

Then he turned back to the bed. His palm flattened on her side of the mattress, the linen cold. He held it there, the fibers catching on his calluses.

"Isolde."

Not a count. Not a figure. Just the name, quiet in the dark room.

Light came in a long grey bar across the floorboards, stretching toward the table. He did not need her to come back tonight. He said it to the empty air, to the wall, to the window that had already refused to answer.

"I do not need you to come back tonight. But I would like it known that the bed is the wrong size."

The lane stayed silent. The grey bar widened, pale dust motes drifting in the slant.

He sat at the table and opened the gauge-book. The needle stood at seventeen and had not moved. Sixteen and a half. About thirty-three. He traced the column with a fingertip, the paper rough under his nail, and found that he was not reading it. He was only keeping his hand on a page that did not change.

The yard gate rattled.

Kael's head lifted. The sound came again, heavier this time, the clang of iron on iron. He set the gauge-book down and put a hand to the wall, levering himself up with a breath that caught in his chest. The left leg held, trembling.

The gate stood at the end of the yard, barred from inside. Beyond it the lane was empty but for the pick, a figure in Council grey, motionless as a boundary stone. Two men flanked the pick, unnamed, silent, their hands flat on the bars.

Joren and Corren held the gate on this side. Joren's scarred hand lay flat against the post. Corren's face was blank, his jaw set.

"No," Joren said. One word. The fewest he could make it.

The pick did not speak. The two men did not speak. The gate did not move.

Kael went down the stair to the yard with one hand on the wall, taking each tread slowly, the left foot arriving a breath after the right. His boots hit stone. He stopped short of the gate.

"Joren and Corren."

He said it aloud, the way he always did before going anywhere. A rule. A habit. The names anchored him.

Joren glanced at him, once, then back at the pick. Corren did not look.

The pick raised a hand. A gesture, not a signal. The two men shifted weight but did not release the bars.

Kael watched the pick, in Council grey, hands folded, nothing of a face to read. The order had run out with the day before. The paper that named the pick was ash now, and the pick stood at the gate anyway.

The pick knew it. The two men knew it. Joren and Corren knew it. Kael knew it.

No one moved.

He turned from the gate and crossed to the cellar door, his shoulder grazing the wall of the boiling house, his left foot dragging a little on the wet stone. The vents breathed mineral air behind him. "Joren and Corren," he said again over his shoulder, and heard Joren's grunt come back across the yard. The cellar stairs dropped into shadow.

Odo Marl stood at the bottom, broad shoulders folded against the damp. Tessa Rook crouched beside a coil of rope, chalk-line at her belt, checking a knot for the third time. They looked up when his shadow fell across the landing.

"No one goes below until Sol is back."

Kael's voice carried down the stair. Odo's jaw tightened. Tessa's fingers stilled on the rope.

"Myself included."

Odo nodded once. Tessa tied off the knot and clipped the rope to her belt. She did not ask why. She did not argue. The two of them knew the plan. They knew the rig.

Kael did not say where the cords ran. He said only what stayed still.

"The cords stay coiled. The plate stays quiet. Nobody pulls."

Odo grunted. Tessa stood, wiping chalk dust on her trousers. Neither of them looked at the empty place beside him where she would have stood, and he was grateful, and ashamed of being grateful.

Kael turned back up the stair. The passage door stood open ahead of him, a black rectangle at the head of the second stair. He had taken this door once before, aloud, in front of Odo and Tessa. Today the plate lay quiet below him, and the line was awake without a sound, the way a sleeper is awake with the eyes shut. The attempt had woken it. It did not burn. Nothing was paid. The gauge still read seventeen.

He set one shoulder against the wall beside the passage door. He set his feet on his side of the threshold, one boot heel touching the threshold stone, the other planted on the landing.

The air in the passage moved, slow and warm, carrying the mineral tang, and the iron of the frame was warmer under his hand than any iron had a right to be. He held the door frame, fingers white on iron. His left leg screamed, a white-hot line from hip to knee. He did not shift. He did not step across.

The plate lay quiet. The line stayed awake. The stored seventeen waited in the dark.

He held the frame. The breath he held did not end. He did not call it anything. A man standing in a doorway has not done what the doorway could see.

Ansa came up the lane in the afternoon, a pot cradled in both hands. She moved slow, her brown knotted fingers white on the handle, steam rising thin and straight into the grey air. She did not look at the gate. She did not look at the pick. She walked past the boiling house yard and up the stair to the room over the tar-shop, her boots silent on the treads.

Kael was at the table, the gauge-book closed under his hand. He heard her before he saw her, the creak of the stair, the thud of the pot on the hearth.

"She ate."

Ansa's voice was the one that had once given a whole answer in a single word. Eat.

Kael rose, one hand on the table and then on the chair back. The left leg was worse today, the numbness climbing, the knee locking without consultation. He made the crossing in four steps.

"Thank you."

He did not ask more. He did not ask where, or when, or how much. He did not ask if the broth had been thick or thin, if the barley had been soft, if the fat had risen in yellow eyes. He knew Ansa's hand. He knew the count. He knew the habit. He wanted to know whether Sol had sat or stood to eat it, and he kept the question behind his teeth, because the answer was hers to tell.

Ansa nodded. She wiped her hands on her apron, the cloth grey with tar and time. She went back down the stair without another word.

The pot sat on the hearth, steaming. Kael lifted the lid. The broth was thick and brown-grey, barley and root, a skin of fat gone gold at the edges. No meat. He ate standing, from the pot, with the spoon she had left hooked on the rim and one hand flat on the table. It tasted of cold stone and patience, and he understood that this was how it had tasted to her, two hundred steps down, and that she had eaten it anyway.

The passage had not changed, and no sound came up from it, though he kept listening for one past the sound of his own pulse. The plate was awake. The line was drawing. The stored seventeen waited.

He returned to the table and sat. The corner by the wall where the stick usually stood was bare, and he kept looking at it. The gauge-book lay closed. The lamp was still unlit. The grey bar of light had crossed the floor and climbed the opposite wall, narrowing as the afternoon wore on.

Dusk came grey and slow. The lane emptied of the last cart. The tanner's rooster crowed once, late and ragged, then fell silent.

Joren appeared at the head of the stair. He came up with the stiffness of a man who had spent the day standing at a gate, his scarred hand on the rail, his face grey in the fading light. Corren followed, two paces behind.

Kael did not rise. He watched them from the table.

"The cold has passed the elbow."

Joren's voice was quiet. Flat. The warden's tone.

Kael's fingers tightened on the gauge-book cover. The initials I.V. pressed into his palm.

He laid his hand flat on the closed book and waited for the morning.

The night deepened. The passage gave no sound and did not change. Kael did not move from the table. The gauge-book stayed closed under his hand. When the grey bar of light returned across the floorboards, he was still there.
