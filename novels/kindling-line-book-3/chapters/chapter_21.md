The morning air bit at the fingers and the face. Sol pulled her undyed coat tighter at the throat, the copper clasp catching the pale light that filtered through the grey sky. Kael stood beside her at the stair head of the Lower Spine anchor, the stick in his right hand, his left leg taking its weight without tremor. The Thorne house steward waited at the top of the descent, a narrow man in Thorne grey with violet cuffs, his face designed for not being remembered. He held the visitors' book open. His pen hovered.

"Lady Vane," he said. "Warden Ashworth."

"Isolde Vane," Sol said. She signed. Her hand leaned the other way from Kael's, the letters upright, the descenders open. Kael signed beside her, his square hand moving sure and quick. The steward closed the book without comment. He did not offer a lamp. He did not offer a guide. He stepped back against the stone and let the dark stair yawn before them.

Sol lifted the oilcloth bundle that held the leaf and Renn's sheet. The diagram lay folded inside, its edges worn smooth by the facing stone that had hidden it for years. Kael reached for the lamp hook at the stair head. His fingers closed on cold iron.

"I will carry the light," he said.

Sol adjusted the bundle against her hip. "You will carry the stick. The leg decides."

"It is not the leg today."

"It is the leg every day until it is not." She fell into step behind him as the stair narrowed to a single file. The stone was damp underfoot. Water dripped from a crack in the wall, a slow metronome. The lamp flame steadied in the still air.

Kael's voice came back to her, low and rhythmic. "One. Two. Three. Four."

He counted under his breath, the way Odo had taught them. At forty Sol joined in without being asked. "Forty-one. Forty-two. Forty-three."

They counted side by side where the stair allowed it, one behind the other where it did not. The walls pressed close. The air grew colder. The light fell in a circle around their feet.

At one hundred and seventeen Kael said, "One hundred and eleventeen."

Sol's voice was flat. "One hundred and eighteen."

He paused a beat. "I did that on purpose."

"You are a very bad liar."

A huff of breath, almost a laugh. "The worst."

They continued. The stair turned, a tight spiral cut into the living rock. Their shoulders pressed together for twenty steps, neither leaving a gap. The wool of his coat was rough against her sleeve. At one turn her hand found the small of his back for balance and stayed there, fingers spread over the warmth beneath the fabric. He did not shift away. The count continued, his voice steady in her ear.

One hundred and thirty. One hundred and thirty-one. One hundred and thirty-two.

Somewhere past one hundred and fifty she stopped counting aloud. He kept going for both of them, quiet now, so she could think. She noticed. She did not say thank you.

The spiral opened into a round chamber. The size of a small church, the digest had said. A dark stone slab as long as a barge lay in a pit veined with old silver. The anchor plate stood at the north end in a shallow socket: black iron, a hand's breadth thick, wide as a door, scorch fanning out like a scorched hand. The upper stair had been shut by Thorne since the accident. Malrik's concession had opened it.

Kael lowered himself onto the last step. The stick rested across his knees. The lamp sat beside him, throwing long shadows across the vaulted ceiling.

"I will sit here," he said. "The room is yours."

Sol nodded. She walked to the anchor plate. She did not touch it. The iron was cold, or seemed cold after the stair. The scorch marks fanned from the socket like fingers frozen mid-gesture. Her mother had sat here thirteen years ago. She had walked out on her own feet and never fully walked back into her body. She had died at forty-two, three winters after her Reckoning. Sol had been twelve at the Reckoning.

"Sixteen and a half," Sol said to the plate. The words fell into the chamber and returned, quiet. "Eighteen, less half a year at the petition, half at the third-gallery plate, half at the test burn. Sixteen and a half."

She set the oilcloth bundle on the stone floor beside the pit. She unwrapped the leaf first. The paper was stiff, the ink brown at the edges. The newest row — Vane and Ashworth — carried the witness line with Aldous's name, the hand upright, the tail closed, practised, careful to look younger than it was. Her mother's row was older. The lines of figures marched across the columns. The total at the end stood a year above the sum of the lines. The difference sat in a second, darker hand under the word carried. The hand leaned to the right.

One year.

She laid the leaf flat. Beside it she set Renn's sheet, the folded survey of the east that Ulla had given her. One cellar marked warm. The boiling-house cellar. She took the diagram from behind the facing stone at the base of the east wall, the oilcloth supple in her fingers. She unfolded it on the stone beside the other two.

The thin dotted line from the anchor's south edge ran east toward the cliff face and the Lower Reach, off the paper. Renn's sheet marked the boiling-house cellar warm. The dotted line ended where the warm mark sat.

The anchor and the second line were one system.

Sol stood over the three papers. The lamp light caught the copper clasp at her throat. Her mother's carried year. One year, in the second hand that leaned right. The stored sum in the gauge read seventeen. The years were not traceable to their owners. Renn had said the gauge was fourteen when he came down and seventeen now: the three-year rise came off the nearest living body while he was locked in. The text called it Renn's one line, never explained.

Sol knew only from the leaf that a year had been carried off her mother's row. She could not know it was in the seventeen. She decided to count it as hers anyway. The decision sat in her chest, heavier than the bundle had been.

She wanted to be angry with her mother for the contract. She wanted to find the hot spark of betrayal, the clean flame of resentment. She searched for it in the chamber where her mother had sat, in the scorch marks that fanned like a hand, in the cold iron that had taken years from the woman who had given Sol life.

She found only the count. Sixteen and a half. One year carried. Seventeen stored. The numbers aligned and misaligned and aligned again.

Her fingers brushed the leaf's edge. The paper was rough, the fibres catching her skin. The carried hand leaned right. The works steward's hand. The works steward who had taught Aldous his letters, who had written the second hand on every row for forty years, who had crossed out her own name the day she began to carry.

Sol's mother had not been the works steward. The leaf showed Vane rows, not Thorne rows. But the carried hand on her mother's row leaned right, the same lean. The same hand that had written the second hand for forty years.

A decision. Not proof. The text would say it was a decision.

She heard Kael shift on the step behind her. The stick tapped once against stone. The lamp flame wavered.

"You are quiet," he said.

"I am thinking."

"Thinking what."

She did not turn. "Whether the road from this plate to the boiling house runs both ways."

"It runs one way. The stored years move to the nearest living body when the pegs pull. The cost moves to the nearest living body when the line burns. Same rule. Opposite directions."

"And if the nearest living body at the boiling house is the pick."

"Then the pick takes the stored sum. The cost falls on the next nearest. Thirty feet away. The margin we measured."

"The margin is fear."

"The margin is the rule."

She turned then. The lamp light caught his face, the line of his jaw, the grey at his temples. His pale eyes held hers. The stick lay across his knees. The table peg rode in his pocket, warm against his hip. The ledge peg rested in hers. Twin pegs. Same mold. Placed.

"Whoever closes the east line," she said, "closes the far end of my mother's road."

Kael did not answer. The chamber held the silence. The drip from the crack in the wall marked time. The line breathed twelve beats in the dark below, the east pulse adding its breath to every cycle.

Sol picked up the leaf. She folded it carefully, the way Renn had taught her, the creases aligning with the original folds. She placed it back in the oilcloth. Renn's sheet followed. The diagram last, its dotted line pointing east, toward the cliff, toward the Lower Reach, toward the boiling house where the gauge read seventeen and the second line stored years carried off other people over forty years.

She wrapped the bundle. She tied the twine. The knot seated clean.

"Ready," she said.

Kael rose from the step. The stick came up with him, fluid, practiced. He took the lamp from the stone. The flame steadied.

They climbed.

The count began again at the first turn. Kael's voice, low and rhythmic. One. Two. Three. Four. Sol joined at forty. They counted side by side where the stair allowed, one behind the other where it did not. Their shoulders pressed together on the narrow turns. Her hand found his back again, the warmth through wool.

At the top the steward waited, the visitors' book closed, his face giving nothing away. The grey sky stretched above the cliff. The yard below held Odo's broad shape at the gauge hut, Tessa's chalk-line snapping.

Sol stood beside Kael on the landing. The wind tugged at her coat. The copper clasp caught the light. She looked at the east, where the dotted line ran toward the cliff face and the Lower Reach, where the boiling-house cellar sat warm under Renn's mark, where the second line breathed twelve beats and stored seventeen years.

"A share of the seventeen may be my mother's," she said. One sentence. The wind took it.

Kael did not flinch. The wind tore at his coat, the grey wool snapping, but his face stayed turned toward her, pale eyes steady in the pale light. The stick did not move in his hand. The table peg pressed against his hip through the pocket wool.

He did not ask what she meant. He did not tell her the years were not traceable. He did not remind her that Renn had said the gauge rose from the nearest living body, not from a row on a leaf.

He said, "We knew the road ran both ways."

The words were quiet. The wind nearly took them. But they landed between them, heavier than the bundle in her arms.

Sol nodded once. The copper clasp caught the light, threw it back dull and warm. "We knew."

The steward had not moved. His face gave nothing away. The visitors' book stayed closed against his chest. The violet cuffs were the only colour on him. He watched them with the patience of the stone he stood on.

"Will you be wanting the book signed again on the way out," he said. It was not a question. His voice was flat, unused to curiosity.

"No," Sol said. "We are done here."

The steward inclined his head. A fraction. Barely visible. He turned and pulled the heavy door shut behind him. The iron groaned on its hinges. The latch fell with a finality that echoed in the stairwell.

They stood alone on the landing. The yard spread below them — the gauge hut, the tar shop, the rope bridge swaying faint in the wind. Odo's broad shape moved at the hut, reading the gauge. Tessa's chalk-line snapped, sharp as a whip crack. The line breathed twelve beats in the dark below. The order ticked down in Kael's pocket. Thirteen days to the formal demand. The pick named on Day Twenty-Six.

Kael shifted his weight. The stick tapped the stone. His left leg held. "The map comes tomorrow. Aldous said noon. The room over the tar shop."

"I heard."

"Renn's survey. The peg locations. The rope rig. The puller positions. The signal code. The timing."

"I heard."

He waited. The wind pushed her hair across her face. She did not brush it away. Her fingers stayed curled around the oilcloth bundle, the twine biting into her palm.

"What are you thinking," he said.

She looked at him then. The grey sky behind him. The line of his jaw. The stick in his hand. The table peg warm against his hip. The ledge peg warm against hers. Twin pegs. Same mold. Placed.

"That the works steward carried for forty years on the order of a dead man," she said. "That she taught Aldous his letters so someone would know the hand. That she wrote his name on the witness line herself so a clerk would compare hands and ask who taught him. That she wanted to be found. Not by the Council. Not by Thorne. By someone who would understand."

"She was found," Kael said. "By Aldous. By us."

"By Aldous," she agreed. "By us. And she locked Renn in the third gallery to keep him away from the pegs, not knowing the line would draw on him. She crossed out her own name the day she began. She asks to be given to no one until the line closes."

The words lay between them. The wind held them. The line breathed.

"She is not my mother," Sol said. "The leaf shows Vane rows. Not Thorne rows. But the carried hand on my mother's row leans right. The same lean. The same hand that wrote the second hand for forty years."

"A decision," Kael said. "Not proof."

"A decision," she repeated. "The text will say it is a decision."

He nodded. Once. The stick did not waver. "Then we decide together. When the time comes."

"The time comes on Day Twenty-Eight. The formal demand. The Lower Reach. The custodian reads the order. The pick stands at the plate. Odo and Tessa pull the rope rig in one breath. Both pegs. Simultaneous. If the timing fails by a heartbeat, the pegs bind. The line does not close. The stored sum stays. The order runs out. The Council sends another pick. Another attempt. Another year."

"We have thirteen days to learn the room," Kael said. "Fourteen to the end of the order. We learn the pegs. The rope rig. The pullers. The signals. The contingencies."

"Everything," she said.

"Everything."

He fell into step beside her as they left the landing, descending the broad stair that led from the anchor complex down to the yard. The stone was worn smooth by generations of boots. The air warmed as they dropped, the smell of tar and iron replacing the cold damp of the deep stair.

They did not count aloud. The shared count had done its work. But their footsteps fell in rhythm, heel-toe, heel-toe, the same pace, the same stride. At the turn where the stair narrowed, his shoulder brushed hers. Neither stepped away.

The yard was busy. Odo looked up from the gauge hut, his broad face creasing in something that might have been a greeting. He raised a hand. Tessa snapped her chalk-line again, the sound cutting through the evening. She did not look up. The gauge read twelve. Twelve. Twelve. Holding.

Mara's room was over the tar shop, at the back, where Yelva's healer space gave onto the yard. Sol's eyes found the window. The curtain did not move. Mara's cold was at the forearms now, climbing on nights after someone had gone below. The draw would end when the line closed. The line would close when the pegs pulled. The pegs would pull when Odo and Tessa hauled the rope rig in one breath, beyond the room's reach.

Thirty feet. The margin they had measured. The margin that was fear. The margin that was the rule.

Kael stopped at the tar shop door. The stick leaned against the jamb. He turned to her. The grey light caught the silver at his temples, the line beside his mouth, the pale eyes that had watched her all the way up from the anchor plate.

"Four words," he said.

She waited. The wind tugged the bundle in her arms. The copper clasp was cold against her throat.

"I learned from you," he said.

Her mouth moved. Not a smile. Something quieter, deeper, that did not need a name.

"The worst student," she said.

"The worst." He let it settle. "But I am learning."

She nodded. Once. The bundle shifted in her arms. She turned to the door. The latch lifted. The door swung inward on hinges that had known forty years of Ansa's hands.

The room over the tar shop held the smell of yeast and ash, of cold tea and bread crusts on wooden trenchers. The grey light deepened through the window. The primer was gone, tucked into Aldous's coat. The map would come tomorrow. Noon. The room over the tar shop.

Thirteen days. Fourteen. The pick named on Day Twenty-Six.

Sol set the oilcloth bundle on the table. She untied the twine. The knot gave way clean. She unfolded the diagram first, the dotted line pointing east, toward the cliff, toward the Lower Reach, toward the boiling house where the second line stored seventeen years and breathed twelve beats.

She laid Renn's sheet beside it. The warm mark on the boiling-house cellar.

She laid the leaf last. The newest row — Vane and Ashworth — with the witness line, the upright hand, the closed tail. Her mother's row with the carried year in the right-leaning hand.

Three papers. One system. One road.

She did not touch the anchor plate. She had not touched it. She would not touch it until the close, if she stood at the rope's end and Kael stood at the plate, or if the pick stood at the plate and Kael stood at the rope's end, or if the pick refused and the Council sent another.

The wind rose outside. The chalk-line snapped. The gauge held twelve.

Sol pulled out the chair. She sat. The wood was warm from the afternoon sun. She looked at the three papers, at the dotted line, at the carried year, at the seventeen stored.

Kael sat beside her. The stick propped against the table leg. The table peg warm against his hip. The ledge peg warm against hers in her pocket.

Twin pegs. Same mold. Placed.

Thirteen days. Fourteen.

She reached for the tea pot. The liquid was cold. She poured anyway. Two cups. She pushed one across the table to him.

He took it. His square hand closed on the ceramic. He did not drink. He watched her.

The grey light deepened. The yard sounds rose and fell. The line breathed twelve beats in the dark below.

Sol lifted her cup. The steam rose, thin and pale. She did not drink. She watched the steam curl and vanish.

"Tomorrow," she said. "We learn the room."

"Tomorrow," he agreed. "We learn everything."

The cups sat between them, untouched, cooling in the grey light. The three papers lay flat. The dotted line pointed east. The carried hand leaned right. The stored sum waited in the gauge.

Thirteen days. Fourteen. The pick named on Day Twenty-Six.

Neither of them moved. The room held its breath.
