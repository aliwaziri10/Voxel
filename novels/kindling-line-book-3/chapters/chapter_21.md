The morning air at the stair head of the Lower Spine anchor was cold enough to hurt the teeth. Sol pulled her undyed coat closed at the throat and shifted the oilcloth bundle on her hip. Beside her Kael stood with the stick in his right hand and his weight on both legs, and he did not look at the leg, and she did not look at it for him.

The Thorne house steward waited at the head of the descent. He was a narrow man in Thorne grey with violet cuffs, and his face had been made, or had made itself, for not being remembered. It was a face you could study for a full minute and be unable to draw afterward. He held the visitors' book open on his forearm and a pen in the other hand, and he said nothing at all until they were close enough that he did not have to raise his voice.

"The book," he said.

Sol took the pen. She wrote her name on the next ruled line, *Isolde Vane*, the letters upright and open, and the date beside it. Kael signed under her in his square quick hand. The steward looked at neither signature. He closed the book on his forearm without comment and stepped back against the stone of the wall. He did not offer a lamp. He did not offer to walk down with them. He stood the way a post stands in a yard, there because a hand had driven it in.

Behind him the stair opened into the dark.

Kael took the lamp from the hook at the stair head. The iron was cold enough that she saw him flex his fingers once before he closed them. "I will carry the light," he said.

"You will carry the stick," Sol said. "The leg decides."

"It is not the leg today."

"It is the leg every day until it is not."

He looked at her for a moment, and then he went down first. She followed. The stair was cut into the rock, a tight spiral worn hollow in the middle of each tread by generations of boots, and it was only wide enough for one body at a time. Water ran down a crack in the wall and dripped on the stone at the turn, slow, a sound like a counted pulse. The flame in the lamp stood up straight and did not move. There was no draught here. The air had lain in the stair so long that it had taken on the temperature of the rock.

"One," Kael said, under his breath. "Two. Three. Four."

He counted the way Odo had counted on this stair, flat and even, not for anyone's benefit, only to keep the number in order. She had heard Odo do it the first time she came to the Lower Spine, when she had stayed at the register desk with the book and listened to two men go down past her into the dark and their voices fade to a murmur of numbers. She had not gone down with them then. Kael had gone, and Odo, and she had stayed with her pen. She had never asked herself why it had seemed right. It had simply been where she was put.

At thirty-nine she found that her mouth had begun to move. At forty she said it aloud.

"Forty-one," she said. "Forty-two."

Kael did not turn. His shoulders eased by the width of a finger. "Forty-three."

They counted together after that. Where the stair allowed it they went side by side, with his sleeve against hers and the lamp between them throwing one pool of light on the steps, and where it did not they went one behind the other and she kept her eyes on the back of his coat and listened to his voice come up through the dark. The numbers went by. The air got colder. Past the sixtieth step the rock of the wall changed from grey to a deep rust-brown, and veins of paler stone ran through it that caught the lamp and let it go.

At one hundred and seventeen Kael said, "One hundred and eleventeen."

"One hundred and eighteen," said Sol.

There was a pause the length of a step. "I did that on purpose."

"You are a very bad liar."

A breath of air went out of him that might in another man have been a laugh. "The worst."

"The worst I have met."

He did not answer, but his back loosened, and the lamp-light swung a little as if he had shifted the weight of his whole body from one foot to the other. The stair turned, and turned, and a shoulder of rock pressed her arm against his. She left it there. He did not move away. For twenty steps they went down with the wool of his coat rough against her sleeve and the count steady in the dark, and it was the closest she had been to anyone for a long time, and the stone did not care.

One hundred and thirty. One hundred and thirty-one. One hundred and thirty-two.

Past one hundred and fifty she stopped counting aloud. She did not decide to. The numbers simply stopped coming out of her, and her mind went off down the stair ahead of her feet to the room at the bottom, and she walked on with her hand on the wall and her eyes unfocused. Kael went on. He kept the count going for both of them, quieter now, a thread of sound below the threshold of her attention, so that when she did come back to herself she was walking on the beat and had not missed a step.

She noticed it. She did not say thank you. She suspected he knew she had noticed, and that this was part of the arrangement.

At two hundred and eleven the stair ended.

The chamber opened out around them all at once, and the lamp, which had seemed so bright on the stair, shrank to a small yellow coin in a very large dark. The room was round. It was the size of a small church, and the roof went up into a vault she could not see the top of. The floor fell away from the foot of the stair into a wide shallow pit, and in the pit lay a slab of dark stone as long as a barge, laid flat, veined through with threads of old silver that took the lamplight and made a faint cold shimmer of it. Nothing moved on its surface. At the north end, in a shallow socket cut into the rock, stood the anchor plate.

She had heard it described, and Kael had drawn it for her once on the back of a tally sheet. It was black iron a hand's breadth thick and as wide as a door, and from its socket a scorch fanned out across the stone like the print of a hand that had been pressed down hard and held. The marks were old. The rock had been burned and had then healed over in a glaze, so that the fingers of the scorch gleamed a little, as if wet.

The upper stair had been shut by Thorne since the accident. Malrik had opened it, in the end, because he had no other way to say yes. She had not thought of that, climbing down. She thought of it now.

Kael lowered himself onto the last step. The stick came to rest across his knees. He set the lamp beside him on the stone and its light went up the curve of the wall and made long shadows of the two of them.

"I will sit here," he said. "The room is yours."

It was an ordinary thing to say. It was what a man says when a woman has said she would like to see a place on her own. There was no weight in it, no burn waiting, no count to be struck at the end. She noticed that too. It was the plainest courtesy he had offered her in nineteen days, and the fact that it was plain made it one she could take without having to hold it up and look at it.

"Thank you," she said.

She walked down into the room.

The floor of the pit was cold through her boots. She went round the edge of the slab, not across it, keeping the rim of the silver-veined stone between her and the dark water-stain at its centre, and came up on the north side where the plate stood. She stopped an arm's length from it. She did not touch it.

Up close it did not look like a tool of any kind. It looked like a door that had been shut by a hand that never meant to open it again. The iron was dull and very slightly pitted. A film of dust lay along the top edge of it. Along the lower edge, where the plate met the socket, there was a seam no wider than a thread, and the lamp-light did not go into it.

Her mother had sat here.

She had been told so, and she had read it, and she had not until this moment stood in the place and understood that it meant a woman in a chair, in a room this size, with her hands on the cold iron and the whole weight of another person's debt coming down through her palms. Her mother had walked out of this room on her own feet. That much had been said to Sol more than once, as if it were a comfort. She had walked out on her own feet and she had never fully walked back into her body. She had come home to the house on the western face and sat at the table with her hands round a cup that she could not feel, and she had died at forty-two, three winters after.

Sol had been twelve at the Reckoning. She remembered the cup.

"Sixteen and a half," she said to the plate.

The words went out into the chamber and came back to her, quiet, a little late, from the far wall. She had not planned to say them. They were simply what she had. She said them the way a person says her own name at a door.

"That is what I have. Sixteen and a half years. You have had a share of it, and the rest of it is mine, and I do not know yet what I will do with it." She stopped. The plate did not answer, and she had not expected it to. "My mother's carried year is on the leaf. I have not read it to anyone. I am reading it to you."

She knelt and set the bundle on the stone beside the pit and undid the twine.

The leaf came out first. It was stiff, the paper gone the colour of weak tea, the ink brown at the edges where the fibres had drunk it. She laid it flat, and held the corners down with the heel of her hand and the lamp. Rows of names and figures ran down it in a clerk's hand, and every row ended in a total, and every total, when she added the figures above it, stood exactly one year above the sum of the lines.

The difference was written in a second hand. It was darker and heavier than the first, and it sat under the word *carried* at the end of each row, one small figure at a time. The hand leaned to the right.

On her mother's row the difference was one year.

Sol read the figure three times. It did not become larger. It was only one year, written small, in ink that had gone brown. It was a figure that a clerk could have added to a ledger on a slow afternoon without looking up from a cup.

She took Renn's sheet from the bundle and unfolded it beside the leaf. Ulla had put it into her hands with both of her own, the way one hands over a thing that is still alive. Renn's small level script covered it, and his chalk lines, and in the east, near the edge, a mark he had made with a ring round it and the single word *warm*. The boiling-house cellar.

Last she reached behind the facing stone at the foot of the east wall, where Kael had left it, and drew out the diagram in its oilcloth. The oilcloth was supple. It had been kept dry. She unwrapped it and spread it on the cold floor beside the other two.

A thin dotted line ran from the south edge of the anchor on the diagram, east, toward the cliff face and the Lower Reach, and it ran off the paper at the edge. She laid Renn's sheet against its edge so the two papers met, and the dotted line went on across the join, a little crooked, because Renn's survey and the old diagram had not been drawn to the same scale. It went on east. It went on to the ring and the word *warm*, and stopped there.

She sat back on her heels.

The anchor and the second line were one system.

She had known it without knowing it. She had said the words to Kael more than once, in the tar shop, in the dark, with a lamp between them. But there is a difference between saying a thing and kneeling in a cold room with three pieces of paper and watching a single pencilled line walk from one to the other without a break. It did not feel like discovery. It felt like being told a thing she already knew by a stranger who had not known she knew it.

She tried to be angry.

It seemed to her that she should be. Her mother had put her name to a contract. Her mother had sat at this plate and had been bound, or had believed she was, and the years she had burned had gone down a road that ran from this room to a cellar in the east, and a wage had been paid for them. She knew what that wage had bought, and the rooms of the house on the western face that had been let go one at a time in the years afterward. There was a clean clear fire waiting for her at the back of all that, if she would only reach in and take it.

She searched for it. She searched the whole of the chamber for it, in the scorch that fanned like a hand and the dull iron and the dust on the lip of the plate. She went back, in her mind, to the cup on the table and to the fingers that had not closed round it.

She found no anger. She found a count.

Sixteen and a half. One year carried. Seventeen stored. The numbers came up in front of her one at a time and lay down in a row, and when she looked at the row it balanced and did not balance and balanced again, and she could not tell, kneeling there with the lamp-light on the paper, which of those was the true state of it.

The stored years were not traceable. She had been told that. Renn had been told it by the gauge, and Kael by the plate, and she had read it in the old words herself. The seventeen was a sum with no names in it. A year carried off her mother's row did not go into a cell with her mother's name on it. It went into the stored sum, among all the other years, and was lost.

She could not know that the year was in the seventeen. She could know only what the leaf told her, that a year had been carried off her mother's row, and what Renn's gauge told her, that seventeen years were stored in a cellar at the end of a dotted line. Between those two facts there was no bridge. There was only a gap, and the gap was the size of a person's choice.

She decided to count it.

It was not a finding. She set it down in herself carefully, as a thing she was choosing and not a thing she had proved, the way you lay a stone in a wall knowing it will hold weight only because you have decided to build on it. A year was in the seventeen, and it was her mother's, and it was hers to carry. The text, if anyone ever wrote it down, would call it a decision. She would not argue.

The decision settled in her chest, and it was heavier than the bundle had been.

Behind her, on the last step, the stick tapped once against stone. The lamp flame wavered and steadied.

"You are quiet," Kael said. He had kept his voice low, and the chamber made it small.

"I am thinking."

"What are you thinking?"

She did not turn. "Whether the road from this plate to the boiling house runs both ways."

There was a silence on the step. She could hear him turn it over.

"The stored years run one way," he said at last. "From the plate to the east. Whatever is carried goes down the line and stays. I do not know that anything comes back."

"That is not what I asked."

"No," said Kael. "I do not think it is."

He did not say anything else, and she was grateful for it. She folded the leaf along its old creases, the way Renn had taught her, the edges meeting the folds without forcing. It went into the oilcloth. Renn's sheet followed. The diagram went last, with the dotted line facing outward, east, toward the cliff and the Lower Reach and the cellar marked warm.

"If any hand closes the east line," she said, to the plate, to the room, "they close the far end of my mother's road."

Behind her the step creaked as Kael shifted his weight. He did not tell her she was reading too much into it. He did not tell her it was not a road. He said nothing, and the nothing was exact, and she knew he had understood.

She tied the twine. The knot seated clean.

"Ready," she said.

Kael rose from the step. The stick came up with him, fluid, as if it were a thing he had decided to use and not a thing that held him. He took the lamp off the stone. The flame steadied. For a moment the two of them stood at the foot of the stair with the whole round dark of the chamber behind them and the plate at its north end, black on black.

"Is it enough?" he said.

"It is what there is."

They climbed.

The count began again at the first turn, Kael's voice low and steady in the cold. "One. Two. Three. Four." She joined at forty, and neither of them remarked on it. Going up was harder. The stair was steeper than it had seemed coming down, and by the hundredth step her breath was short and her legs had begun to remember every flight in the tar shop and every night on the Lower Spine. Where the stair narrowed her hand found his back through the coat, and stayed, a flat warm patch under her fingers that rose and fell with his breathing. He did not comment. He did not slow down, either, and she was grateful for that more than for any comfort he might have offered.

She did not speak. The thing she had decided lay in her like a swallowed coal, and she carried it up the stair step by counted step and did not set it down. Seventeen. A share of the seventeen. If she said it here, in the dark, with the water dripping and his voice going on in front of her, he would have to hold it on the stair with her, and there was nowhere to put it. She would say it at the top, in the open, where there was air.

At one hundred and ninety she could see the grey of the sky.

At two hundred and eleven she came out into the wind.

The steward was where they had left him, with the visitors' book shut against his chest and his hands folded on it. The sky was a flat bright grey. Below the landing the yard of the anchor complex spread out in its work, the pitch vats and the coiled lines and a cart standing with its shafts on the ground, and beyond the wall the long slope of the Reach fell away east toward the cliff.

Sol stopped on the landing. Kael stopped beside her. The wind got into her coat and her hair and she let it.

She looked east, where the slope went down and a roof showed here and there among the folds of rock, and where, far off, a thread of pale smoke went up from low ground that she knew was the boiling house. She looked at it for as long as it took to draw three breaths.

"A share of the seventeen may be my mother's," she said.

It was one sentence. The wind took it, and part of it went over the wall, and part of it must have reached him, because he did not move.

He did not flinch. The wind tore at the grey wool of his coat, and his face stayed turned toward her, the pale eyes steady. He did not ask what she meant. He did not tell her that the years could not be traced, though he knew it as well as she did, and he did not tell her that the gauge counted bodies and not rows on a leaf.

"We knew the road ran both ways," he said.

It was quiet and flat, and it was almost lost in the wind. But it landed between them, heavier than the bundle in her arms, and she found that she could put the coal down at last. He had picked it up. He had not asked whose hand it was in.

She nodded once. "We knew."

The steward had not moved from where he stood against the wall. He regarded them with the patience of the stone he was leaning on.

"Will you want the book signed again, going out?" he said. It was not quite a question. His voice was flat and unused to curiosity.

"No," said Sol. "We are done here."

He inclined his head by the smallest fraction. Then he turned and drew the heavy door of the stair house shut behind him, and the iron groaned on its hinges, and the latch fell with a sound that went on echoing in the stair after it was gone.

They stood alone on the landing. The wind pushed her hair across her face, and she did not brush it away. After a moment Kael shifted his weight, and the stick touched the stone.

"The count was good," he said. "On the way up."

"It was yours."

"It was ours. You came in at forty."

"I always come in at forty."

"I know," he said. "I was counting on it."

He had not meant it as a joke. She heard that, and she heard the small warmth under it, and she laughed, short and surprised, with the wind taking the sound away over the wall. His face changed. It was only a small movement at the corner of his mouth. But it was a movement she had not seen on him before this month, and she kept it, the way she had kept the count.

They went down the broad stair that led from the landing to the yard. The stone was worn smooth in the middle of each step by years of boots. The air warmed as they went, and the smell of tar and iron came up to meet them in place of the cold damp of the deep stair. They did not count aloud. The count had done its work. But their feet fell in time without either of them choosing it, heel and toe and heel, the same pace, the same stride, and at the turn where the stair narrowed his shoulder brushed hers, and neither of them stepped away.

At the foot of the stair Sol stopped and turned her face up into the grey light.

Behind her the anchor plate lay in its socket, two hundred and eleven steps down, black on black. In front of her the Reach went down to the cliff in its ranks of roof and smoke. Between the two, over the shoulder of rock, a thin straight line ran east that no one but she and Kael could see. It began at a door, and it ended at a cellar. Along it, in the dark, a year that had been her mother's was waiting to be counted, and she had counted it.

She hitched the bundle higher on her hip and went on down into the yard with Kael beside her, and the wind at her back.
