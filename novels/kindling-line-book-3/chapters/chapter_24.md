The lamp had burned itself low by the time they woke. Morning light slipped through the cracked window, pale and thin, and found the cups where they had been left on the table. The broth pot was cold. Ansa's bread board held only crumbs.

Kael sat on the floor with his back against the wall, one leg stretched out, the other bent. The stick lay in the corner where it had fallen. His coat hung open and his collar was askew. Sol sat in the chair across from him with her undyed coat pulled tight at the throat, and she held a cup in both hands. Steam rose between them.

"You hedged in front of the whole Reach," she said.

The words carried no edge. Her mouth curved at one corner.

Kael lifted his own cup. The tea was bitter, steeped too long, and he drank it anyway.

"I do not promise what I cannot measure," he said. "About thirty-four."

She laughed. The sound was quiet and surprised, as if she had forgotten she owned it.

"About," she repeated. "Is that a legal term?"

"It is an actuarial one."

"Actuarial."

"The Hall uses it. You live long enough to see a pattern, you stop calling it exact."

She set her cup down, and the tea shivered in it. "You can keep it. The word. The number. The hedge."

"I did not offer it for keeping."

"No. You offered it because you meant it."

The silence that followed weighed nothing. It was the silence of two people who had said the hardest thing aloud to a crowd the evening before and found the morning still arriving. Kael watched her hand rest on the table, the nails trimmed short, the knuckles pale. He thought of the count she had given the Lower Reach, sixteen and a half, and the count he had given back, about thirty-four. The gap between them was seventeen and a half. The gauge in the boiling-house yard read seventeen, and it had not moved in days.

He set the thought down. The morning was not for arithmetic.

Last night she had laughed until she shook, and then shaken without the laughing, and she had let him see that she was afraid of being seen. This morning her hands were steady on the cup. He understood that the steadiness was a thing she had decided on, and that she had decided to let him watch both. He did not say so. He drank the bitter tea instead, and she saw him do it, and her mouth moved again.

She gathered the cups as she stood and set them in a row on the bread board, and swept the crumbs into a heap with the side of her hand. "Ansa will have opinions about this room," she said. "She gave forty households one word last night, and I think she means to spend the rest of the week on us."

"It was the right word."

"It was," said Sol, and ate the heap of crumbs off her palm, and did not apologize for it.

His left leg prickled. Pins where there had been numbness: the change had come on slowly, night by night, since the long stair at the anchor three days before. On level ground the leg held. On stairs it still failed him, and the stick stayed in his hand for those.

"Your wrist," Sol said.

He glanced down. His sleeve had ridden up, and the skin underneath showed pale, the veins a faint blue.

"May I?"

He held out his hand, palm up. She rose and came around the table and knelt beside him, her knees against his. Her palm settled on the bare skin of his wrist, fingers spread light. She did not speak. Her eyes dropped to the pulse point, and he felt the contact travel up his arm, warm and deliberate.

"Steady," she said, after a long moment. "Even."

"Because I am not looking at you."

Her head came up. Her eyes met his.

"You are looking at me now."

"Ah."

The syllable carried more than the word should hold. Her thumb moved in a slow stroke along the tendon, and the cold tea and the crumbs and the cracked window dropped out of the room. He wanted this to go on. He wanted the light to change its angle on the boards and the cups to go cold all the way through, and the only decision of the day to be whether to kiss her again or simply breathe in time with her.

"Odo sent word," Sol said. She did not move her hand. "He wants us at the gauge hut this morning. He says he will lay it out once, to both of us, and not twice."

"That sounds like him."

"It does." Her thumb stopped. "Up, then. Slowly. I would like to watch you do it."

He got the good leg under him, and then the other. It took his weight. The pins sharpened into a bright thread from hip to knee, and he stood and breathed and did not fall. It was a small thing, standing up. He had crossed the whole of the Reckoning Hall under a hundred eyes and thought nothing of it, and this cost him more. Sol stayed on her knees a moment longer than she needed to, watching his face instead of the leg. Her own face told him nothing, which he had learned was how she showed relief.

"You will want that for the stair," she said, and nodded at the corner.

"I will want that for the stair," he agreed, and fetched it.

They went down together, Sol a half step ahead. Pitch came up through the boards, thick and sweet, and under it the cooler smell of hemp from the shop below. "Mind the sixth tread," she said, and he said he had been minding it since Ansa warned Aldous, and she looked back at him with her eyebrows raised as if he had made a joke. He supposed he had. The sixth tread gave a little under his boot and held.

At the foot of the stair the door opened onto the lane. His fingers would not manage the top button of his coat, and he leaned the stick against the doorframe to use both hands. Sol watched him do it. She looked at the stick, then at him, then at the lane, and she said nothing at all, and let him go out ahead of her.

The lane to the boiling-house yard was level, worn smooth by generations of carts and feet. The carters were already at their work, and the vats were steaming, and the air was sharp with tar and river water and the green scent of pine off the cliffs. Kael walked it the way he had walked it as a boy, without thinking, which was the whole of the trouble. Each stride sent the pins flaring and then settling. The leg held. His breath had gone to the count before he told it to: in four, hold six, out eight.

The yard was waking around them, and it looked at them. A carter at a vat lifted two fingers from the handle of his barrow. A woman hauling a sack to the shop door nodded as she passed, not quickly and not unkindly. Last night they had told the Lower Reach what they were to one another, and the Reach had answered with a loaf. This morning it answered with nods.

"Nod back," Sol murmured.

He nodded back. His face did not know what to do and did it badly. "That is new," he said.

"It will be less new tomorrow." She kept her eyes on the lane. "And anyone who asks the Reach about us will get a very friendly answer."

He had not thought of that. He thought of it now, and walked on.

Quick feet came up behind him on the packed earth. Tam jogged out of the shop's side door with the stick held in front of him like a message he had been told to deliver, and drew level, and held it out. He looked at Kael, and then at Sol. His face gave away nothing. He did not say a word. He put the stick into Kael's hand, turned, and jogged back the way he had come.

Kael stood with the wood in his fist. He had walked the length of the lane, and the stick had not crossed his mind.

"That is how far," Sol said. Her voice was mild.

"I am not pleased about it."

"You are frightened."

"Yes."

"Then you will be careful with it," she said, and took his free hand for three steps, and let it go.

"Ansa raised that boy to say nothing at the right moments," she added, after a while. "He is very good at it."

"He is better at it than I am."

"You are improving."

He was not triumphant. He was afraid to trust the leg, the way a man is afraid of a column that has finally balanced after a night of not balancing, and goes on looking for the error that must still be in it. He carried the stick the rest of the way instead of leaning on it, and neither of them remarked on that either.

The boiling-house stood on the far side of the yard, and the iron door to its cellar was shut, the metal gone dark with damp. He had gone down behind that door alone, once, and had come up with his left leg no longer his own, and the leg had not been entirely his since. He did not look at the door for long. Sol, beside him, looked at it exactly as long as he did, and then at him, and neither of them said that either.

The yard opened ahead of them, a wide square of beaten earth with the vats steaming along its north side and the boiling-house at the far end. The gauge hut stood apart from both, at the east edge, as though it did not care to be mistaken for either: a low block of grey stone and tarred timber with a hatch set in its side at the height of a man's chest. Odo Marl was already there, broad and unhurried, his big hands flat on the rim. Tessa Rook leaned against the wall beside him with a chalk line coiled at her belt and the look of a young woman who had slept little and meant to be cheerful about it. The door of the hut stood open behind them. Inside he could see a stool worn hollow by two people taking turns on it, a shelf with a lamp and a stub of pencil on a string, and a rag hung from a nail for wiping the glass of the dial.

Odo nodded to them. He lifted the hatch, read the dial, and closed it. Then he lifted it again, read it again, and closed it again. Two readings, every time. Kael had watched him do it across a gauge-book and a boiling-house yard and had never once seen the second disagree with the first.

"Seventeen," Odo said. "Unchanged."

"I took the reading after you spoke last night," he added. "Seventeen then as well."

"I did not think it would care," said Sol.

"It does not," said Odo. "But a man likes to be sure."

Tessa's eyes went to the stick Kael carried and did not lean on, and then to his face, and she was kind enough to say nothing about it.

Tessa uncoiled her chalk line and snapped it taut against her palm. A little white dust rose and settled on her sleeve. She did it when she wanted to keep her hands busy, Kael thought, and she wanted that now.

"The plan," Odo said.

He spoke as if he were reading out a survey: no inflection, no hurry, each item set down beside the last. "Two pegs. One breath. Cords tied to the pegs and run out through the door. Two pullers at the far end of the passage, beyond the room's reach. They pull together, on the count, in the trough of the cycle."

"The trough," Sol said.

"The needle drops at the bottom of the cycle and holds there for the length of one breath," Tessa said. "Between the twelfth and the first. That is where we pull."

"Sol sits at the plate," Odo went on. "The pullers are Tessa and myself."

Kael said nothing. His hand closed on the head of the stick, and he made it open again. Under his sleeve, where her thumb had rested that morning, his pulse went hard and then, because he made it, level.

"Thank you for saying it plainly," Sol said.

"There is no good way to say it," said Odo. "I picked the short one."

Tessa crouched by the wall of the hut and chalked a row of marks on the stone beside the hatch: eleven short ticks in a line, and a twelfth set a little apart from the rest, and after it a long blank gap. "The twelfth is the second line's," she said. "The gap after it is the trough. That is the only part of the cycle where a hand on a cord would do anything." Kael noted that she had set the twelfth apart without being asked, the way a clerk marks a late entry so that no one mistakes it for part of the original count.

Tessa reached inside the door of the hut and brought out the gauge-book, and held it open to him. He took it and read it the way he had read ledgers for ten years, with his eyes first and his judgment after. The night readings ran down the page in two hands, Odo's blocky capitals and Tessa's quick slant, every entry made twice. Seventeen. Seventeen. Seventeen. He had spent a decade distrusting columns that agreed with themselves. This one agreed with itself in two hands, and he believed it.

"It does not fall at night," Tessa said. "We thought it might, when the town goes quiet."

"It does not rise either," said Odo.

"It does not move," Kael said, and handed the book back.

"Who sits the night readings?" Sol asked.

"We trade the halves," said Tessa. "Odo takes the first, I take the second."

"And when you are tired?"

"Then he reads it twice and I read it three times." Her smile came and went. "It has not made a difference yet."

Kael had a second question ready, and behind it a third, the way he had always had them, lined up to draw the whole shape of a thing out of the person in front of him before the tea was cold. He felt them standing there. He let them stand. Odo had said it once, to both of them, and Kael found he could hear it better without cross-examining it.

Sol had come to stand at his shoulder. He felt her there before he turned his head, a warmth along his arm that did not lean and did not leave. She had not looked at him once since Odo spoke her name for the plate. She was looking at the hatch, and the stone, and the line of chalk dust on Tessa's thumb.

"The cycle is eleven breaths," Odo said, "with a twelfth added by the second line. When the second line closes, the stored sum goes to the nearest living body."

He did not say who that would be. Tessa did not say it. Sol did not say it. The hatch ticked as the stone began to warm in the sun. Across the yard a cart wheel ground over a rut and went on, and a gull wheeled low over the river and was gone.

Kael heard himself think that pulling a cord would be a way to stand in the fight without standing next to her. The thought came whole, with its reasons already attached, and he looked at it as he would have looked at a figure that had turned up in the wrong column. He did not say it. He did not know yet whether he was allowed to want it.

He looked at the chalk on the wall instead. Eleven ticks, the twelfth standing apart, and the long blank after it. It was only a gap in a row of marks. To a man who had spent ten years reading the blanks in columns for what they were hiding, it looked exactly like a place where a person could stand.

"It looks smaller on a wall," Sol said quietly, so that only he would hear.

"Most things do," he said. "That is why we write them down."

"Is that why you write everything down?"

"It is why I used to." He considered it. "I think I am trying to stop."

She did not answer, but her shoulder pressed harder against his arm, and stayed there.

Tessa wound the chalk line back onto its spool, slowly, and clipped it to her belt. She looked at Sol, and then at Kael, and her quick face went quiet.

"Whoever stands at the plate is nearest," she said, softly. "That is not a choice. That is where the line is."

Nobody answered her. Sol's shoulder stayed where it was against his arm. Odo's broad hands rested on the hatch, the knuckles pale where he gripped the iron rim, and after a moment he lifted it for a third reading, which he never did. The needle sat where it had sat all night.

"Seventeen," he said again, to nobody, and closed it.
