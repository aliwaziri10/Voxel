The lamp had burned low by the time they woke. Morning light slipped through the cracked window, pale and thin, and found the tea cups where they had been left on the table. The broth pot was cold. Ansa's bread board held only crumbs.

Kael sat with his back against the wall, one leg stretched out, the other bent. The stick leaned in the corner where it had fallen. His coat lay open. His shirt collar was askew. Sol sat opposite him, her undyed coat pulled tight at the throat, the copper clasp catching the light. She cradled a cup in both hands. Steam rose between them.

"You hedged in front of the whole Reach," she said.

The words carried no edge. Her mouth curved at the corner.

Kael lifted his own cup. The tea was bitter, steeped too long. He swallowed.

"I do not promise what I cannot measure," he said. "About thirty-four."

She laughed. The sound was quiet, surprised, as if she had forgotten she could make it.

"About," she repeated. "Is that a legal term?"

"It is an actuarial one."

"Actuarial."

"Wards use it. The paid trade. You live long enough to see a pattern, you stop calling it exact."

She set her cup down. The liquid trembled. "You can keep it. The word. The number. The hedge."

"I did not offer it for keeping."

"No. You offered it because you meant it."

The silence between them held no weight. It was the silence of two people who had spoken the hardest truths the night before and found the world still turning. Kael watched her fingers rest on the table, the nails trimmed short, the knuckles pale. He thought of the count she had spoken to the yard. Sixteen and a half. He thought of the count he had spoken back. About thirty-four. The gap between them seventeen and a half, or seventeen, depending on which figure you trusted. The gauge in the hut below read seventeen. It had not moved in days.

He pushed the thought aside. The morning was not for arithmetic.

His left leg tingled. Pins, not numbness. The change had come gradual, night by night, since the stair descent three days past. On level ground the leg held. On stairs it still betrayed him. The stick remained necessary there. He had not climbed a flight without it since the public step.

"Your wrist," Sol said.

He glanced down. His sleeve had pulled back. The skin showed pale, the veins faint blue lines.

"May I?"

He extended his hand, palm up. She rose from her chair and came around the table. Her knees brushed his as she knelt. Her palm settled against the bare skin of his wrist, fingers spreading light. She did not speak. Her eyes dropped to the pulse point. He felt the contact travel up his arm, warm and deliberate.

"Steady," she said after a long moment. "Even."

"Because I am not looking at you."

Her head lifted. Her eyes met his.

"You are looking at me now."

"Ah."

The syllable carried more than the word should hold. Her thumb moved, a slow stroke along the tendon. The room contracted to the space between them. The cracked window, the cold tea, the bread crumbs ceased to exist.

He wanted to stay in this moment. He wanted the morning to stretch until the light changed angle and the tea grew cold and the only decision required was whether to kiss her again or simply breathe in rhythm.

A step on the ladder. The trapdoor lifted.

Tam appeared in the square of light, the stick in his hand. He looked from Kael to Sol, his face blank. He held the stick out.

"You left this," he said.

Kael stared at the wood. He had walked to the ladder, descended two rungs, turned toward the yard gate. The leg had moved. The stick had not crossed his mind.

"That is how far," Sol said. Her voice was mild. Her hand remained on Kael's wrist.

Tam did not smile. He did not frown. He set the stick against the wall beside the ladder and climbed back down.

Kael exhaled. Sol's fingers tightened, fractionally.

"Tea," she said. "We were discussing tea. The steeping time. The bitterness."

"Actuarial terms," Kael agreed. "The bitterness is a known variable."

She released his wrist and returned to her chair. She lifted her cup. He lifted his. They drank in silence, the pretence fragile and perfect.

When the cups were empty, Kael rose. The leg took his weight. The pins sharpened, a bright needle thread running from hip to knee. He did not reach for the stick.

"Walk with me," Sol said.

They climbed the ladder together. The morning air hit them, sharp with tar and river water and the green scent of pine from the cliffs. The yard was waking. Smoke curled from Ansa's chimney. Renn sat by the fire with Ulla, his splinted wrist propped on a folded blanket. Corren and Joren stood at the gate, the older warden's scar catching the light. Odo Marl and Tessa Rook waited by the gauge hut, chalk lines coiled at their belts. Bram was nowhere in sight.

Kael set off across the packed earth. Sol fell into step beside him. The lane to the boiling-house yard was level, worn smooth by generations of carts and feet. He walked without the stick. Each stride sent the pins flaring, then settling. The leg held. It held.

He was not triumphant. He was frightened to trust it.

The gauge hut stood at the east edge of the yard, a low structure of grey stone and tarred timber. Odo lifted the hatch as they approached. He peered inside, read the dial, closed the hatch. He did not speak until they stopped before him.

"Seventeen," he said. "Unchanged."

Tessa uncoiled a chalk line from her belt. She snapped it taut, the dust rising white.

"The plan," Odo said. "Two pegs. One breath. Cords tied to the pegs and run out through the door. Two pullers at the far end of the passage beyond the room's reach. Pulling together on the count in the trough of the cycle."

He spoke the words as if reciting a survey. No inflection. No urgency.

"Sol sits at the plate," he continued. "The pullers are Tessa and myself."

Kael said nothing. His jaw tightened.

Tessa lifted the gauge hatch again. She peered in, read, closed it.

"Still seventeen," she said. "Does not fall at night."

"The cycle is eleven with a twelfth added," Odo said. "The second line adds the twelfth. When the pegs pull in one breath, the stored sum goes to the nearest living body."

He did not say who that would be. Tessa did not say it. Sol did not say it.

Kael heard himself think that pulling would be a way to be near the fight without being near her. He did not say it.

The pins in his leg faded to a dull hum. The morning light strengthened. Somewhere a bird called, a harsh cry over the river.

Tessa coiled her chalk line. She looked at Sol. She looked at Kael.

"Whoever stands at the plate is nearest," she said, softly. "That is not a choice. That is where the line is."

The words hung in the air between them, heavier than the chalk dust settling on Tessa's fingers. Odo said nothing. His broad hands rested on the hatch, the knuckles pale where he gripped the iron rim. The gauge hut breathed its slow rhythm, eleven heartbeats and a twelfth, the second line adding its count to the cycle.

Kael felt the statement land in his chest. Not a choice. That was the rule, stripped of every gentleness. The nearest living body paid. The nearest living body received. The line did not negotiate.

Sol did not move. Her face was calm, the copper clasp at her throat catching the morning light. But he saw the pulse in her neck jump, once, hard. She had known. She had always known. The public step, the count spoken aloud, the seventeen years waiting in the dark — every step had led here, to this hut and this moment and the plate where she would sit.

Tessa coiled the chalk line slow, deliberate. The white dust drifted. She clipped the line to her belt. Her eyes moved from Sol to Kael and back, assessing, measuring.

"The cords," Odo said. "We rig them today. Test the pull. The pegs do not move until the day."

"Today," Tessa repeated. "The trough of the cycle. We need the count."

Kael's throat moved. "The count."

"Eleven breaths. The twelfth on the second line. You pull in the trough between twelve and one. One breath. Both cords. Together."

"One breath," Kael echoed. "Two pullers. One breath."

"Together," Odo said. "If one pulls early, the peg holds. If one pulls late, the peg holds. The mechanism requires simultaneity. That is the design."

Kael nodded. His left leg ached, a deep dull throb in the hip where the numbness had lived. The pins had faded. The leg held. He did not reach for the stick.

"Where are the cords?" Sol asked. Her voice was steady. Her hands, folded at her waist, showed no tremor.

"Coiled in the passage," Tessa said. "Two hundred feet each. Hemp, tarred, tested to three hundred weight. We ran them yesterday while you were at the anchor plate."

Kael looked at her. "You ran them."

"Odo measured. I hauled." She shrugged, a quick economical motion. "The passage is clear. The pegs are accessible. The door swings out. We pull from the far end, beyond the room's reach. The cords feed through the hinges."

"Through the hinges," Kael repeated. "The door stays closed."

"The door stays closed," Odo confirmed. "The plate room seals. The pullers remain outside. The wielder remains inside. That is the rule."

Sol's fingers tightened on her coat. "The wielder."

"Sol," Kael said.

She did not look at him. Her eyes stayed on Odo, on Tessa, on the gauge hut's closed hatch. "The wielder sits at the plate. The nearest living body. That is not a choice. That is where the line is."

She had repeated Tessa's words. Not a question. A statement of fact, laid bare in the morning light.

Tessa's mouth twisted, a flicker of something that might have been respect. "Yes."

"The stored sum goes to the nearest living body," Sol continued. "Seventeen years. The gauge reads seventeen. It has not moved. When the pegs pull, those seventeen years find the nearest living body. Me."

"Sol," Kael said again. Lower. Urgent.

She turned then. Her face was pale, the freckles standing stark across her nose. Her eyes found his, held.

"I am not afraid," she said. "I am not. But I need you to hear me say it. I need you to hear me say that I know. That I have always known. The public step was not for them. It was for you. So you could not pretend there was another way."

The yard noises faded. The smoke from Ansa's chimney. The bird's harsh cry. The river's distant rush. The world contracted to the space between them, the gauge hut at their backs, the plan laid out in cold terms by two techs who had seen too many lines close.

Kael's hand rose. He did not touch her. He let it hang in the air between them, the square palm open, the fingers slightly curved.

"I am not pretending," he said. "I have never pretended. I have been counting the days since the stair. Since the rope. Since the first time you let me count for you. Every day. Every number. I know what sits at the plate. I know what waits in the dark. I know what the rule says."

"Then say it."

The demand was quiet. Not a plea. A requirement.

Kael breathed. The pins in his leg sharpened, a sudden bright flare. He ignored it.

"I know that when the pegs pull, the stored years go to the nearest living body. I know that you will sit at the plate. I know that you will be nearest. I know that seventeen years will find you. I know that you will carry thirty-three. I know that I will carry about thirty-three. I know that the gap closes. I know that we grow old together."

He spoke the words one by one, each landing like a stone in still water. The count. The stored sum. The mechanics. The ending.

"I know that I will not pull the cords. I know that Odo and Tessa will pull. I know that I will stand at the passage end, beyond the room's reach, and I will wait. I know that I will not be near you when it happens. I know that the rule puts me where it puts me, and the rule is not kind, and the rule does not bend."

His voice cracked on the last word. He did not stop.

"But I also know that I love you. I know that I have loved you since the stair. Since before the stair. Since the first time I counted aloud and realised I was counting for you. I know that I am not going anywhere. I know that I am here. In the room. At the rope's end. Yours."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full of everything they had not said, every night in the room over the tar shop, every descent and ascent, every touch and every word spoken and unspoken.

Sol's hand moved. Slow. Deliberate. She covered his hanging palm with her own, fingers interlacing, grip tight. The copper clasp at her throat caught the light. The gauge hut breathed its twelve beats.

"Good," she said. "That is good. Now tell me about the cords."

Kael let out a breath that shook. A laugh, broken and real, escaped him. "The cords. Hemp. Tarred. Two hundred feet. Tested to three hundred weight."

"Run through the hinges."

"Run through the hinges. The door stays closed. The pullers stand beyond the room's reach. One breath. Together. In the trough between twelve and one."

"Say the trough."

"The trough between twelve and one. The cycle's lowest point. The line's quietest moment."

"Say it again."

"The trough between twelve and one. One breath. Together."

She nodded. Her eyes were bright, wet at the edges. She did not wipe them. She did not look away.

"Then we rig today," she said. "We test the pull. We make sure the cords do not snag. We make sure the pegs turn clean. We make sure Odo and Tessa can pull in one breath, together, without hesitation."

"Today," Kael agreed. "We rig today."

Odo cleared his throat. The sound was loud in the quiet. "The passage is this way. The pegs are at the ledge and the table. The cords are coiled. We begin now."

He turned and walked toward the boiling-house cellar, his broad shoulders blocking the light. Tessa followed, her chalk line clicking at her belt. She glanced back once, her gaze finding Kael, then Sol. Something in her expression softened, the briefest shift.

"Good luck," she said. Quiet. For them alone.

Then she was gone, descending into the cellar's mouth.

Kael and Sol stood alone in the yard. The morning light strengthened, burning off the river mist. The pins in his leg had settled to a dull hum. The stick leaned against the gauge hut, forgotten.

He looked at her. She looked at him. The copper clasp threw a warm glint onto her cheek. The gauge hut's hatch stayed closed. The line breathed.

"Today," he said.

"Today," she agreed.

They walked toward the cellar together, shoulders brushing, step for step. The leg held. The count held. The plan held. And in the dark below, the twin brass pegs waited in their sockets, patient and cold, for the breath that would close the line and send seventeen years to the nearest living body — to her, to Sol, to Isolde Vane, who had sixteen and a half years and a love that had no number and a man who would wait at the rope's end until the world ended or the line closed, whichever came first.

The cellar mouth swallowed them. The light faded. The work began.
