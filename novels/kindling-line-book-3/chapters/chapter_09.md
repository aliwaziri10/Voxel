Night had settled over the Lower Reach. The lamp on the sill was out. Below them the terrace path curled away into darkness, the ward-stones invisible, the tannery vats silent. One blue point burned at the metering chamber window, steady as a held breath.

Sol sat with her back against the window frame, legs drawn up, the wool of her undyed coat pulled tight at the collar. Kael sat beside her, his stick laid across his knees, the violet cuffs of his coat turned back. The night air carried mineral water and cold iron from the boiling house vents. Somewhere a gull cried, carried up from the water.

"He would give them to you."

Sol turned her head. Kael's profile was sharp against the dark, the line of his jaw, the bridge of his nose. His eyes were fixed on the blue point.

"Who?"

"You. Me. The years." His voice was low, stripped of the ledger cadence he used for tallies. "If it worked that way. I would give them to you."

The words landed between them, plain and heavy.

"It does not work that way."

"I know."

"Transfer is involuntary. The nearest living body pays. Nothing can be chosen. No ward-binding. No chosen buffer. No oath that moves years." She spoke each clause as if reading from a ledger. "You know the rule. You spoke it on the terrace this morning."

"I spoke it."

"Then why say it?"

Kael was silent. The blue point did not waver. Wind moved through the shutters, lifting the hair at his temple.

"Because I want you to know what I would do."

Sol's fingers curled into the wool at her knee. "I know what you would do. That is the problem."

A breath. Two. The gull cried again, farther now.

"You make it sound like a flaw."

"It is a flaw. In the arithmetic. In the," She stopped. Her mouth twisted. "Do you intend to put it in a contract? A clause with a signature line?"

Kael's mouth twitched. "I have already drafted the clause."

"Let me guess. The party of the first part hereby conveys,"

"All years remaining, current and future, to the party of the second part, in perpetuity, subject to the Kindling's consent."

"The Kindling does not consent. The Kindling burns. The nearest body pays. Your clause is rubbish."

"Rubbish with a signature line."

"Rubbish."

His hand lay on his thigh, palm up, the square palm scarred at the back, the fingers relaxed. Sol reached across the space between them. Her fingers closed on his wrist, turned his hand over. The skin was warm. The lines of his palm were deep, etched by rope and rope-rig and the stick he carried.

She traced the life line with her thumb, slow, deliberate, the way she had once checked a climbing rope for wear, fingertip pressure, searching for the fray that meant failure.

"I do not want your years."

Kael's breath caught. His hand did not pull away.

"I want you to have them."

The words hung in the dark. The blue point burned. The wind tasted of iron and salt.

He turned his hand under hers, catching her fingers, lacing them through his own. The grip was firm. His thumb brushed the heel of her palm.

"Say it again."

"No."

"Say it as a wish. Not an offer. A wish."

She was silent. The shutters rattled. The blue point held.

"I wish you to have them."

His hand tightened on hers. He did not speak. She did not speak. They sat on the sill above the dark Reach, hands clasped, the night moving around them, and the wish sat between them like a stone laid on a ledger, acknowledged, recorded, not undone.

The morning after, Odo converted the east gauge by Renn's survey table.

Sol stood at the metering chamber window, the blue lamp at her back. The gauge glass was thick, the fluid dark, the needle motionless. Odo bent over his slate, pencil moving, then turned the table and checked it again.

"Seventeen," he said. His voice was steady. The limp was gone from his step, the grimace gone from his face. He marked the slate, pencil precise. "Read twice and confirmed. The stored sum is seventeen years."

Tessa capped her chalk. "The gauge does not go down at night. It has held through every reading we have taken."

Sol's gaze dropped to the Instrument of Dissolution on the table beside the gauge. Council custody. Stored charge under Council seal. The parchment was yellow at the edges, the ink brown. She had seen it before, the night the petition was entered, the Chancellor's thin voice reading the clauses.

Whoever the Council sent to stand at the line would be the nearest.

The thought settled in her chest, cold and familiar. She wanted those years. She hated that she wanted them.

Mara's cold climbed with the line's draw. Closing the line would stop it. The stored sum would go to the nearest living body. The same rule as cost, in the other direction.

"Seventeen," she said again. The word tasted of iron.

Odo gathered his slate. Tessa folded her chalk line, belt clicking. "Ansa wants us at the tar shop. She says a stranger has been measuring the east approach with a chain."

Sol's hand tightened on Kael's. The blue point at the metering chamber window did not waver.

"A stranger," she said. "Measuring the east approach with a chain."

"Ansa's words," Tessa said. "Not mine. She sent Tam to find us. He is waiting at the tar shop."

Odo's pencil paused on the slate. "The east approach. The second line runs under the boiling house cellar. The chain would trace the surface path above it."

"Or the surface path to the Lower Reach," Kael said. His voice was flat. "Either way, someone is scouting the ground."

"Or the Council has sent a surveyor of its own," Tessa said. She did not look at them. Her eyes tracked the blue point. "Measuring. Waiting."

The wind gusted through the shutters. The lamp flame at the metering chamber window burned steady, fed by a line that did not go down at night.

Sol released Kael's hand. She stood, the wool of her coat settling around her. The cold iron of the window frame bit into her palm where she braced herself.

"Then we go to the tar shop," she said. "We hear what Ansa heard. We see what the chain measured."

Kael rose beside her. His stick came up smooth. His left leg took the weight without a hitch. The violet cuffs caught the blue light. "Joren and Corren," he said. "If the stranger is Council, we do not go alone."

Tessa's chalk line clicked at her belt. "I will send word. They are at the terrace path, stone five. Corren's wrist held. He will move fast."

"Send word," Kael said. "We move now."

They left the metering chamber. The stair down to the terrace path was steep, the iron rungs cold under their hands. Sol went first. Kael followed, stick tucked under his arm, breathing even. The blue point shrank behind them, a single eye watching the gauge that read seventeen and did not fall.

The terrace path wound through the morning grey. Ward-stones stood like dead sentinels, their gauge glass dark, their copper wire green with verdigris. The tannery vats below were black shapes steaming in the cold. The boiling house vents exhaled mineral heat, a grey plume that smelled of iron and old water.

Tam waited at the tar shop door. Ansa's nephew. The boy who had signed on the tenth and withdrawn on the fifteenth. His face was pale in the lantern light hung over the threshold. The flame threw his shadow long across the cobbles.

"Ansa's inside," he said. His voice was low. "She has the chain. The stranger left it. Coiled on the workbench like a dead snake."

"Where is the stranger?" Kael asked.

"Gone. Before I arrived. Ansa said he moved like he knew the ground. Measured from the boiling house cellar mouth to the cliff edge. Marked the spots with chalk. Counted paces. Wrote numbers in a notebook. Did not speak. Did not look at her."

"Did he see you?"

Tam shook his head. "I came up the back alley. Ansa was already at the workbench, the chain in her hands. She motioned me quiet. She said find you. She said the chain matches the survey. Renn's sheet. The warm cellar marked on the east."

Sol's breath caught. Renn's sheet. The folded survey Ulla had given her. One cellar marked warm.

"Show us," she said.

Tam turned. The door swung inward. The tar shop smelled of pitch and pine resin and the slow burn of the kettle in the corner. Ansa stood at the workbench, her back to the door. The chain lay coiled beside a notebook, its links dull grey. Chalk marks scored the bench top: numbers, paces, arrows.

Ansa did not turn. Her voice carried the rasp of forty years at the tar. "He knew the line. He measured the line. He did not measure the path."

Sol stepped closer. The notebook lay open, page after page of figures in a neat, upright hand.

"Every pace matches the sheet," Ansa said. "The warm cellar. The second stair. The plate."

Kael moved to the bench. His square hand hovered over the notebook. He did not touch. "Then someone has a copy of Renn's sheet, or has walked where Renn walked."

"Or has been told where to measure," Sol said.

The chain lay coiled like a dead snake. The chalk marks scored the wood, distances and angles, the geometry of a room someone meant to enter.

Sol's hand found Kael's sleeve. Her fingers curled into the wool at his forearm. She did not grip. She did not turn him. She held on.

The tar shop door opened. Joren entered first, his scarred hand resting on the hilt at his belt. Corren followed, younger, his eyes scanning the bench, the notebook, the chain, the faces.

"Ansa sent word," Corren said. "A stranger measuring the east approach. Chain. Chalk. Notebook."

"We walk the approach at first light," Joren said. His voice was gravel. "All of us."

"Joren and Corren," Kael said. His hand covered hers on his sleeve. The square palm warm. The fingers closing slow. "Aloud. We go together."

The kettle hissed. A beam settled overhead. Ansa coiled the chain, her movements precise, practised, the links sliding through her fingers.

Sol thought of the blue point at the metering chamber window, steady as a held breath. Seventeen years stored. One wish spoken in the dark.

"I wish you to have them," she had said.

He had not answered. He had only held her hand.

Sol did not let go.
