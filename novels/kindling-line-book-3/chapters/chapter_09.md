Night had settled over the Lower Reach. The lamp on the sill was out. Below them the terrace path curled away into darkness, the ward-stones invisible, the tannery vats silent. One blue point burned at the metering chamber window, steady as a held breath.

Sol sat with her back against the window frame, legs drawn up, the wool of her undyed coat pulled tight at the collar. Kael sat beside her, his stick laid across his knees, the violet cuffs of his coat turned back. The night air carried mineral water and cold iron from the boiling house vents. Somewhere a gull cried, carried up from the water.

"He would give them to you."

Sol turned her head. Kael's profile was sharp against the dark, the line of his jaw, the bridge of his nose. His eyes were fixed on the blue point.

"Who?"

"You. Me. The years." His voice was low, stripped of the ledger cadence he used for tallies. "If it worked that way. I would give them to you."

The words landed between them, plain and heavy.

"It does not work that way."

"I know."

"Transfer is involuntary. The nearest living body pays. Nothing can be chosen. No ward-binding. No chosen buffer. No oath that moves years." She spoke each clause as if reading from a ledger. "You know the rule. You spoke it on the terrace this morning."

"I spoke it."

"Then why say it?"

Kael was silent. The blue point did not waver. Wind moved through the shutters, lifting the hair at his temple.

"Because I want you to know what I would do."

Sol's fingers curled into the wool at her knee. "I know what you would do. That is the problem."

A breath. Two. The gull cried again, farther now.

"You make it sound like a flaw."

"It is a flaw. In the arithmetic. In the —" She stopped. Her mouth twisted. "Do you intend to put it in a contract? A clause with a signature line?"

Kael's mouth twitched. "I have already drafted the clause."

"Let me guess. The party of the first part hereby conveys —"

"All years remaining, current and future, to the party of the second part, in perpetuity, subject to the Kindling's consent."

"The Kindling does not consent. The Kindling burns. The nearest body pays. Your clause is rubbish."

"Rubbish with a signature line."

"Rubbish."

His hand lay on his thigh, palm up, the square palm scarred at the back, the fingers relaxed. Sol reached across the space between them. Her fingers closed on his wrist, turned his hand over. The skin was warm. The lines of his palm were deep, etched by rope and rope-rig and the stick he carried.

She traced the life line with her thumb, slow, deliberate, the way she had once checked a climbing rope for wear — fingertip pressure, searching for the fray that meant failure.

"I do not want your years."

Kael's breath caught. His hand did not pull away.

"I want you to have them."

The words hung in the dark. The blue point burned. The wind tasted of iron and salt.

He turned his hand under hers, catching her fingers, lacing them through his own. The grip was firm. His thumb brushed the heel of her palm.

"Say it again."

"No."

"Say it as a wish. Not an offer. A wish."

She was silent. The shutters rattled. The blue point held.

"I wish you to have them."

His hand tightened on hers. He did not speak. She did not speak. They sat on the sill above the dark Reach, hands clasped, the night moving around them, and the wish sat between them like a stone laid on a ledger — acknowledged, recorded, not undone.

The morning after, Odo converted the east gauge by Renn's survey table.

Sol stood at the metering chamber window, the blue lamp at her back. The gauge glass was thick, the fluid dark, the needle motionless. Seventeen. The number had not moved since Renn was found. It had not moved in forty years before that.

"Seventeen," Odo said. His voice was steady. The limp was gone from his step, the grimace gone from his face. He marked the slate, pencil precise. "Confirmed. The stored sum is seventeen years."

Tessa capped her chalk. "The gauge does not go down at night. It has not gone down in six days of readings."

"Six days," Sol said. "Since the first stone."

"Since the first stone," Odo agreed. "The nearest living body stopped standing near the plate. The gauge held."

Sol's gaze dropped to the Instrument of Dissolution on the table beside the gauge. Council custody. Stored charge under Council seal. The parchment was yellow at the edges, the ink brown. She had seen it before, in the Chancellor's office, the night the petition was entered. The Chancellor's thin voice reading the clauses. The custodian's order dated that same night, thirty days from its date, served sixteen days later.

Whoever the Council sent to stand at the line would be the nearest.

The thought settled in her chest, cold and familiar. She wanted those years. She hated that she wanted them.

Mara's cold climbed. The line's draw. The wrists on the fourth. The forearms by Day 8. The elbows by Day 27. Closing the line would stop it. The stored sum would go to the nearest living body. The same rule as cost, in the other direction.

"Seventeen," she said again. The word tasted of iron.

Odo gathered his slate. "The custodian's order has fourteen days remaining. The first attempt on Day Twenty-Nine."

"Fourteen," Tessa said. She folded her chalk line, belt clicking. "Ansa wants us at the tar shop. She says a stranger has been measuring the east approach with a chain."

Sol's hand tightened on Kael's. The blue point at the metering chamber window did not waver.

"A stranger," she said. "Measuring the east approach with a chain."

"Ansa's words," Tessa said. "Not mine. She sent Tam to find us. He is waiting at the tar shop."

Odo's pencil paused on the slate. "The east approach. The second line runs under the boiling house cellar. The chain would trace the surface path above it."

"Or the surface path to the Lower Reach," Kael said. His voice was flat. "The custodian's pick is named on Day Twenty-Six. Today is Day Seven. The Council has not named the pick. A stranger with a chain suggests they are scouting the ground before the pick arrives."

"Or the pick is already here," Tessa said. She did not look at them. Her eyes tracked the blue point. "Measuring. Waiting for the order to run out."

The wind gusted through the shutters. The lamp flame at the metering chamber window burned steady, fed by a line that did not go down at night.

Sol released Kael's hand. She stood, the wool of her coat settling around her. The cold iron of the window frame bit into her palm where she braced herself.

"Tam," she said. "He came alone?"

"Ansa would not send him alone if she thought danger," Tessa said. "She sent him because he knows the tar shop. He knows the back ways. He signed the standard and withdrew and still works the tar. He moves quiet."

"Tam moves quiet," Odo agreed. "But a stranger with a chain does not measure for quiet. They measure for distance. For peg placement. For the rope rig that pulls twin brass pegs in one breath from outside the room."

The words laid themselves out. The pegs. The rope rig. The two pullers beyond the room's reach. Odo and Tessa. The custodian's order with fourteen days remaining.

"Then we go to the tar shop," Sol said. "We hear what Ansa heard. We see what the chain measured."

Kael rose beside her. His stick came up smooth. His left leg took the weight without a hitch. The violet cuffs caught the blue light. "Joren and Corren," he said. "If the stranger is Council. If the stranger is the pick. We do not go alone."

Tessa's chalk line clicked at her belt. "I will send word. They are at the terrace path, stone five. Corren's wrist held. He will move fast."

"Send word," Kael said. "We move now."

They left the metering chamber. The stair down to the terrace path was steep, the iron rungs cold under their hands. Sol went first. Kael followed, stick tucked under his arm, breathing even. The blue point shrank behind them, a single eye watching the gauge that read seventeen and did not fall.

The terrace path wound through darkness. Ward-stones loomed like dead sentinels, their gauge glass dark, their copper wire green with verdigris. The tannery vats below were black shapes steaming in the night. The boiling house vents exhaled mineral heat, a grey plume that smelled of iron and old water.

Tam waited at the tar shop door. Ansa's nephew. The boy who had signed on the tenth and withdrawn on the fifteenth. His face was pale in the lantern light hung over the threshold. The flame threw his shadow long across the cobbles.

"Ansa's inside," he said. His voice was low. "She has the chain. The stranger left it. Coiled on the workbench like a dead snake."

"Where is the stranger?" Kael asked.

"Gone. Before I arrived. Ansa said he moved like he knew the ground. Measured from the boiling house cellar mouth to the cliff edge. Marked the spots with chalk. Counted paces. Wrote numbers in a notebook. Did not speak. Did not look at her."

"Did he see you?"

Tam shook his head. "I came up the back alley. The tar buckets were empty by the door. Ansa was already at the workbench, the chain in her hands. She motioned me quiet. She said find you. She said the chain matches the survey. Renn's sheet. The warm cellar marked on the east."

Sol's breath caught. Renn's sheet. The folded survey Ulla had given her. One cellar marked warm. The second stair. The chalk on the stair riser: THIS ONE HAS NO BOTTOM I HAVE FOUND.

"Show us," she said.

Tam turned. The door swung inward. The tar shop smelled of pitch and pine resin and the slow burn of the kettle in the corner. Ansa stood at the workbench, her back to the door. The chain lay coiled beside a notebook, its links dull grey. Chalk marks scored the bench top — numbers, paces, arrows.

Ansa did not turn. Her voice carried the rasp of forty years at the tar. "He knew the line. He measured the line. He did not measure the path."

Sol stepped closer. The notebook lay open. The handwriting was neat, precise, the letters upright with closed descenders. A practised hand. Careful to look younger than it was.

"Renn's hand," she said. The words came out quiet. "Or the hand that taught him."

Ansa's shoulders shifted. "The works steward taught him. The works steward taught the boy who forged the witness line. The same hand leans right. The same hand carried the lines for forty years. Now a stranger comes with a chain and measures the east approach in paces that match the survey. The warm cellar. The second stair. The plate."

Kael moved to the bench. His square hand hovered over the notebook. He did not touch. "The custodian's pick. Named on Day Twenty-Six. The order served on Day Sixteen. The Council had sixteen days to name the pick before they told us. They named the pick. The pick is here. Measuring."

"The pick is a ward-taker of the Council's roll," Tessa said from the doorway. Her chalk line was coiled. Her face was pale. "One ward-taker. The order names only the role. Not the name. The pick measures the ground. The pick knows the pegs. The pick knows the rope rig. The pick waits for Day Twenty-Nine."

"Day Twenty-Nine is the first attempt," Odo said. He had followed them in, his slate under his arm, his knee bending smooth. "The order runs out on Day Thirty. The pick has one day to close the line before the order expires. If the pick fails, the Council must reissue. New order. New thirty days. New pick."

"Or the pick succeeds," Kael said. "The pegs pull. The second line closes. The stored sum goes to the nearest living body. The pick stands at the plate. The pick takes seventeen years."

"Or the pick brings a body," Sol said. Her voice was stripped of humour. "A body to stand at the plate. A body to be the nearest. The pick does not take the years. The body takes the years. The pick walks away with a closed line and a clean ledger."

The notebook sat on the bench. The chain lay coiled beside it. The chalk marks scored the wood: distances, angles, the geometry of a rope rig that needed two pullers beyond the room's reach.

"Who is the body?" Tam asked. "Who stands near the plate on Day Twenty-Nine?"

The question hung in the tar-thick air. The kettle hissed. A beam settled overhead.

Sol looked at Kael. His profile in the lantern light. The scar on the back of his hand. The stick leaning against the bench. The violet cuffs turned back.

"The nearest living body," she said. "The rule does not choose. The rule measures distance. The plate is in the chamber behind the iron door. The second stair descends to it. The room is small. The plate is at the foot of the stair. The pegs are in the ledge and on the table. The rope rig runs from the pegs up the stair, through the iron door, out the cellar mouth, to the pullers waiting beyond the room's reach."

"Odo and Tessa," Kael said. "They are the pullers. They stand outside the room. They pull in one breath. The pegs come free. The line closes. The stored sum goes to the nearest living body inside the room."

"Who is inside the room?" Ansa asked. She turned then. Her face was lined, her eyes dark. "The pick stands at the plate. The pick is the nearest. The pick takes the years. Unless another body stands closer."

"A body the pick brings," Tessa said. "A body the pick positions. A body the pick sacrifices."

"Or a body that chooses to stand there," Odo said. His pencil tapped the slate. "The rule does not ask consent. The rule measures distance. If Sol sits at the plate, and the pick stands three paces back, and Kael stands two paces back — Kael is the nearest. Kael takes the years."

The silence stretched. The kettle hissed. The chain lay coiled like a dead snake.

"I will sit at the plate," Sol said. "On Day Twenty-Nine. I will sit at the plate on Day Forty-Three. I will sit at the plate every time the line demands it."

"Sol," Kael said. Her name was a warning. A plea. The argument in his head made visible.

She turned to him. The lantern light caught the grey of her eyes. The line of her jaw. The hand that had traced his palm on the sill, checking for wear.

"I do not want your years," she said. "I want you to have them. But I will not let the pick bring a stranger to die at the plate. I will not let the Council choose the nearest living body. I will be there. I will be the nearest. The stored sum comes to me. Seventeen years. Added to the sixteen and a half I have left. Thirty-three and a half. You have thirty-three. We grow old together."

"The arithmetic does not work that way," Kael said. "The stored sum goes to the nearest living body. If you are the nearest, you take it. If I am the nearest, I take it. We cannot both be the nearest. One body. One transfer. The rule does not split."

"Then we make sure of the distance," she said. "We make sure of the room. We make sure of the pegs. We make sure of the pullers. We make sure of the pick."

"And if the pick brings a body?" Ansa asked. "A body the pick positions closer than you? A body the pick sacrifices?"

"Then that body takes the years," Sol said. "And we fail. And Mara's cold keeps climbing. And the line keeps drawing. And we try again on Day Forty-Three. Or we do not."

"You speak as if failure is an option," Kael said. "Failure is not an option. The custodian's order runs out on Day Thirty. The pick has one attempt. If the pick fails, the Council reissues. The line stays open. Mara's cold keeps climbing. The draw does not stop. The wrists. The forearms. The elbows. The shoulders. The chest. The heart. The line takes her piece by piece until the line closes or she dies."

"The line closes when the pegs pull," Odo said. "The pegs pull when the rope rig works. The rope rig works when the pullers pull in one breath. The pullers pull when the signal comes. The signal comes from the plate. From the body at the plate. From Sol."

"Or from the pick," Tessa said. "Or from the body the pick brings."

The notebook on the bench waited. The chain waited. The chalk marks waited.

Sol's hand found Kael's sleeve. Fourth touch. Her fingers curled into the wool at his forearm. She did not grip. She did not turn him. She held on.

"Fourteen days," she said. Low. For him alone.

"Fourteen," he agreed. His hand covered hers. The square palm warm. The fingers closing slow. "Fourteen days to learn the room. Fourteen days to learn the pegs. Fourteen days to learn the rope rig. Fourteen days to make sure the nearest living body is the one we choose."

"The rule does not choose," Ansa said. She picked up the chain. The links slid through her fingers, dull grey. "The rule measures. But the room can be measured. The pegs can be placed. The rope rig can be rigged. The pullers can be positioned. The signal can be agreed. The distance can be controlled."

"Controlled," Kael repeated. "Not chosen. The difference matters."

"Does it?" Ansa coiled the chain. Her movements were precise, practised. "The pick measures. We measure. The pick places. We place. The pick pulls. We pull. The difference is who holds the rope."

"And who sits at the plate," Sol said.

The tar shop door opened. Corren entered first, his scarred hand resting on the hilt at his belt. Joren followed, older, heavier, his eyes scanning the bench, the notebook, the chain, the faces.

"Ansa sent word," Corren said. "A stranger measuring the east approach. Chain. Chalk. Notebook. Matches the survey."

"Matches Renn's sheet," Joren said. His voice was gravel. "The warm cellar. The second stair. The plate. The pegs. The rope rig. The pick is here. The pick is ready."

"Day Twenty-Nine," Odo said. "Fourteen days. The first attempt."

"Fourteen days," Tessa echoed. She capped her chalk line. The click was final.

Sol's fingers tightened on Kael's sleeve. The wool was rough. The warmth beneath it was steady. The blue point at the metering chamber window burned in her memory, steady as a held breath. Seventeen years stored. Forty years carried. One wish spoken in the dark.

"I wish you to have them," she had said.

He had not answered. He had only held her hand.

The kettle hissed. A beam settled. The chain lay coiled in Ansa's hands, measured and marked for a rope rig that would pull twin brass pegs in one breath, from outside the room's reach, by two pullers who would not be the nearest living body.

Fourteen days.

Sol did not let go.
