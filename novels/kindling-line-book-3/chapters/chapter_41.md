The cellar mouth yawned before them, a dark throat in the boiling-house cellar floor. Lantern light caught the damp stone, the iron rungs set into the wall, the first turn of the stair going down into warmth.

Odo and Tessa went down first. The cords lay coiled over their shoulders, thick hemp that would draw the pegs when the time came. Their boots rang on the iron, slow and deliberate.

"Your reading of the sill was off by a hair," Tessa said, her voice low, bouncing off the curved walls. "The chalk will show it."

"My reading was exact," Odo replied, not looking back. "Yours was the one that wandered."

"Then the chalk will show that too."

Sol watched them go, the lantern light swallowing them whole. The argument faded into the stairwell, low and steady, the sound of two people who had done this together for years and would do it again.

She stood at the cellar mouth with the cold at her back and the warmth rising from below. The cloth-wrapped peg pressed against her hip through her coat. Kael carried his in his own pocket, one each, as they had agreed. He planted his stick beside her and the ferrule bit the stone. At the top of the cellar steps Joren and Corren stood in their grey cloaks, as still as gateposts.

Sol turned to Kael. The lantern caught the grey at his temple, the line of his jaw, the white strand threading above his ear.

"I want to go down the stair with you," she said. "Not behind. Not ahead. Beside you, where the stair allows. Will you walk beside me?"

He did not answer at once. His eyes held hers. A count of three passed, marked by the clack of Odo's boots on the iron and the low hum that came up from below.

"Yes," he said.

"That was three," she said.

"It was." The corner of his mouth moved. "You counted too."

"It has become a habit. I am not proud of it."

"I am a little proud of it."

The first steps were wide enough for two. The stick clicked on every riser. *Click. Step. Click. Step.*

"That is the best sound in the Reach," Sol said.

"It is the sound of a man being slow on purpose."

"It is the sound of a man who is coming. Which is better."

He glanced at her, the corner of his mouth lifting again. "I would like to be heard from further off in future."

"Then I will hear you from the top of the world."

At the turn the stair bent left and the wall widened into a narrow landing, room enough for two to stand. The warmth thickened there. It smelled of mineral and old iron and the yeast-sour tang that lived in the boiling-house walls, and the hum was close now, a low pulse she could feel in her ribs. Below the landing the stair ran on down to its foot. From here she could see the edge of the bare iron door, a darker rectangle in the lantern glow, and the plate at its base.

Sol stopped. "Wait here," she said.

Kael's hand tightened on the stick. "I can see the plate from the turn."

"You can watch from there. You are allowed one day of watching. Tomorrow you will be at the door."

"That is a very generous allowance."

"I am a generous woman. You should not tell anyone."

He did not move from the turn. The stick stayed planted and his weight settled on the good leg, and he let her go without another word. She knew what that cost him, and did not say so.

She went on alone. The last steps were steep, the iron cold under her boots, then warm, then hot as she neared the foot. The floor at the bottom was bare stone and not wide. The bare iron door stood against the far wall with the plate set at its base, black, and before it a single worn step where a person could sit. Odo was down on one knee at the sill with a lump of chalk, laying a mark with his thumb along the line. Tessa stood over him with the lamp held up to the lintel. Neither of them looked round. They had the faces of people who would not be spoken to until a mark was right.

She did not sit. She held her hands out over the plate and felt the heat come up her wrists like water from a pump. Then she laid her palm flat on the iron, once, and took it away again.

It was warm. It was warm the way the knot over the Upper Iron Door had been warm, like skin, and a little more than that.

She climbed the same steps back up. Kael had not moved. The lantern on the step lit his face from below, the grey at his temple, the shadow under his cheekbone where the stick had pressed him the night before.

She stood in front of him and held out her palm. The heat came off it, almost visible, a shimmer in the cold air.

"The iron is warm," she said.

She reached up and pressed her palm to his cheek.

His skin was warm. The same warmth, the plate's warmth carried up the stair in her hand, or one source feeding both of them. She felt his pulse jump under her fingers, quick and alive.

"That is what it is like," she said.

His eyes closed. His face leaned into her hand, a small motion, complete.

"It is like you," he said.

"That is not a compliment to the iron."

"It is not meant for the iron."

They stayed. The hum rose and fell. The stick clicked once as he shifted. Her thumb traced the line of his cheekbone, the roughness of his jaw, the white strand at his temple. The lantern threw their shadows long against the curved wall, leaning toward each other until they were one shape.

"Marks are down," Tessa's voice called up the stair, faint but clear. "Odo wants the sill looked at before he rubs it out and starts over."

"I never said I would rub it out," said Odo, further off.

Sol's fingers did not leave Kael's face. "I will tell the chalk to wait."

"The chalk has waited forty years," he said, his voice low, shaped by the stairwell.

His hand came up and covered hers and held it against his cheek. The warmth went round, plate to palm to cheek to palm.

"Forty years," she said. "And we are the ones who found the bottom."

"Renn found the bottom. We are the ones who have to close it."

"Tomorrow the pick comes."

"And the day after, the line."

The words hung in the warm air. Below them the hum went on in its slow rhythm, and Odo's voice came up the stair, reading a measure aloud, and Tessa answered it.

Sol let her hand slide from his cheek. His hand fell to the stick.

"Go," he said. "Look at your chalk. I will be here."

"I will count aloud," she said. "So that you can hear it from the turn."

"Then I will listen."

She went down the last steps a second time. At the foot Odo had finished. Two marks stood white on the stone, one on the lintel above the iron door and one on the sill below it, and he was bent over the lower one with his thumb and forefinger spread, measuring it against the upper by eye and then by hand. He did it again.

"Twice," he said, and straightened.

Tessa ran her chalk-line from the one mark to the other and held it taut. She looked at it for a long moment.

"A hair to the left," she said, with dignity. "Mine. The chalk shows it."

"It does," said Odo.

"You may say it."

"I do not need to."

Tessa crouched at the gauge and read it. Then she read it again, her lips moving. "Seventeen. Fixed."

"Twice read," Odo said.

The number stood in the small room and nobody added to it. Odo wiped the chalk from his thumb onto his coat.

"Tomorrow there will be cords behind you and the two of us on them," he said. "Today there is only the step. Learn it."

"I mean to."

"Good. I will not say so twice."

Sol looked at the single step before the plate, and at the black iron behind it, and found that her hands were steady.

"I am going to sit," she called up the stair, so that it would carry.

"I am here," came Kael's voice from the turn, level and unhurried.

Odo and Tessa drew back to the foot of the stair without being asked and stood very quiet. Sol sat down on the single worn step. The heat reached her through her coat, through her boots, and she laid her palm flat on the iron.

The warmth met her hand, steady and alive. A hum traveled up her arm, not sound but sensation, the line's pulse settling toward her own. For a breath the plate seemed to know her. Not as a ward. Not as a name. As a body that had come to the right place.

She did not burn. She did not pull away.

The last time she had come to this iron she had burned a year at it on purpose, and Kael had run in and paid the whole of it before she could stop him. She had come down the stair braced for that again, shoulders and wrists and all. The pull did not come. There was only the warmth, and the slow rise and fall of it under her palm, and the plain fact of her own hand on the iron.

Eleven breaths. The line's charge cycle. She counted them in the bones of her wrist. *One. Two. Three.* The hum rose and fell. *Four. Five. Six.* The warmth deepened. *Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven.*

A pause. A twelfth breath, slower, deeper, the second line's addition.

The iron had not done anything. Everything else in this business had been decided by other people: an order with a seal on it, a column of figures that did not add, a hand that had carried years and entered them under another name. The plate had decided nothing. It was warm because it was warm, and she was sitting because she had chosen to sit, and it was the first thing in all of it that had not been decided for her.

"Eleven," she said aloud. "And the twelfth after. Same as before."

Her voice went round the little room. Up the stair, once, the stick clicked.

"I am not afraid of it today," she said, to the iron, to the two techs, to the man at the turn.

The twelfth breath passed under her hand.

"And I am," she said. "A little."

The admission sat in the warm dark. Nobody answered it, and nobody needed to. The plate went on humming. From the turn there came no word at all, only the stillness of a man who had heard and was staying where he was.

She took her hand from the iron. The warmth lingered in her palm and her fingers, a ghost heat that would not wash off. She stood.

Odo and Tessa went up first, the cords whispering on their coats, as they had come down. Sol followed them. Kael was waiting at the turn with the stick planted and the lantern beside his boot.

"You said it aloud," he said, very low.

"You were listening."

"I said I would."

She stopped beside him a moment, close enough that her sleeve touched his. Then she took the wrapped peg from her pocket and laid it on a dry step above the damp. He took his from his own and laid it beside hers. Two bundles of cloth, side by side, giving nothing away.

Then she went on up with the techs, and Kael came last, stick and step, stick and step, the sound of a man who was coming.

The cellar mouth opened above them. Cool air met her face, with the smell of tar and mineral dust and the wind off the cliff. Joren stood at the top of the cellar steps with his scarred hand resting on his belt. Corren stood a pace behind him, his face calm, the lines around his eyes deeper in the lantern light.

Joren turned his head as they came up out of the stair.

"The Council's grey has been seen on the Council road," he said. "Three figures."

Nobody answered him at once. Kael came up the last of the steps and stopped at her shoulder.

"Have they turned toward us?" Sol asked.

"Not yet," said Corren. "They are on the road."

Joren looked out over the yard wall toward the east approach and said nothing more. Behind them, down in the stair, the plate went on with its eleven breaths and its twelfth.
