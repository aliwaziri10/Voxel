The stair spat her out into air that bit. First light lay thin and grey across the lane, the cobblestones still holding the night's chill. Sol stood at the top of the two hundred and eleven steps, the stick held out in front of her like a flag, her knuckles white on the worn grain. Her breath came in clouds that did not rise. The muscle in her thighs trembled, a clean ache she had earned step by step.

Kael stood three paces away, his back to the lane, his face turned toward the stair mouth. He had come up from the lane and gone no farther. Joren had told him she would be coming. He had promised to wait where he was asked.

The stick was warm. His palm had shaped it night after night. Hers had carried it up from the dark and the wood had taken her heat and given it back.

He took it from her. His fingers closed on the ferrule, the shaft, the groove worn by generations of boots. For a long moment neither of them spoke. The lane held its breath. The boiling house vents breathed mineral air. Somewhere a rooster crowed, late and ragged.

"You carried it very well," he said.

His voice was rough, unused. The grey light caught the line of his jaw, the shadow under his cheekbones.

"It is a heavy stick," she said. "And a good one. I have told it so."

He made a sound, not quite a laugh. "I have been talking to a window."

"I have been talking to a stick."

They looked at each other. The almost-laugh rose in her chest, a fragile thing that had not lived there in three days. His mouth twitched, the corner lifting. The sound he made this time was closer to a chuckle, low and surprised.

The moment stretched. He shifted his weight, one boot heel lifting. His body leaned toward her, half a step, the intention written plain in the tension of his shoulders. Then he stopped. The heel settled. The lean became a stillness.

She saw it. He saw her see it.

"I am keeping to my side of the step," he said.

"The step is wide enough for both," she said. "And I would like you to come to me when I ask. Not before."

He nodded, the movement small. "I know. It is a very small distance to be so hard."

She moved before she could think. Her palm landed flat on his chest, over the wool of his coat, over the bone and the muscle and the heart going too fast. She felt the rhythm hammer against her hand, frantic and alive.

His hand came up. He covered hers. He did not press. He did not pull. He only held, his square fingers spreading over her knuckles, his thumb resting on the back of her wrist.

"Slower," she said.

He breathed. The air moved in his lungs, a long slow pull. She felt the expansion under her palm, the ribs rising. He let it out. The hammer slowed. Another breath. Another. The rhythm steadied, grew heavy, grew calm.

He did not say yes before arguing. She did not ask him anything. He only breathed, and she felt it under her hand, the storm settling into a tide.

"I have a great deal to say," she said. "And not here."

"The room is the right place."

They turned. The lane stretched grey and empty before them. They walked side by side, a hand's breadth apart, not touching. The stick tapped a slow rhythm on the stones in his grip. Her fingers found his sleeve, the rough wool at his cuff, and took hold.

The room over the tar-shop smelled of tar and old broth and the mineral tang that never quite left the air. The trapdoor fell shut behind them with a thud that sealed the lane away. The lamp was unlit. Grey light came through the window, a long bar across the floorboards that caught dust motes and the worn edges of the table.

Kael set the stick against the wall. The ferrule found its groove. He did not sit. He stood with his weight on his right leg, the left braced, the numbness a white line he did not show.

Sol pulled the chair out. She sat. Her hands folded on the table, the initials I.V. burned into the wood in front of her, faded to brown.

"The flicker," she said. "I felt it at the plate. I felt the line wake and I did not step back. I let it take the year. I let you pay it."

The words fell into the room, flat and final. She did not look away from his face.

"The shame," she continued. "It is not that I burned. It is that I did not stop it. That I knew what would happen and I stood there anyway. I will not let you pay for me again. I will ask you aloud. And I will wait for your answer."

Kael's jaw worked. His hand moved to the back of the chair, fingers curling on the wood.

"I did not decide," he said. "I moved. The plate hummed and the line woke and I was in the room before I knew I had chosen to be. I wrote that I would say it first. I did not. I do not know that I can promise it. I will not say I can."

The honesty landed harder than a lie would have. She saw the muscle in his jaw jump. She saw the pulse in his throat, steady now under the wool.

"I have read the fifth page," she said. "I do not want a promise about your body. I want you to answer when I ask."

He was quiet. The room held the hum from the passage below, a low vibration in the floorboards. The gauge-book lay closed on the table, its cover worn smooth at the corners.

"I will try," he said.

The words were rough. They cost him something. She saw it in the way his shoulders settled, the way his breath left him in a long slow rush.

"That is the true answer," she said. "And I will take it."

She leaned back. The chair legs scraped on the boards.

"It is not solved," she said. "You have failed once. I will not pretend you have not."

He met her eyes. "I have failed once. I will not pretend I have not."

"The next attempt is not tomorrow."

"No."

"I will ask Renn, through Ulla, to draw the room. So that it is settled where each person may stand. Who can be nearest to the plate. The plan cannot go forward on habit."

"Agreed."

The word hung between them. Simple. Heavy.

She felt lighter. The weight that had sat in her chest since Day 29 had lifted, just a fraction, just enough to notice. She did not trust it.

"I feel lighter," she said aloud. "And I do not trust it."

He made that sound again, the not-quite-laugh. "Neither do I."

The lamp stayed unlit. The grey bar of light climbed the opposite wall. The hum from the passage did not waver. They spoke in turn, the plain things that had no place in the lane or on the stair. The flicker. The shame. The year he had paid and the year she had let him pay. The fifth page in his coat. The map that did not exist yet. The habit that had nearly killed them both.

When the words ran out, the room was darker. The window showed a square of deepening grey.

He moved first. The stick came with him. He crossed to the bed and set it down, then turned back to her. She rose. The chair scraped again.

The door closed. The latch caught.

They lay down together, the bed narrow and the blankets thin. She settled with her back to his chest. His arm came over her, heavy and warm, his hand resting on her ribs. The stick leaned against the wall beside the bed, patient and worn.

"The sock is a very good sock," she said into the pillow.

She felt his chest move, the silent laugh. "I have had good reason to be grateful for it."

No friction. They had said the hard things. They were tired.

His breath stirred the hair at her temple. Slow. Even. The rhythm she had felt under her palm on the stair, now at her back.

"Thirty-three," she said under her breath.

"Sixteen and a half."

Neither added. Neither subtracted. The numbers lay in the dark between them, two figures that governed everything and explained nothing.

"Can we have the whole hour tomorrow?" she asked. "The one I asked for before."

She felt him shift, the wool of his coat rustling. His hand moved on her ribs, fingers tapping once against the fabric.

"I have it in my coat."

The lane stayed silent. The hum from the passage stayed steady. Sleep pulled at her, gentle and unresisted. The last thing she felt was his thumb tracing a slow circle on her side, a touch that asked nothing and gave everything.

---

The trapdoor creaked.

Sol woke to grey light and the smell of tar. Kael was already moving, the stick in his hand, the left leg stiff as he crossed to the ladder. The bed was cold on his side.

"Ansa," he said.

She climbed the ladder, the pot cradled in both hands. Steam rose thin and straight. Her brown knotted fingers were white on the handle. She did not look at the gate. She did not look at the pick. She set the pot on the hearth and wiped her hands on her apron.

"Good."

The word landed in the room, final and unadorned. Sol swung her legs over the bed edge. The floorboards were cold under her feet.

"Renn first," she said to Kael.

He nodded once. The stick tapped the floor. He went down the ladder into the lane.

She followed, the undyed coat settling on her shoulders. The morning air bit. The pick stood at the gate, a figure in Council grey, motionless as a boundary stone. Two men flanked the pick, unnamed, silent. Joren and Corren held the gate on this side.

Kael stopped three paces from the gate. "Joren and Corren."

The names anchored him. Joren glanced at him, once, then back at the pick. Corren did not look.

Sol walked past them, up the lane toward the boiling house yard. The cellar door yawned. Odo Marl stood at the bottom, broad shoulders folded against the damp. Tessa Rook crouched beside a coil of rope, chalk-line at her belt.

"No one goes below until Sol is back," Kael had said the day before. "Myself included."

The techs had nodded. The cords stayed coiled. The plate hummed. Nobody pulled.

She descended the ladder. The passage mouth gaped at the foot of the second stair, a black rectangle in the stone. The hum rose through her boots, a resonance in the bone. The air moved slow and warm, carrying the mineral tang.

Odo looked up. Tessa stood, wiping chalk dust on her trousers.

"Renn," Sol said. "Ulla. The room needs drawing. Where each person may stand. Who can be nearest to the plate."

Odo grunted. Tessa clipped the rope to her belt.

"We will send word," Odo said.

She nodded. Turned. Climbed back up the ladder into the lane. The pick watched her. The two men watched her. Joren and Corren watched her.

Kael fell into step beside her, a hand's breadth apart. Her fingers found his sleeve again.

The stair mouth waited, two hundred and eleven steps dropping into dark. She did not look down. She looked at the lane, at the boiling house, at the gate where the pick stood motionless in Council grey.

"Day 32," she said. "Thirty-three days since the petition. The order is ash."

"The pick does not know it," Kael said. "Or does not care."

"Either way. The map comes first."

They walked. The rooster crowed again, late and ragged. The lane emptied of the last cart. The hum from the passage followed them, a low vibration in the stone that did not waver and did not stop.

They reached the gate where the pick stood motionless in Council grey. Joren and Corren held the crossbar, the wood groaning under the pressure of the two men on the other side who did not speak and did not yield. The pick's face was a grey cloth mask, eyes the colour of the lane stones.

Kael stopped three paces back. "Joren and Corren."

The names anchored him. Joren glanced once, scar pale in the morning light. Corren did not look.

Sol stopped beside Kael, a hand's breadth between them. Her fingers found his sleeve. The hum from the passage rose through the soles of her boots, a low vibration that had not wavered through the night and did not waver now.

The pick raised a hand. The two men shifted. The gate held.

"Day 32," Sol said. Her voice carried to the pick, to the men, to the wardens. "Thirty-three days since the petition. The order is ash."

The pick did not move. The two men did not move.

"The pick does not know it," Kael said. "Or does not care."

"Either way." She looked at the gate, at the grey figure, at the crossbar that would not budge. "The map comes first."

Odo's voice drifted up from the cellar below, muffled by stone. "We will send word."

The rope stayed coiled. The cords stayed coiled. The plate hummed. Nobody pulled.

She turned. The lane stretched grey and empty toward the stair mouth, the two hundred and eleven steps waiting in shadow. The boiling house vents breathed mineral air. The rooster crowed again, late and ragged, the sound fraying at the edges.

Kael fell into step beside her. Her fingers tightened on his sleeve. The stick tapped a slow rhythm on the stones.

They walked up the lane, away from the gate, away from the pick, toward the stair where the day had begun. The hum followed them, a resonance in the bone, a tremor in the stone that did not waver and did not stop.

At the stair mouth, Ansa waited. The empty pot hung from her crooked fingers, steam gone cold. She did not look at the gate. She did not look at the pick. Her eyes found Sol.

"Good."

The word landed, final and unadorned.

Sol released Kael's sleeve. "Renn first."

They began the descent, two hundred and eleven steps into dark, the stick tapping, the hum rising, the lane emptying behind them.
