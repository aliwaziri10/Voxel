The stair spat her out into air that bit. First light lay thin and grey across the lane, the cobblestones still holding the night's chill. Sol stood at the top of the two hundred and eleven steps, the stick held out in front of her like a flag in both hands, the empty pot hooked over her elbow by its handle, her knuckles white on the worn grain. Her breath came in clouds that did not rise. The muscles in her thighs trembled, a clean ache she had earned step by step.

Kael stood just beyond her reach, his back to the lane, his face turned toward the stair mouth. He had come up from the room and gone no farther. Joren had told him she would be coming. He had promised to wait where he was asked, and the asking had ended at the top of the steps.

The stick was warm. His palm had shaped it night after night. Hers had carried it up from the dark and the wood had taken her heat and given it back.

He took it from her. His fingers closed on the shaft, found the band of smooth wood where his palm belonged, and settled into it. For a long moment neither of them spoke. The lane was empty and still. The boiling house vents breathed mineral air. A rooster crowed in the tanner's yard, late and ragged. Sol let the pot slide down her arm and set it on the top step beside her boot, where it rang once against the stone and was still.

"You carried it very well," he said.

His voice was rough, unused. The grey light caught the line of his jaw, the shadow under his cheekbones.

"It is a heavy stick," she said. "And a good one. I have told it so."

He made a sound, not quite a laugh. "I have been talking to a wall."

"I have been talking to a stick."

They looked at each other. The almost-laugh rose in her chest, a fragile thing that had not lived there in three days. His mouth twitched, the corner lifting. The sound he made this time was closer to a chuckle, low and surprised.

In the grey light she saw what Joren had not told her. Above his ear there was a patch the width of two fingers where the dark hair had gone white, as if a thumb had pressed the color out of it. She looked at it too long. He let her.

"It is only hair," he said.

"It is the year." She did not reach for it. "I will not tell you it suits you."

"I would not believe you."

Her laugh came out cracked, and he did not look away from it.

The moment stretched. He shifted his weight, one boot heel lifting. His body leaned toward her, half a step, the intention written plain in the tension of his shoulders. Then he stopped. The heel settled. The lean became a stillness.

She saw it. He saw her see it.

"I am keeping to my side of the step," he said.

"The step is wide enough for both," she said. "And I would like you to come to me when I ask. Not before."

He nodded, the movement small. "I know. It is a very small distance to be so hard."

She moved before she could think. Her palm landed flat on his chest, over the wool of his coat, over the bone and the muscle and the heart going too fast. She felt the rhythm hammer against her hand, frantic and alive.

His hand came up. He covered hers. He did not press. He did not pull. He only held, his square fingers spreading over her knuckles, his thumb resting on the back of her wrist.

"Slower," she said.

He breathed. The air moved in his lungs, a long slow pull. She felt the expansion under her palm, the ribs rising. He let it out. The hammer slowed. Another breath. Another. The rhythm steadied, grew heavy, grew calm.

She did not ask him anything. He only breathed, and she felt it under her hand, the storm settling into a tide.

"I have a great deal to say," she said. "And not here."

"The room is the right place."

They turned. The lane stretched grey and empty before them. They walked side by side, a hand's breadth apart, not touching. The stick tapped a slow rhythm on the stones in his grip. Her fingers found his sleeve, the rough wool at his cuff, and took hold.

The room over the tar-shop smelled of tar and old broth and the mineral tang that never quite left the air. The door fell shut behind them with a thud that sealed the lane away. The lamp was unlit. Grey light came through the window, a long bar across the floorboards that caught dust motes and the worn edges of the table.

Kael set the stick against the wall beside the bed. He did not sit. He stood with his weight on his right leg, the left braced, the numbness a white line he did not show.

Sol pulled the chair out. She sat. Her hands folded on the table beside the gauge-book, and she saw him look at them. The base of her fingers was raw where the stick had rubbed, a red band across both palms.

"You carried it a long way," he said.

"Two hundred and eleven steps down, and the same up." She turned one hand over on the wood. "I am told the habit is easier on the hand that has it."

He did not smile. He crossed the floor, the left foot arriving a breath after the right, and stood at the edge of the table and looked at her palm without touching it, the way he would read a gauge he did not want to disturb. Then he went back to the wall. She understood that he had been keeping to his side of the table again, and that it had cost him the same as before.

"The flicker," she said. "When the year tore out of you, for one heartbeat I was glad it was not me. I said it to the stair, and the stair did not answer. I am saying it to you."

The words fell into the room, flat and final. She did not look away from his face.

"The shame," she continued. "It is not that I burned. It is that I was glad, and I will not be less ashamed tomorrow. I will not let you pay for me again. I will ask you aloud. And I will wait for your answer."

Kael's jaw worked. His hand moved to the back of the chair, fingers curling on the wood.

"I said it to my mother's wall first," she said. "It is easy to say a hard thing to a wall. It does not leave the room because it cannot. I wanted to say it once to a person who could."

"I am in the room," he said.

"You are."

"I did not decide," he said. "I moved. The plate woke and the line woke and I was on the stair before I knew I had chosen to be. I wrote that I would say it first. I did not. I do not know that I can promise it. I will not say I can."

The honesty landed harder than a lie would have. She saw the muscle in his jaw jump. She saw the pulse in his throat, steady now under the wool.

"I have read the fifth page," she said. "I do not want a promise about your body. I want you to answer when I ask."

He was quiet. The room held the quiet of the passage below, and no sound came up from it. The gauge-book lay closed on the table, its cover worn smooth at the corners.

"I will try," he said.

The words were rough. They cost him. She saw it in the way his shoulders settled, the way his breath left him in a long slow rush.

"That is the true answer," she said. "And I will take it."

She leaned back. The chair legs scraped on the boards.

"It is not solved," she said. "You have failed once. I will not pretend you have not."

He met her eyes. "I have failed once. I will not pretend I have not."

"Tell me about Mara."

He told it plainly, as he would have read it from a ledger. The cold had passed the elbow at dusk the day before. Joren had said so quietly and had not said what he thought of it. The pick was at the yard gate and had not come in, and Joren and Corren said No in the fewest words they could find.

"I stood at the door of the passage yesterday while the plate was awake," he said. "I kept my feet on my side of the threshold the whole of it. It changed nothing. It was only a thing I could do."

"It is not nothing."

"It is not enough."

"Both," she said, and he nodded, because it was the true answer too.

"Does the leg hurt?" she asked, aloud, and waited.

He took the time to answer, and she let him. "Not the way it did. It is cold from the hip down and it takes longer to do what I tell it. Yelva says a few days."

"Then we will take a few days."

"The next attempt is not tomorrow."

"No."

"I will ask Renn, through Ulla, to draw the room. So that it is settled where each person may stand. Who can be nearest to the plate. The plan cannot go forward on habit."

"Agreed."

The word sat between them, short and plain, and it weighed what it said.

She felt lighter. The weight that had sat in her chest since the plate had lifted, just a fraction, just enough to notice. She did not trust it.

"I feel lighter," she said aloud. "And I do not trust it."

He made that sound again, the not-quite-laugh. "Neither do I."

She thought of the seventeen as it sat in the gauge, her mother's one year and sixteen she could not account for. She did not try to account for them tonight. She had said what she had climbed the steps to say, and the sum could wait for the room Renn would draw.

The lamp stayed unlit. The grey bar of light climbed the opposite wall. The passage below gave no sound and did not change. They spoke in turn, the plain things that had no place in the lane or on the stair. The flicker. The shame. The year he had paid and the year she had been glad she did not. The fifth page in his coat. The map that did not exist yet. The habit that had nearly killed them both.

When the words ran out, the room was darker. The window showed a square of deepening grey.

He moved first. The stick came with him. He crossed to the bed and set it down, then turned back to her. She rose. The chair scraped again.

He dropped the latch.

They lay down together, the bed narrow and the blankets thin. She settled with her back to his chest. His arm came over her, heavy and warm, his hand resting on her ribs. The stick leaned against the wall beside the bed, patient and worn.

"The sock is a very good sock," she said into the pillow.

She felt his chest move, the silent laugh. "I have had good reason to be grateful for it."

There was nothing left to argue. They had said the hard things and they were tired, and the bed was narrow enough that neither of them had to ask which side was whose.

His breath stirred the hair at her temple. Slow. Even. The rhythm she had felt under her palm at the top of the steps, now at her back.

"Thirty-three," she said under her breath.

"Sixteen and a half."

Neither added. Neither subtracted. The numbers lay in the dark between them and were left there.

"Can we have the whole hour tomorrow?" she asked. "The one I asked for before."

She felt him shift, the wool of his coat rustling. His hand moved on her ribs, fingers tapping once against the fabric. She covered it with her own.

"I have it in my coat."

The lane stayed silent, and no sound came up from the passage below. She did not sleep at once. She counted her own breaths with her eyes shut, eleven and then the twelfth, the way she had counted them at the plate, and the room was quiet enough that she heard his breathing slow and fall into step with the count until she stopped being the only one keeping it. Sleep pulled at her, gentle and unresisted. The last thing she felt was his thumb tracing a slow circle on her side, a touch that asked nothing and gave everything.

---

Sol woke to grey light and the smell of tar. The bed was cold on his side, and for the length of one breath she lay still and did not call out, because she had asked him to answer when she asked, and she had not asked yet.

"Here," he said from the table.

He sat with the gauge-book open and the stick against the wall beside him. He had been awake long enough to read the same column twice. He did not stand. He looked at her, and the look said that he was keeping to the chair on purpose.

She dressed in the undyed coat and said nothing about the chair. At the head of the stair she stopped and asked it aloud, the first time, to see whether it could be done on an ordinary morning. "Will you walk up the lane with me, and stop where I stop?" He waited. She counted the wait, four breaths, and did not fill it. "I will try," he said. It was the same answer as the night before, and she took it the same way.

They went down the stair together, Kael with one hand on the rail and the stick in the other, the left foot arriving a breath after the right, Sol a tread below him on the side where the drop was. The lane was grey and nearly empty. The boiling house breathed steam at the far end of it. They did not talk. The quiet between them had been earned, and neither of them spent it.

Ansa was already at the top of the two hundred and eleven steps. She was bent over the top step, her knotted fingers closing on the handle of the pot Sol had left there, and when she straightened she held it against her apron in both hands, empty and scoured. She did not look down the stair. She did not look at the stick, or at the undyed coat. She looked at Sol's face, and then at Kael's, and then at the hand's breadth of morning air between them.

"Good," she said.

It was the whole of what she had come to say. She turned and went up the lane with the pot, and her boots made no sound on the stones.

Sol watched her go. The word sat in her chest beside the lightness she did not trust, and for once the two of them did not quarrel.

"Renn first," she said.

"Renn first," Kael said, and put his weight on the stick, and went up the lane beside her.
