The cliff road narrowed to a single worn track where the wind came off the water without warning. Sol felt it first in her sleeves, then in the hair across her cheeks, then in the way her breath shortened. The drop on the left fell straight to the tide line, a grey wall streaked with salt and bird lime. The right side pressed against cold rock, damp with seep. The sky had gone pink at the edges, the color of a bruise healing.

Kael walked half a step ahead, his stick tapping a rhythm on the stone. His left leg moved clean on level ground, the numbness quiet for now. She watched the swing of his coat, the set of his shoulders, the way his head turned fractionally toward the sound of the surf below.

The road curved. The drop side switched.

She moved before he could. Two quick steps, her boot finding the outside edge, her shoulder brushing the rock face as she passed him. The wind caught her coat and flared it.

Kael stopped. His stick planted. He looked down at her boots on the dangerous side, then up at her face.

A laugh started in his chest and came out whole, rough with surprise. The first one she had heard from him since the public step.

"It is not funny," she said.

"It is the most unnecessary thing anyone has done for me on this road." He was still laughing, and tried to stop, and could not. "The first time we walked it, I took the drop side and you let me."

"I did not know better then."

"And now you do."

"Now I know what the wall costs you and what the edge costs me. I would rather it cost me." She lifted her chin against the wind. "The road is the same width for both of us."

"The drop does not care whose shoulder touches the wall," he said, grave as a judge.

"The drop does not know whose side it is. Neither do I. I would rather it learned."

"That is bad cliff law."

"I am a bad lawyer. And the road is not yours."

He shifted his weight, the stick sliding on gravel as he moved to reclaim the outside. She brought her free hand up, palm flat against his chest, fingers spread over the wool.

"No."

His hand rose, hovered at her wrist. "My leg can feel the wall. That is more than yours can feel the edge."

"That is exactly why."

He let his hand fall. He let her stay where she was.

She had not known, the first time, what the outside edge cost him. She had let him take it because he asked in the plain voice he used for facts, and she had thought it was one. She knew better now, and the knowing sat in her chest beside the wind.

The wind gusted again, stronger this time, whipping a strand of her hair across her mouth. She tucked it back, then reached for his hand where it hung at his side. Her fingers found his, cold knuckles, warm palm. She drew his arm through the crook of her elbow, settling his weight against her side without making it look like help.

Their hips knocked as they fell into step. He did not move away.

"The wind is trying to read our conversation," she said.

"It can have the unimportant half."

For a little way they walked in step. Not counting. Not planning. The rhythm found itself, two strides matching without effort. The pink light deepened, bleeding into purple along the horizon. Far below, a seal barked, the sound carrying up the cliff face, and a second answered it from farther down the shingle.

"You are very quiet," he said.

"I am being a wall." She did not look at him. "It is harder than it sounds."

He laughed again, softer, and she held the sound against her ribs, because she did not know how many more of them there would be to keep.

She said, "I want to have been on this road with you on a day when nobody was dying."

The words sat in the space between them, honest and small.

He was quiet for three breaths. Four. "That is a fair thing to want. And I will keep it."

They went quiet because the healer's lamp was in sight, a yellow glow behind a shuttered window at the back of the tar-shop lane. The road widened. The drop fell back. The wind lost its teeth.

Sol kept hold of his hand until the door, then let go at the threshold without being asked.

The back room smelled of dried herbs, lye soap, and the sharp mineral tang of the boiling-house vents that ran beneath the lane. Yelva stood over the narrow bed, her hands folded at her waist, her face calm in the lamplight. Jars lined the shelf behind her, each labeled in a square, unhurried hand. Tessa Rook knelt beside the bed, a gauge-book open on her knee, chalk dust on her fingers.

Mara lay propped against pillows, her skin pale as the grey dawn. Her sleeves were turned back past the elbows. The cold was a white line climbing past the forearm, stopping at the joint, sharp and deliberate, as if drawn with a ruler. Along the inside of her arm ran a ladder of small chalk ticks in Yelva's hand, one for every night, wrist to elbow, and the last tick sat at the bend of the arm with nowhere left to climb but upward.

Joren stood by the door, his scarred hand braced on the frame. His eyes moved from Mara to Sol to Kael and back again. He did not speak. He had the look of a man who had been standing in that spot since the lamp was lit and meant to go on doing it.

"Elbows," Yelva said. Her voice was low, pitched for the room. "Reached them last night. The climb follows the nights anyone goes below. It has not missed one."

Sol nodded. She had known. She had counted the nights as everyone in the room had counted them, and the count had reached the joint of the arm and stopped there, waiting to be added to.

"Does it hurt?" she asked Mara.

"Not as it did." The warden's voice had come back to its own weight in the days since she woke, dry and level. "It aches the way a tooth aches when the weather turns. I can lift a cup. I cannot feel whether it is hot."

Sol sat down on the stool by the bed. She did not ask leave. She laid her palm over the white line at Mara's forearm, lightly, the way she would have laid it on a ward-stone, and the cold came up into her hand at once, flat and patient, with no pulse in it at all. It was not like the plate. The plate was warm and wanted an answer. This asked nothing. It only went on, and she understood, with her hand on it, that it would go on until the line was closed.

Mara watched her do it. She did not pull the arm away. "Cold as a well bucket," she said. "Do not stay too long. I will not have you catching it."

"It does not catch."

"Everything catches." She turned her head on the pillow. "Ashworth. You walked in on your own leg."

"On level ground," said Kael.

"Walk the rest the same way. I have no wish to be told you have gone down a stair on your face." Her eyes moved to Sol. "Is he doing as he is told?"

"He is doing as he said he would."

"That is a different thing, and a better one." The corner of her mouth moved. "I only ever got the first."

Tessa looked up from the gauge-book. She had not been drawn into the talk and did not seem to mind. "Seventeen. Fixed. Odo read it at dawn and I read it at midday, twice each, and the needle has not trembled."

Tessa wrote the figure again without looking at it, and then looked at it, the way a person does who has been told to read everything twice. Seventeen. The stored sum. The years waiting in the second line, held, unmoving, not falling in the night and not rising in the day. A number that had stopped being news and had become a weight.

Kael had not moved from the foot of the bed. He stood with both hands on the head of the stick and watched Sol's face and not the room, as if the clasp were the lesser thing in it.

Sol reached into her coat pocket. Her fingers closed on the copper clasp, the three knotted strands warm from her body. She had carried it four days against her hip and had not once taken it out in company. She drew it out now and held it on her open palm toward the bed, where the lamp could find it.

The warden's eyes tracked the movement. Her gaze settled on the copper, on the knots, and Sol watched her go back to a bridge at dusk, to a hooded shape at the far rail, to a thing that had shone when nothing else did.

"That one," Mara said.

She asked nothing. Not where it had come from. Not who had given it. Not what it meant. She looked at it as a person looks at a face she has been carrying a long time and has finally been shown.

Sol's throat moved. "She will be heard. And not yet."

Mara's mouth curved, a ghost of an expression. "I can wait. I am good at waiting." The eyes came up and held Sol's, direct and unclouded, the eyes that had looked over every recruit on the Ashworth parade ground. "I ask one thing. Tell me the day the line closes."

"I will tell you myself," Sol said. "On the day. Before the lane knows."

"Before me, no one."

"Before you, no one."

Mara closed her eyes. Her breathing slowed, the rhythm of a woman storing strength. "Then I will wait for the end of the draw."

Yelva had not moved, but the line of her shoulders eased. She reached for a chalk stub on the shelf, bent over the arm, and went carefully over the last tick at the bend, though it had not faded. It was a habit with her. She did not like a mark to look as though it might be rubbed out.

Sol put the clasp back in her pocket. It took more effort than lifting it had.

Joren pushed off the doorframe. He did not look at Kael, and he did not look at Sol. He crossed to the bed and laid his scarred hand for a moment on the blanket beside Mara's, not touching, and then he went to the door and took up his post again on the other side of it, outside, in the lane.

Tessa closed the gauge-book. She rose in one motion, chalk dust falling from her knees. "Odo has the cords laid out in the yard. He wants to know whose pulse he is listening for at the plate. He says it makes a difference to how he counts."

The room grew quiet. The lamp flame leaned. Mara's hand lay on the blanket, the fingers curled loose, the cold climbing unseen beneath the skin.

Nobody answered Tessa. Sol felt Kael shift beside her, the air between them carrying the ghost of the cliff road, the wind, the laugh, the weight of his arm through hers.

"Tell him to come to the lane," Sol said. "I will say it to him there."

Tessa nodded, once, and went out. The door let in a breath of cold and closed on it.

Yelva spoke without turning from her jars. "If nobody goes below tonight, the cold will hold where it is. I tell you so you decide with the count in front of you. Tomorrow night I may not be able to say it."

"Nobody goes below tonight," said Sol.

"Then I will sleep." Yelva lifted a jar, tilted it to the light, set it down, lifted another. She had the air of a woman who has made her peace with waiting and does not mean to be thanked for it. Sol looked at her hands, square and scrubbed, steady on the glass, and thought that every room needed one person who did not look up. She looked at Mara last, at the arm with its ladder of chalk, twenty years of walking in front of an Ashworth and the whole of it now written on her skin like a tally, and she did not let her face change.

"Rest," Sol said to Mara. "I will come when it is done."

"I know you will," said Mara, with her eyes shut.

Kael inclined his head to Mara, and Sol rose from the stool, and they went out together.

The lane received them. Night had fallen full while they sat in the back room. Stars pricked the sky, cold and sharp above the cliffs. The wind had dropped to a breath that stirred the washing on the lines strung between the tar-shop and the healer's wall. Joren stood a little way down the lane with his back to them and his face to the dark, a man keeping a gate that was not there. "Lord Ashworth," he said, without turning, and Kael answered "Joren," and that was the whole of it, and it was enough. Tessa had already gone, and a light moved in the yard where she was rousing Odo to the question.

Sol stopped on the step with her hand on the door's iron latch. She had not planned to say it here. She had planned to say it in the lane, to Odo, in the plain voice she used for tide tables, with Kael at her shoulder. But he was beside her now, in the dark, with the stick under his arm and his face turned to the stars, and she found that she could not say it to Odo first.

"The one at the plate must be me," she said. "With the rope rig, and Odo and Tessa on the cords."

Kael did not argue. He did not hesitate. He turned his head and looked at her, and in the dark his eyes held no argument at all.

"I know," he said. "I will be at the door."

The words landed light in the cold air. She had braced for them to be heavy. She had braced for the old weight, the old reach of his hand toward her sleeve, the argument he had never once failed to begin. Instead there was only the lane and the washing moving on its lines and the sound of the surf from far below, and a lightness in her chest that rose and did not settle. Deciding it aloud had made it lighter, not heavier.

She wondered whether that was a warning.

She did not know. She stood with it, in the cold, with her hand on the latch, and let herself not know, because there was nothing else to be done with the doubt but carry it. The lightness stayed. So did the doubt, beside it, a stone in clear water, visible and still.

"Does it feel lighter to you as well?" Kael said. "Having said it."

"Yes." She looked at him. "Does that frighten you?"

"It frightens me that it does not." His mouth moved, wry, in the starlight. "I came out of that room prepared to argue, and I have been standing here working out where the argument went. I find that I am practicing."

"Practicing what?"

"Being at the door."

She laughed, once, a short breath, and it went away on the wind. It was not that the thing was easy. It was that he had said it plainly, and she had heard it plainly, and neither of them had reached for the other's sleeve.

"Odo is coming," Kael said gently. "I will wait with you."

"In a moment."

She pulled the door shut. The latch clicked, a small iron sound. Through the old wood, as the catch seated, she heard Mara's voice, low and quiet, aimed at the ceiling or the vents beneath the lane or the line that ran deep and blind under the boiling house.

"Soon."
