The cliff road narrowed to a single worn track where the wind came off the water without warning. Sol felt it first in her sleeves, then in the hair across her cheeks, then in the way her breath shortened. The drop on the left fell straight to the tide line, a grey wall streaked with salt and bird lime. The right side pressed against cold rock, damp with seep. The sky had gone pink at the edges, the colour of a bruise healing.

Kael walked half a step ahead, his stick tapping a rhythm on the stone. His left leg moved clean on level ground, the numbness quiet for now. She watched the swing of his coat, the set of his shoulders, the way his head turned fractionally toward the sound of the surf below.

The road curved. The drop side switched.

She moved before he could. Two quick steps, her boot finding the outside edge, her shoulder brushing the rock face as she passed him. The wind caught her coat and flared it.

Kael stopped. His stick planted. He looked down at her boots on the dangerous side, then up at her face.

A laugh started in his chest and came out whole, rough with surprise. The first one she had heard from him since the public step. Since the Lower Reach. Since they had stood before the crowd and spoken their counts aloud.

"It is not funny," she said.

"It is the most unnecessary thing anyone has done for me on this road," he said. "And I have been on it with a surveyor."

"The road is the same width for both of us," he added, grave as a judge. "The drop does not care whose shoulder touches the wall."

"The drop does not know whose side it is," she said. "And neither do I. I would rather it learned."

"That is bad cliff law."

"I am a bad lawyer. And the road is not yours."

He shifted his weight, the stick sliding on gravel as he moved to reclaim the outside. She brought her free hand up, palm flat against his chest, fingers spread over the wool.

"No."

His hand rose, hovered at her wrist. "My leg can feel the wall. That is more than yours can feel the edge."

"That is exactly why."

He let his hand fall. He let her stay where she was.

The wind gusted again, stronger this time, whipping a strand of her hair across her mouth. She tucked it back, then reached for his hand where it hung at his side. Her fingers found his, cold knuckles, warm palm. She drew his arm through the crook of her elbow, settling his weight against her side without making it look like help.

Their hips knocked as they fell into step. He did not move away.

"The wind is trying to read our conversation," she said.

"It can have the unimportant half."

For a little way they walked in step. Not counting. Not planning. The rhythm found itself, two strides matching without effort. The pink light deepened, bleeding into purple along the horizon. Somewhere below, a seal barked, the sound carrying up the cliff face.

She said, "I want to have been on this road with you on a day when nobody was dying."

The words sat in the space between them, honest and small.

He was quiet for three breaths. Four. "That is a fair thing to want. And I will keep it."

They went quiet because the healer's lamp was in sight, a yellow glow behind a shuttered window at the back of the tar-shop lane. The road widened. The drop fell back. The wind lost its teeth.

Sol kept hold of his hand until the door, then let go at the threshold without being asked.

---

The back room smelled of dried herbs, lye soap, and the sharp mineral tang of the boiling-house vents that ran beneath the lane. Yelva stood over the narrow bed, her hands folded at her waist, her face calm in the lamplight. Tessa Rook knelt beside the bed, a gauge-book open on her knee, chalk dust on her fingers.

Mara lay propped against pillows, her skin pale as the grey dawn. Her wrists were visible where the blanket pulled back, the cold a white line climbing past the forearm, stopping at the elbow joint. The line was sharp, deliberate, as if drawn with a ruler.

Joren stood by the door, his scarred hand braced on the frame. His eyes moved from Mara to Sol to Kael and back again.

"Elbows," Yelva said. Her voice was low, pitched for the room. "Reached them last night. The climb follows the nights someone goes below. It has not missed one."

Sol nodded. She had known. The cold had been at the forearms on Day 20. The elbows on Day 27. The arithmetic was simple and cruel.

Tessa looked up from the gauge-book. "Seventeen. Fixed. Odo checked at dawn. I checked at midday. The needle has not trembled."

Seventeen. The stored sum. The years waiting in the second line. The number that had not changed since Day 7.

Sol reached into her coat pocket. Her fingers closed on the copper clasp, the three knotted strands warm from her body heat. She drew it out and held it toward Mara.

The warden's eyes tracked the movement. Her gaze settled on the copper, on the knots, on the small upright woman who had worn it for forty years.

"That one," Mara said.

She asked nothing. Not where it came from. Not who had given it. Not what it meant.

Sol's throat moved. "She will be heard. And not yet."

Mara's mouth curved, a ghost of expression. "I can wait. I am good at waiting."

Her eyes held Sol's. "Tell me the day the line closes."

Sol opened her mouth. The words formed, shaped by the wind on the cliff road, by the laugh that had come from Kael's chest, by the weight of his arm through hers.

"Day Thirty," she said. "The order runs out on Day Thirty. The formal demand is read on Day Twenty-eight. The attempt comes on Day Twenty-nine."

Mara's gaze flicked to Joren. The older warden's face did not change.

"Three days," Mara said. "Three nights."

"The draw ends when the line closes," Sol said.

Mara closed her eyes. Her breathing slowed, the rhythm of someone storing strength. "Then I will wait for the end of the draw."

Joren pushed off the doorframe. "I will walk the perimeter with Corren. The pick will be watched. The Council road will be watched."

He looked at Kael. "Lord Ashworth."

"Joren."

The warden nodded once and left, the door clicking shut behind him.

Tessa closed the gauge-book. She rose in one fluid motion, chalk dust falling from her knees. "Odo has the rig ready. Both pegs. One breath. Two pullers beyond the room's reach."

She looked at Sol. "The plate?"

"Sol sits at the plate," Kael said from the doorway. His voice was steady. "I stand at the door."

Tessa's eyes moved between them. She nodded, once, and followed Joren out.

The room shrank. The lamp flame leaned. Mara's hand lay on the blanket, the fingers curled loose, the cold climbing unseen beneath the skin.

Sol felt Kael shift beside her. The air between them carried the ghost of the cliff road, the wind, the laugh, the weight of his arm through hers.

She said, "The one at the plate must be me. With the rope rig. Odo and Tessa on the cords."

Kael did not argue. He did not hesitate. "I know. I will be at the door."

The words landed light in the heavy air. Deciding it aloud had made it lighter, not heavier. Sol felt the strange lift in her chest, the way the certainty rose instead of settling.

She wondered whether that was a warning.

Mara's voice came from the bed, quiet, directed at the ceiling.

"Soon."

Sol did not move at once. The word hung in the lamplight, a single syllable that seemed to expand until it filled the corners of the room. Mara's eyes stayed closed. Her breathing kept its slow, stored rhythm. The cold at her elbows did not pulse, did not flare. It simply was, a white line drawn by a ruler hand.

Kael's fingers brushed Sol's wrist, the lightest pressure. A question without sound.

She nodded. Once. The motion cost her something she could not name.

They moved together toward the door, their steps matched on the uneven boards. Yelva did not look up from her jars. The healer's hands moved among dried leaves and powdered chalk, sorting, measuring, the work of a woman who had made her peace with waiting.

Sol paused at the threshold. The lamp flame leaned toward her, then righted. She turned her head.

Mara had not moved. The blanket rose and fell. The white line at her elbows caught the light.

"Soon," the warden said again. Quieter this time. A word shaped for the ceiling, for the vents beneath the lane, for the line that ran deep and blind beneath the boiling house.

Sol's hand found the latch. Iron cold against her palm. She pulled the door shut. The click was final, a small sound that carried the weight of three days and seventeen years and a pick named in secret.

The lane received them. Night had fallen full while they sat in the back room. Stars pricked the sky, cold and sharp above the cliffs. The wind had dropped to a breath that stirred the washing on the lines strung between the tar-shop and the healer's wall.

Kael walked beside her, the stick tapping a slow rhythm. His left leg moved clean. The numbness had not returned.

"Three days," he said.

"Three days."

"The formal demand tomorrow. The attempt the day after. The order ends the day after that."

"The arithmetic does not change."

"No."

They passed the tar-shop. The scent of pitch and pine resin hung thick in the cold air. Ansa's window showed a rectangle of gold, the old splicer still awake, still working. A shadow moved behind the curtain — Tam, perhaps, or Ansa's boy. The tar did not stop for the calendar.

Sol's hand rose to her coat pocket. The copper clasp rested there, the three knotted strands warm against her fingers. She had not given it to Mara. She had only shown it. The steward's clasp, carried forty years, the hand that wrote the second row on every page of the seized ledger. The hand that had taught Aldous his letters so someone would know. The hand that had locked Renn in the third gallery to keep him alive.

Malrik did not know. The Council did not know. The pick, whoever they were, did not know.

Only Sol and Kael knew. And Yelva, who had seen the copper. And Mara, who had named it.

The secret sat in Sol's chest, lighter than it should have been. The warning she had felt in the back room did not fade. It settled, a stone in clear water, visible and still.

"What does the page say?" she asked, the words emerging before she decided to speak them.

Kael did not startle. His stride did not break. "The page in my pocket?"

"The one you wrote last night. The sixth page."

His thumb found the coat lining, the shape of the folded paper beneath the wool. "It says her name. The first word. The rest is blank."

"Blank."

"Waiting."

They reached the ladder well. The trapdoor stood closed. The grey ribbon of Bram's cuff was nowhere visible. The clerk had gone back to the Council road, to carry notes both ways, to watch and be watched.

Kael lifted the latch. The hinge whispered. He descended first, the stick finding the rungs, his body moving with the ease of a man who had climbed this well for twenty years. Sol followed, her boots sure on the iron.

The room over the tar-shop was cold. The lamp on the table had gone out hours ago. The gauge-book lay open on the sixth page, the nib dry beside it. The first word stood alone in Kael's square hand: *Isolde.*

Sol crossed to the table. She picked up the pen. The metal was cold. She uncapped it, set the nib to the paper beneath his name.

*Isolde,* she wrote. *Kael. Three days. The plate. The door. The pegs. The rope. Odo. Tessa. Joren. Corren. The pick. The order. The end.*

She capped the pen. The click sounded loud in the quiet room.

Kael stood by the hearth, his back to the cold grate. His arms were crossed. His face was turned toward the window, the black glass reflecting nothing.

"The wind has died," he said.

"It has."

"The road will be quieter tomorrow."

"Tomorrow is the formal demand. The Lower Reach will not be quiet."

"No. It will not."

He turned. The grey light from the window caught his face, the lines, the stubble, the eyes that held the night. He did not smile. He did not frown. His face was the neutrality he had practised, the mask that failed when she laughed.

"Come to bed," he said.

The words were not an invitation. They were a statement of fact, a recognition of the hour and the body's need and the three days that waited.

Sol set the pen down. The gauge-book closed under her hand. The cover was worn smooth at the edges, the leather soft as skin.

"In a moment."

She went to the window. The cliffs stood black against the stars. The tide line was a white blur where the water broke. Somewhere below, the second line pulsed its twelve-breath cycle, adding a breath to every round, carrying years no one had named into a gauge that read seventeen.

The pick would stand at the line when the order ran out. The pick knew the plate. The pick knew the Reach. The pick knew where the nearest body stood.

Sol's hand pressed flat against the glass. Cold bit her palm, sharp and clean.

"We make certain of where the nearest body stands," she said to the glass.

The reflection of her face stared back, pale and still. Behind her, the room held its breath. The lamp was cold. The gauge-book was closed. The pen lay capped.

Three days.

She turned. Kael had not moved. He waited, the stillness of a man who had learned to wait in rooms like this, on roads like the cliff road, in the dark beneath the boiling house.

She crossed to him. The floorboards did not creak. She stopped before him, close enough to feel the heat of him through the wool, close enough to see the grey of his eyes in the starlight.

Her hand found his coat pocket. The folded page. The promise. The fear.

"Keep it where you can reach it," she said.

"I will."

"Read it again. Before Day Twenty-eight. Before Day Twenty-nine. Before Day Thirty."

"I will."

She stepped into his arms. His hands settled on her back, wide and warm. Her forehead found his chest, the rough wool, the steady beat beneath. His chin rested on her crown. His breath moved through her hair.

The wind stirred outside. A loose shutter slapped once, twice, then stilled.

"Three days," she said into the fabric.

"Three days," he answered.

The word *Soon* echoed in the space between heartbeats. Not Mara's voice. Her own.
