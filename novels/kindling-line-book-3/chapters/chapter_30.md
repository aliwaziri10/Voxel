The lamp burned low, its flame catching the window glass and throwing it back dim. The shutters were latched against the lane. Two cups of tea sat on the table, cold and untouched, the steam long gone from them.

Kael lay on the bed in his coat, boots off, the blanket pulled smooth beneath them. Sol lay beside him, her head finding the curve of his shoulder, her hand settling flat over his heart through the wool. His hand rested between her shoulder blades, palm warm against the fabric of her undyed coat. They were dressed for leaving. The night had folded itself around the day ahead and neither of them had named it.

"Sixteen and a half," she said.

The words lay in the quiet like a stone dropped in still water.

"About thirty-four," he answered.

She lifted her head, the lamplight catching the grey of her eyes. "You still hedge."

"I am rounding toward the truth."

A breath. Then the corner of her mouth moved. "That is the first time anyone has called a hedge a direction."

"I aim to be precise."

"Precision is not a direction either."

His thumb moved once, a slow stroke against the cloth at her back. "Who says it first tomorrow night?"

The question hung. The formal demand came with morning. The attempt would follow. The order ran out on the thirtieth day. But the night after that — if there was a night after that — someone would have to speak the numbers aloud.

"Whoever is awake," she said.

"That is the unfairest rule I have heard."

"Because you are always the one awake."

"Because you are the one who says it in your sleep."

She was quiet for a beat. "You do. And it is not a number."

The admission sat between them, soft and unguarded. His pulse under her palm was steady, slower than the last time she had counted it. She knew because she had counted it, night after night, her fingers finding his wrist in the dark before sleep took her.

"Sixty-two," she said.

He turned his head toward her. "Sixty-two what?"

"Your pulse. Just now. It is steadier than yesterday."

A pause. The lamp flame leaned, recovered. "That is because you are counting."

"I will go on counting."

Her head settled back against his shoulder. His hand flattened wider across her back, the heel of his palm resting at the base of her spine. The room held the smell of cold tea and lamp oil and the mineral tang that seeped up from the vents beneath the lane. Outside, the wind had dropped to a breath that barely stirred the washing lines.

He said, "I would like the night to be longer than the order."

"The order does not get a say in how long the night is."

"No. It does not."

They lay still. The kind of stillness that comes when two bodies have learned the shape of each other's quiet. Her fingers curled once against his coat, a small unconscious motion. His breath moved through her hair. The lamp threw long shadows across the floorboards.

He started to speak — his chest expanding, the words forming — and her fingers pressed against his mouth. Two fingertips, light and final.

"Tonight is not for the plan," she said. "I have it. You have it. What is left over is for us."

His lips moved against her fingertips. The vibration of a laugh, swallowed. "I am afraid to waste the night on the plan."

"You are wasting it on being afraid of wasting it."

The pressure of her fingers eased. His mouth found the heel of her palm, a kiss that tasted of salt and tea and the mineral tang. She let her hand fall to his chest again, over his heart, feeling the rhythm she had memorized.

The window rattled once in its frame. A loose shutter somewhere down the lane slapped twice and stilled.

"Three days," he said into the dark.

"Three days."

"The formal demand tomorrow. The attempt the day after. The order ends the day after that."

"The arithmetic does not change."

"No."

She counted his pulse again, silent this time. Sixty-one. Sixty. Fifty-nine. Steady as the tide line she had watched from the cliff road.

Her eyes closed. The weight of his hand between her shoulders anchored her. The night stretched, long and unmeasured, and for the first time in weeks the counts were not a ledger entry. They were a good-night. A thing said to each other, not to the room or the line or the custodian's order.

Sleep came without ceremony. The lamp burned down. The tea grew colder.

---

Morning arrived grey and slow. The light that seeped past the shutters was thin, the colour of wet ash. Kael woke with Sol's hair against his cheek, her breathing deep and even, the rhythm of someone who had slept past the first grey hour. He did not move. The room held the shape of the night, the cold tea, the lamp wick curled black against the glass.

He eased himself up. His left leg stiffened at the hip, the numbness flaring dull and familiar before settling. He did not reach for the stick. The floor was level. The ladder well yawned dark in the corner. The gauge-book lay closed on the table, the pen capped beside it.

Sol stirred. Her eyes opened, grey in the dimness. She did not smile. She did not frown. Her face was the neutrality she wore when the day demanded it, the mask that failed only when she laughed.

"Day Twenty-eight," she said.

"Day Twenty-eight."

The words were a count. A marker. Not a conversation.

He found his boots by the bed. Pulled them on. Laced them slow. The stick leaned against the table where he had left it. He took it up, tested the weight, the ferrule sound on the boards.

Sol was already dressed. Her coat hung straight. The copper clasp rested in her pocket, the three knotted strands warm against the wool. She moved to the window, unlatched the shutters. The lane lay quiet below, the tar-shop door closed, Ansa's window dark. The cliff road was a pale line against the rock, empty of traffic.

"They will come early," she said. "The custodian. The pick. Bram with the scroll."

"The demand is written. The answer is written. The rest is witness."

She turned from the window. Her hand found his wrist, the pulse point. Held it. Three beats. Four.

"Say it first," she said quietly.

The words were not the hook. They were the promise. He nodded once. The motion cost him nothing and everything.

They climbed the ladder together. The trapdoor opened onto the lane. The air was cold, smelling of pitch and sea salt and the sharp mineral breath of the vents. Bram stood at the gallery mouth, a scroll case under his arm, his grey ribbon cuff catching the weak light. His spine was very straight. The clerk did not smile.

Behind him, two figures in Council grey. One held a scroll case identical to Bram's. The other stood silent, hands folded, face unreadable beneath the hood. The custodian. The pick.

Joren and Corren waited at the top of the steps, the warden's scarred hand braced on his hip. Ansa stood behind them, a shadow in her doorway. The households of the Lower Reach clustered back from the gallery mouth, unnamed and silent.

Kael walked forward. Sol at his shoulder. The stick tapped a slow rhythm. His leg held.

Bram unrolled the scroll. The parchment cracked in the cold. He read without preamble, his voice clear and pitched for the lane.

"By order of the Council custodian, dated the thirtieth of Cinderveil, served the sixteenth day following. The warrants of this audit require the surrender of both brass pegs to the Council's keeping. The custodian's pick shall stand at the line on the day the order runs out. The reserves shall pass at its end."

The words landed on the cold air. The pick did not move. The custodian did not speak.

Kael's square hand closed on the stick. His voice was steady, pitched for the lane and the households and the two figures in grey.

"The warrants of this audit bear two names or they bear none. The line will be closed by the hands that found it, and the years will go where the rule sends them, not where the order does."

The silence that followed was not empty. It carried the weight of seventeen years in a gauge, of a warden's cold climbing toward elbows, of a steward's copper clasp warm in a pocket.

The custodian's head inclined. A fraction. "The order runs out at the end of the thirtieth day. The pick will attend."

No threat. No argument. The two figures turned. The custodian's boots made no sound on the stone. The pick followed, a shadow in grey, the hood never turning.

Bram rolled the scroll. His face gave nothing away. He clicked the case shut.

"Joren and Corren," Kael said aloud.

The wardens moved. Joren's scarred hand rose, a signal. Corren fell in behind. The households shifted, a murmur rising and dying in the same breath.

Kael looked at Sol. Her face was calm. Her hand rested on the coat pocket, the copper clasp.

"We go to the plate tomorrow," he said. "Before the order runs out."

She nodded. Once. The motion was small, contained.

Odo Marl stepped from the shadow of the tar-shop wall. Tessa Rook at his shoulder. The tech's broad frame blocked the lane. The second tech's chalk-line caught the light at her belt.

Odo's voice was flat. "I take everything twice."

Tessa did not speak. She moved to the rig coiled against the gallery wall, her fingers finding the cords, the knots, the twin brass pegs waiting in their leather sleeves. Her touch was methodical. Unhurried. She checked each cord twice. Odo watched, his arms crossed, his eyes on her hands.

Kael felt the pride rise in his chest, clean and sharp. His answer had held. The two sentences. The lane had heard. The custodian had heard. The pick had heard.

And beneath the pride, the old doubt coiled. The fifth page in his pocket. The name written in his square hand. The rest of the page blank, waiting.

He was not sure of himself. He was sure of the answer. The two things were not the same.

Sol's hand found his wrist again. Brief. Grounding.

"Tomorrow, then," he said to the lane.

The words carried. The households heard. The wardens heard. Odo and Tessa did not look up from the rig.

Sol's voice came quiet, for him alone. "Say it first."

Kael did not answer at once. The words sat in the cold air between them, lighter than they should have been, heavier than he knew how to hold. He nodded, a single jerk of the chin, and turned toward the ladder well.

Sol fell in beside him. The stick found the rungs first. He descended into the grey light of the lane, his left leg taking the weight without complaint. The numbness had not returned. The morning air carried salt and pitch and the mineral breath that never left this place.

The households of the Lower Reach had begun to disperse. A few lingered near the tar-shop wall, hands in pockets, faces turned toward the gallery mouth as if the stone might yield another reading. Ansa's door stayed shut. The gold rectangle of her window had gone dark.

Odo and Tessa worked the rig in silence. The twin brass pegs sat in their leather sleeves, dull in the weak light. Tessa's fingers moved over each cord, over each knot, over each sleeve. Odo watched. Neither spoke. The tech's broad shoulders blocked the lane; the second tech's chalk-line caught the light at her belt.

Kael stopped at the foot of the ladder. Sol stopped with him.

"The plate tomorrow," he said. "Before the order runs out."

She nodded. "The plate tomorrow."

"Odo and Tessa on the cords. Joren and Corren on the perimeter. The pick watched."

"The pick watched."

"I will be at the door."

"I know."

The exchange was a ritual. A counting of steps before the dance. He had known the steps for weeks. He had written them on the fifth page, on the sixth page, on the scraps he burned and the ones he kept. The plan was sound. The plan was the only thing that was sound.

And yet.

His hand found the coat pocket. The folded page. The first word: *Isolde.* The rest blank. Waiting.

He had been proud of his answer. Two sentences. Clean. Final. The custodian had heard. The pick had heard. The lane had heard.

But pride was not certainty. Pride was the mask he wore over the doubt that had lived in his chest since the cliff road, since the healer's back room, since the night he had woken with his chin on his arms and the fifth page on the floor.

The doubt had a shape. It was the shape of a leg that might fail on the second stair. The shape of a breath that might come too early or too late. The shape of a rope rig that might snag, a peg that might stick, a puller who might slip.

The shape of a night that might not come.

"Kael."

Sol's voice. Quiet. Grounded.

He looked at her. The grey light caught the line of her jaw, the grey of her eyes, the faint crease between her brows that appeared only when she was watching him watch the dark.

"The plan holds," she said.

"It does."

"The rig holds."

"It does."

"Odo takes everything twice."

"Everything twice."

"Then the rest is not yours to carry alone."

The words were simple. The kind that slipped past the defences he had built over twenty years of wardenship, of silence, of the weight that settled in the space between heartbeats.

His hand left his pocket. Found hers. Cold fingers, warm palm. The familiar anchor.

"I know," he said.

She held his gaze. "Say it again."

"The plan holds. The rig holds. Odo takes everything twice. I will be at the door. You will be at the plate. The line will close. The years will go where the rule sends them."

"And the night?"

His thumb moved over her knuckles. "The night is ours. Whatever length it has."

A ghost of a smile. "That is the best answer you have given today."

"It is the only one that is true."

They stood in the lane, two figures in wool and leather, the stick between them, the rig behind them, the gallery mouth yawning dark above. The wind stirred a scrap of paper against the tar-shop wall. Somewhere above, a gull cried.

"Come," she said. "The room is cold. The tea is cold. The bed is warm."

"An argument I cannot refute."

"You never could."

They climbed the ladder. The trapdoor closed behind them with a thud that sealed the morning out.

The room over the tar-shop was exactly as they had left it. The gauge-book closed on the table. The pen capped. The two cups of tea, cold and untouched, a crust of film on the surface. The lamp wick curled black against the glass.

Kael set the stick against the wall. Unlaced his boots. Pulled them off. The boards were cold beneath his socks.

Sol moved to the hearth. She did not light the lamp. She did not make tea. She pulled the blanket back on the bed, the motion practised, efficient, the motion of a woman who had made this bed alone for years and now made it for two.

He watched her. The way her shoulders moved under the undyed coat. The way her hair fell loose down her back, pale in the dimness. The way she did not look at him, because she did not need to.

She turned. Held out a hand.

He crossed the two strides. Took it. Let her pull him down onto the mattress.

The wool was warm from her body. The blanket rough against his cheek. Her hand found his chest, over his heart, the familiar anchor. His hand settled between her shoulder blades.

The room held its breath. The vents beneath the lane whispered their mineral song. The tide line breathed against the cliffs, a sound too low to hear but present in the bones.

"Sixty," she said.

He did not ask. He knew. Her fingers counting his pulse. The rhythm she had memorized. The rhythm that had steadied since the cliff road, since the laugh, since the night they had stopped counting and started living.

"Yesterday it was sixty-two," he said.

"Yesterday was not today."

"Tomorrow it will be different again."

"Tomorrow we will not be here to count it."

The words landed. The attempt. The plate. The door. The pegs. The rope. Odo. Tessa. Joren. Corren. The pick. The order. The end.

He did not flinch. He had written the words himself. He had spoken them to the lane. He would speak them again to the dark.

"We will be here," he said. "We will be at the plate. We will be at the door. We will close the line."

"And after?"

The question he had not let himself ask. The question the fifth page waited for. The question the blank lines waited for.

"After," he said, "we will count the night. However long it is."

She was quiet. Her fingers curled once against his coat. "That is the second best answer you have given today."

He smiled. The mask cracked. The doubt recoiled. "I aim to improve."

"See that you do."

Her head settled on his shoulder. Her breath warmed the wool. His hand flattened across her back, the heel of his palm at the base of her spine. The familiar shape. The learned quiet.

The lamp stayed dark. The tea stayed cold. The gauge-book stayed closed.

Outside, the lane emptied. The gulls fell silent. The tide turned. The wind dropped to nothing.

In the room over the tar-shop, two bodies breathed in rhythm. Sixteen and a half. About thirty-four. Seventeen stored. Three days.

The arithmetic did not change.

But the night was longer than the order.

And the order did not get a say.

---

The afternoon passed in the quiet work of waiting. Kael checked the rig with Odo and Tessa, his fingers running over the cords, the knots, the twin brass pegs in their sleeves. He walked the perimeter with Joren and Corren, his stick tapping a slow rhythm, his leg holding. He stood at the gallery mouth and looked down the second stair, into the dark where the plate waited in the bare iron door.

Sol sat at the table with the gauge-book open, the pen moving across the sixth page. *Isolde. Kael. Three days. The plate. The door. The pegs. The rope. Odo. Tessa. Joren. Corren. The pick. The order. The end.* She wrote each word on its own line. A list. A litany. A prayer without gods.

Yelva brought broth in a wooden bowl. Mara slept in the back room, the white line at her elbows catching the lamplight. The draw continued. The gauge read seventeen. Fixed.

Bram did not return. The Council road stayed quiet. The custodian and the pick did not reappear.

As the light faded, Kael returned to the room. Sol had capped the pen. The gauge-book lay closed. The broth bowl sat empty on the table.

She looked up. Her face was calm. The neutrality that failed only when she laughed.

"Day Twenty-eight," she said.

"Day Twenty-eight."

"The formal demand is read. The pick is warned. The rig is checked."

"Everything twice."

"Everything twice."

She rose. Crossed to him. Her hand found his wrist. Three beats. Four.

"Tomorrow, then," she said.

The words were not the hook. They were the count. The marker. The promise.

He nodded. "Tomorrow, then."

Her voice came quiet, for him alone. "Say it first."

He did not answer. He did not need to. The night stretched before them, long and unmeasured, and the counts were a good-night. A thing said to each other. Not to the room. Not to the line. Not to the custodian's order.

Sleep came without ceremony. The lamp burned down. The broth grew cold.

Morning would come. The plate would wait. The door would open. The pegs would pull. The line would close.

But tonight, the night was theirs.

And the order did not get a say.
