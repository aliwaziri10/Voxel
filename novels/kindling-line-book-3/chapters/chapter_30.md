The lamp had burned down to a bead of yellow, and neither of them had risen to trim it. The shutters were latched against the lane. Two cups of tea sat on the table, cold and untouched, a skin of film across each.

Kael lay on the bed in his coat, boots off, the blanket pulled smooth beneath him. Sol lay along his side, her head finding the curve of his shoulder, her hand settling flat over his heart through the wool. His own hand rested between her shoulder blades, palm warm against the undyed cloth of her coat. They had not undressed. Neither of them had said why. The night had folded itself around the day ahead and neither of them had named it.

"Sixteen and a half," she said.

The words lay in the quiet like a stone dropped in still water.

"About thirty-four," he answered.

She lifted her head, the lamplight catching the grey of her eyes. "You still hedge."

"I am rounding toward the truth."

A breath. Then the corner of her mouth moved. "That is the first time anyone has called a hedge a direction."

"I aim to be precise."

"Precision is not a direction either."

His thumb moved once, a slow stroke against the cloth at her back. "Who says it first, after?"

The question hung. The morning would bring the Council's demand. The order ran out at the end of the thirtieth day. But the night after that, if there was a night after that, one of them would have to speak the numbers aloud.

"Whoever is awake," she said.

"That is the unfairest rule I have heard."

"Because you are always the one awake."

"Because you are the one who says it in your sleep."

She was quiet for a beat. "You do. And it is not a number."

The admission sat between them, soft and unguarded. His pulse under her palm was steady, slower than the last time she had counted it. She knew because she had counted it, night after night, her fingers finding his wrist in the dark before sleep took her.

"Sixty-two," she said.

He turned his head toward her. "Sixty-two what?"

"Your pulse. Just now. It is steadier than yesterday."

A pause. The lamp flame leaned, recovered. "That is because you are counting."

"I will go on counting."

Her head settled back against his shoulder. His hand flattened wider across her back, the heel of his palm resting at the base of her spine. The room held the smell of cold tea and lamp oil and the mineral tang that seeped up from the vents beneath the lane. Outside, the wind had dropped to a breath that barely stirred the washing lines.

He said, "I would like the night to be longer than the order."

"The order does not get a say in how long the night is."

"No. It does not."

They lay still, in the stillness that comes when two bodies have learned the shape of each other's quiet. Her fingers curled once against his coat, a small unconscious motion. His breath moved through her hair. The lamp threw long shadows across the floorboards.

He started to speak, his chest expanding, the words forming, and her fingers pressed against his mouth. Two fingertips, light and final.

"Tonight is not for the plan," she said. "I have it. You have it. What is left over is for us."

His lips moved against her fingertips. The vibration of a laugh, swallowed. "I am afraid to waste the night on the plan."

"You are wasting it on being afraid of wasting it."

The pressure of her fingers eased. His mouth found the heel of her palm, a kiss that tasted of salt and tea. She let her hand fall to his chest again, over his heart, feeling the rhythm she had memorized.

The window rattled once in its frame. A loose shutter down the lane slapped twice and stilled.

"Sixty-one," she murmured. Then, a while later, so low he felt it more than heard it: "Sixty."

"Good night, Sol."

"Good night."

It was not a ledger entry. For the first time in weeks the counts were not a tally set against the line or the custodian's order. They were what another couple might say before sleep, a thing given from one to the other and to no one else. Sleep came without ceremony. The lamp burned down to nothing. The tea grew colder.

---

Morning arrived grey and slow. The light that seeped past the shutters was thin, the color of wet ash. Kael woke with Sol's hair against his cheek, her breathing deep and even. He did not move for a long moment. The room held the shape of the night, the cold tea, the lamp wick curled black against the glass.

He eased himself up. His left leg stiffened at the hip, the numbness flaring dull and familiar before settling. He did not reach for the stick. The floor was level, and a level floor was a thing the leg could manage by itself. The stick would be for the stair.

Sol stirred. Her eyes opened, grey in the dimness. She did not smile. She did not frown. Her face took on the neutrality she wore when the day demanded it, the mask that failed only when she laughed.

"Sixty," he said, before she could.

"I had it first."

"You were asleep."

"I count in my sleep." She sat up and found her boots. "They will come early. The custodian. Bram with his scroll case. They will want the lane awake to see it."

"The demand is written. The answer is written. The rest is witness."

She stood. Her hand found his wrist, the pulse point, and held it. Three beats. Four. She did not say the number aloud. She let go.

They went down the stair together, Kael taking the treads with the stick and the rail both, the left foot always arriving a breath after the right. The tar shop was cold and smelled of last night's pitch. Ansa's shutters were open, and she was gone from behind them, which meant she had already taken her place at the head of the steps.

The lane was grey and quiet. Beyond the last of the roofs the ropewalk stood as it had stood since the fire, a row of blackened posts with stubs of rope still hanging from the crossbars, swinging a little in the air off the water. The gallery mouth opened beneath it, a low arch of stone with a flight of worn steps leading down to its floor. The households of the Lower Reach had come out and stood back along the walls, a dozen or more, wrapped in their morning coats. Nobody spoke. A child was lifted onto a hip and told, in a whisper, to be still.

Ansa stood at the top of the steps with her arms folded and her sleeves rolled, flour grey on her forearms, looking at nothing. Joren stood a pace below her, the scar on the back of his hand white where it rested on his belt. Corren held the foot of the flight.

At the bottom, under the arch, three figures waited.

Bram stood to the left, his spine very straight, a leather scroll case under his arm and the grey ribbon at his cuff stirring in the draft from the gallery. He did not smile. He did not look at Sol. Beside him stood an official in Council grey, a scroll case of his own in his hand, a role and not a man; Kael had never learned the custodian's name and saw no sign that he was meant to. Behind the custodian, a little apart, stood a second figure in the same grey. It said nothing. It folded its hands and waited. Kael looked at it once and found that his eyes would not stay; there was nothing to hold them, no face turned toward him, only the grey, and he understood that this was by design.

Kael descended. The stick tapped each tread, ferrule finding the worn center of the stone. Sol came behind him, one step above and to the left, where the drop was. At the foot he stopped and leaned the stick against the wall, because the ground was level and he had decided on the stair that he would answer this standing on his own leg. The leg held. The needles in the hip did not rise.

The gallery smelled of cold ash and wet stone. Far back in the dark a drip kept time.

Bram unrolled the scroll. The parchment cracked in the cold. He read without preamble, in the voice of a clerk who had been told to remove himself from the words, clear and pitched for the lane above.

"The Council's custodian demands the surrender of both brass pegs into the Council's keeping. The custodian's pick shall stand at the line on the day the order runs out. At the order's end, the reserves shall pass to the Council."

He lowered the scroll. The words went up the steps and out into the lane and the lane took them in.

The grey figure behind the custodian did not move. Neither did the custodian. They had made no effort to look at the households, and that was its own address.

Kael felt the lane waiting. It pressed at his back like a hand, a dozen quiet households and Ansa's folded arms and the warden's scar. He thought, briefly and without comfort, of the fifth page folded in his coat. When he wanted to step in, he would say so first. He did not know yet whether he would be able to keep the promise he had put on paper, and now, with the whole lane at his back, he would not let that doubt touch his voice.

"The warrants of this audit bear two names or they bear none." He said it to the custodian, level, without raising it. "The line will be closed by the hands that found it, and the years will go where the rule sends them, not where the order does."

He stopped. Two sentences. He had written them in the margin of the third page and rehearsed them once in the dark, and they came out whole.

The silence that followed was not empty. It carried the gauge that did not fall, the cold climbing a warden's arm, the clasp warm in a pocket he was careful not to look at. Sol did not move beside him. He felt her not move.

The custodian's head inclined, a fraction. The voice was dry, without color, a voice used to reading aloud. "The order runs out at the end of the thirtieth day. The pick will attend."

No threat followed. No argument. The custodian turned, and the boots made no sound on the stone. The grey figure fell in behind, the hands still folded, and the two of them went up the steps past Corren and past the warden and past Ansa, who unfolded her arms to let them by and folded them again after. The households drew back along the walls to make a path. Nobody touched them. Nobody said a word. When the grey had passed out of the lane the murmur began, and it rose and died in the same breath.

Bram rolled the scroll. His face gave nothing away, but his thumb, which had been black with ink to the first knuckle the week before, was clean. He clicked the case shut and looked, once, at Kael. Then he went up after the Council.

Kael took up the stick from the wall.

"Joren and Corren," he said aloud, where the whole flight could hear.

Joren's scarred hand rose from his belt, a signal, and Corren fell in behind him. The households shifted. A woman on the far wall let out a breath she had been holding and tried to cover it with a cough. Kael climbed the steps with the stick and the left leg and the rail, and at the top Ansa looked at him and then at the sky and then at the lane, and said, to the lane, "Well. They have read it."

"They have read it," Kael said.

"Eat before you do anything foolish." She went in. It was the longest speech he had had from her all week.

Sol came up beside him. She said nothing about the answer. Her hand rested on her coat pocket a moment, over the clasp, and then dropped to her side. He knew she had heard the whole of it, and that she would keep her opinion until the afternoon, when it would cost her nothing to give it.

The pride came up in him then, clean and sharp, and he let it. The two sentences had held. The lane had heard them. The custodian had heard them and had not answered them, which was worse than an answer and better than a threat. He walked toward the yard with the pride bright in his chest and the old doubt coiled beneath it, small and patient, and told himself the two had nothing to do with each other.

They did. He knew they did. A man could be proud of what he said and unsure of what he would do, and the day ahead was built on the gap between them.

---

The yard behind the tar shop was a bare square of beaten earth and tarred stone, walled on three sides, with a board propped against the far wall and a cart-shed along the fourth. Odo Marl and Tessa Rook had set up the rig on the packed ground, as they had done every afternoon since the trial: two cords from a board with a lamp and a sandbag, through the frame of a door, the leather sleeves laid out on a trestle beside them.

Odo had his arms crossed. Tessa knelt at the first cord with her chalk-line at her belt and her thumb moving along the hemp a hand's breadth at a time, feeling for the fray that her eyes would miss.

"Cord one," she said. She worked from the board to the sleeve and back. "Sound. Cord two." She went through it again, slower, her lips moving over the count of the strands. She did not rush it, and she did not look up to see whether anyone was watching. When she reached the end she began at the beginning.

"Twice," Odo said, to nobody.

"Twice," Tessa agreed.

"I take everything twice." He said it flatly, as another man might have said that he took his tea with milk. "The first time to see what is there. The second time to see what I decided to see the first time."

"He did Renn's sheet three times," Tessa said, her eyes still on the hemp. "Then he asked me for a fourth, because he did not trust his three."

"Twice is the rule," Odo said. "The rest is temperament."

Kael stood at the edge of the beaten ground and watched them. The leg was holding. His hand was steady on the stick. He found that he did not want to interrupt. There was a rhythm to the checking that was almost a comfort, a pair of people going slowly through a thing so that it would not have to be gone through quickly later.

Tessa passed the second cord through her fingers, the sleeve, the knot at the board. She tested each knot with a short sharp tug and listened, as if the hemp might speak. When she was done she stood and wiped her palms on her trousers and said, "Both sound. Both twice. The board is where it will bite, if it bites."

"Then they are sound," Odo said.

Joren had come into the yard behind Kael with the quiet of a man who had been walking quietly for thirty years. Sol came after him. The wardens had finished the lane, and the households had gone in. Kael turned to face them both, and did it before Sol could ask, because he knew the shape of what she would ask and because a thing said first was a different thing from a thing said when pressed.

"I will be at the door," he said. "Whatever happens at the plate, I will be at the door of the passage. If I want to step in, I will say so aloud first, and I will wait for her answer." He looked at Sol, not at Joren. "Not after. First."

Sol's face did not change. But her hand, which had been resting at her side, opened and closed once.

"Joren," Kael said, "you hold the foot of the stair. You are the one who will hear it. If I say it and she says no and I come regardless, you will be at the foot, and I would ask you to say my name."

Joren looked at him for a long moment. The scar on the back of his hand stood out white. "Say it as an order," he said.

"As a request."

"A request I can disobey." The warden's mouth moved at one corner. "An order I will keep." He considered. "I will say your name, my lord. Loudly. And I will stand where you would have to go through me."

"That is all I ask."

"It is not," Joren said. "But it will do."

Sol let out a breath that was not quite a laugh. She came forward and stood beside Kael, close enough that her shoulder touched his, and spoke to the yard in general, to the cords and the board and the two techs who had both found a place to look that was not the three of them.

"We go to the plate tomorrow," she said. "Before the order runs out. All of us at our places."

"At our places," Odo said.

"Everything twice," said Tessa.

"Everything twice," Kael agreed.

Afterward he stayed on in the yard longer than he needed to, watching the light climb the wall. Sol sat on the edge of the trestle with a cup of Ansa's broth between her hands, not drinking it, watching Tessa lay the sleeves out in their order. The gauge hut stood quiet across the way. The needle in it, he knew without looking, read seventeen and had not moved since the day before. It would not move tonight.

Sol drank, finally, and made a face at the cold broth and drank the rest anyway. "Two sentences," she said.

"Two."

"You were proud of them."

"I was." He did not pretend otherwise. "I am."

"Good. Be proud of them tonight." She set the cup down on the trestle beside the sleeves. "Be afraid tomorrow. Tomorrow, and not before it, or you will have spent the fear before the day that needs it."

"That is the most practical thing anyone has said to me this week."

"I am a practical woman." Her mouth tilted. "Ask Ansa."

---

Evening came on the color of old iron. The lane lamps were lit, one in three, as the Reach lit them in lean weeks. Shutters closed along the walls. Yelva crossed the top of the lane with her apron pocket weighted by chalk and a lamp in her fist, on her way to the back room to mark the night's tick on a warden's arm, and did not look up as she passed. Behind one door a child was being put to bed, and a woman's voice, low and patient, went on and on.

Kael stood in the tar-shop doorway with the stick in his hand and looked down the lane toward the gallery mouth. The burned posts of the ropewalk stood black against a sky that had not yet decided to be dark. The rope stubs swung. The steps, empty now, led down into a shadow that was only a shadow.

He thought about the fifth page. He did not take it out. He knew what it said, and he knew the one thing it did not say, which was whether he would be able to do what it promised. A sentence on a page was a sentence. A man standing at a door with a woman's life on the other side of it, with her voice stopped, with the line breathing in the dark, was a different creature, and that creature had never kept a promise made by the first one. That was what he had to ask of himself tomorrow, and there was no custodian or rule or order that would do the asking in his place.

He was proud of what he had said that morning. He was not sure of himself. He had learned in these weeks that the two could be true at once, and that a man could live between them for a very long time.

Behind him the stair creaked. A light step, a hand on the rail. Sol came to stand at his shoulder in the doorway, her undyed coat darker in the dusk, her hair loose. She did not touch him, though her shoulder pressed his, a small steady weight that said what neither of them would say aloud in a lane that was listening. She looked where he looked, down the lane, at the steps and the posts and the swinging stubs.

The lane was listening. He could feel it, the households behind their shutters, Ansa behind hers, Joren at the foot of the flight with his hand on his belt, the gulls gone quiet on the roofs. He lifted his voice, not much, only enough to carry to the end of the lane and the arch and the dark below it.

"Tomorrow, then," he said.

The words went out and did not come back. Down the lane a shutter opened a hand's breadth and closed.

Sol's voice came quiet, for him alone. "Say it first."

He did not answer at once. The words sat in the cold air between them, lighter than they should have been, heavier than he knew how to hold. Then he nodded, once, and the two of them went in and shut the door.
