Grey light slipped through the window of the room over the tar shop. The tea in the tin cups had gone cold. Steam no longer rose between them.

Kael stood beside Sol at the window, his shoulder nearly touching hers. The yard below held the slow motion of morning. Odo's broad shape moved at the gauge hut. Tessa's chalk-line snapped, a sharp sound in the quiet air.

Sol did not turn. Her fingers curled around the rim of her cup. "Two steps."

Kael frowned. "What?"

"Two steps. Move."

He stepped back. One pace. Two. The floorboards creaked under his boots.

"More."

He stepped again. The heel of his boot struck the threshold of the stair doorway.

"There."

He glanced over his shoulder. The stairwell yawned behind him, dark and steep. "I am now standing in the doorway to the stair."

"That is exactly the distance."

He turned back to her. The grey light caught the line of her jaw, the faint crease between her brows. "I am allowed to stand near you."

Her gaze stayed on the yard. "When I am burning, you are not."

"Nobody is burning."

"That is today."

"A sneaky way to win an argument."

She glanced at him then, the corner of her mouth lifting. "I have been practising."

The laugh started in his chest, low and surprised. She answered it, a quieter sound, and for a moment the room held only that. Then the silence settled back between them, heavier than before, shaped like the things they did not say.

Sol crossed the two steps between them. Her palm pressed flat against his chest, over the wool of his coat, over the bone and the muscle and the heart beating its steady count. She held it there, fingers spread, feeling. Then she withdrew her hand, the movement deliberate, unhurried.

"That was nearer than two steps," Kael said.

"I was not burning."

The words hung in the cold tea and the grey light.

She looked up at him. Her eyes were the color of the sky outside, pale and depthless. "Say the rule aloud. Just once. Like a prayer."

Kael's jaw tightened. The stick leaned against the wall by the door, forgotten. His left leg held its weight without complaint. He drew a breath.

"When you are burning, I stand beyond the reach of the room. The nearest living body pays. I will not be that body."

The words sounded ridiculous in the small room, with the cold tea and the grey light and the yard waking below. They sounded true.

Sol's hand found his sleeve, fingers closing on the rough cloth. "It sounds like a vow."

"It is a rule."

"Vows break. Rules hold."

"Rules hold until they do not."

She released his sleeve. Her hand fell to her side. "Then we keep this one."

Before he could answer, a knock came at the door. Three raps, measured, the rhythm of a clerk who had been taught to announce himself before entering.

Kael moved to the door. The latch lifted. The door swung inward.

Bram stood on the landing. The young Council clerk's spine was very straight. The grey ribbon at his cuff caught the light. He held a folded paper in his hand, the edges crisp, the seal unbroken.

"Warden Ashworth," Bram said. His voice was level, pitched for the room and no further. "The Council custodian's order. Served this morning. Day Sixteen."

Kael took the paper. The wax was the color of old bone. The impression showed the scales of the Council, not the seal of any house.

He broke the seal. Unfolded the sheet. The ink was black, the hand precise, each letter formed with care.

Sol moved to his shoulder. Her warmth radiated through the wool. She read over him, her breath a faint stir against his ear.

Kael's eyes found the date first. Day Zero. The night of the petition. Thirty days from its date.

He read the line aloud. "Dated Day Zero. Thirty days from its date. Served Day Sixteen."

The words landed in the room. The grey light did not change. The yard below did not pause.

"The Council spent sixteen of the thirty days before telling me," Kael said. The statement carried no heat. It was a fact, placed on the table like a tally stone.

Sol's hand settled on his arm, fingers light. "That is the plain tactic."

The order claimed the reserves. It appointed a custodian. It stated the custodian would name a body to stand at the line on Day Twenty-Six.

Kael turned the paper over. The back was blank. He looked at Bram. The clerk's face gave nothing away. The grey ribbon at his cuff did not move.

"Sign here," Bram said, indicating a line at the bottom of the page. "Receipt of service."

Kael took the pen Bram offered. The nib scratched. He signed. Kael Ashworth. Day Sixteen.

He handed the pen back. The paper disappeared into Bram's satchel.

"The thirty days run out on Day Thirty," Sol said. Her voice was quiet. "Fourteen days from now."

"Fourteen," Kael agreed.

Bram inclined his head. "The pick will be named on Day Twenty-Six. You will be informed." He turned. His boots struck the stair. The sound faded downward.

The door swung shut. The latch caught.

Kael stood with the memory of the order in his fingers, the date burned into his sight. Day Zero. Day Sixteen. Day Twenty-Six. Day Thirty.

Sol did not move from his side. Her hand remained on his arm. The tea cups sat cold on the table. The grey light deepened toward something harder, colder, as the morning advanced.

"Someone else standing nearest the line would take the stored years," she said. Not a question.

"The custodian's pick."

"The pick stands at the plate. The pegs are pulled. The stored sum goes to the nearest living body."

"Same rule as cost. Other direction."

She nodded once. Her fingers tightened on his arm. "What do you think?"

The question caught him off balance. He had expected her to speak first, to lay out the shape of the problem, to trace the lines of force and counterforce. She had asked him instead.

He looked at her face. The pale eyes. The crease between her brows. The copper clasp at her throat.

"I think the Council knew exactly what they were doing when they dated it Day Zero and held it sixteen days," he said. "I think Malrik conceded the claim on Day Ten knowing this order existed. I think the pick has already been chosen, and the name is waiting in a sealed envelope on a desk in the Lower Spine."

"And the stored years?"

"Seventeen. The gauge reads seventeen. If the pick stands at the plate when the pegs are pulled, seventeen years go to the pick. If I stand at the plate, seventeen years come to me. If you stand at the plate —"

"I will not stand at the plate."

The words were flat. Final. They admitted no argument.

Kael turned toward her. The space between them held the two steps, the rule, the prayer he had spoken. "Sol."

"The plate is mine. The burn is mine. The cost is mine."

"The cost at the plate is not the stored sum. The cost at the plate is the line's price for closing. Half a year. One year. The nearest living body pays that price. The stored sum goes to the nearest living body after."

"I know the mechanics."

"Then you know the pick must not be the nearest living body."

The grey light filled the room. The cold tea. The order folded in his pocket. The count: sixteen and a half for her. Thirty-four for him. Seventeen stored. The gap seventeen and a half.

He had not finished the addition. He would not finish it.

The words settled between them, heavier than the order in his pocket. Kael's gaze dropped to her hand on his arm, the fingers pale against the wool. The copper clasp at her throat caught the grey light, a small bright thing in the dim room.

"You asked what I think," he said. "I think we have fourteen days to learn a room we have not seen, a rope rig we have not pulled, twin pegs that have never been tested together. I think the pick will be named on Day Twenty-Six, and that name will decide whether the stored years go to a stranger or to me."

"And if the name is yours?"

The question was calm. No tremor. No plea.

Kael met her eyes. "Then I stand at the plate. The pegs come free. The stored sum comes to me. The line's price for closing — half a year, one year, whatever it demands — falls on the nearest living body. That body will not be you."

"Because you will stand closer."

"Because I will make certain of it."

Her hand slid from his arm. The loss of contact was a small cold thing. She turned toward the window. The yard below had woken fully. Odo's chalk moved on the slate. Tessa's line snapped again, sharp in the morning air.

"The plate is at the foot of the second stair," she said. "Behind the iron door. The chamber beyond the boiling-house cellar. Renn's chalk on the second stair read: THIS ONE HAS NO BOTTOM I HAVE FOUND."

Kael crossed to the table. His tea cup was cold. He lifted it anyway, drank the dregs, set it down. "Renn's map will show where the pegs sit. Ulla has his survey sheet. One cellar marked warm."

"Renn is not here."

"He is alive. That is more than we had ten days ago."

"Ten days ago he was locked in the third gallery. The steward locked him there. The draw took three years from him while he could not move."

"The steward did not know the line would draw on him."

"How do you know what she knew?"

Kael paused. The stick leaned by the door. His left leg ached, a dull deep throb below the hip that had nothing to do with the climb and everything to do with the cold that lived in the bone. He had learned to walk inside it. He had not learned to speak inside it.

"Because she taught him his letters," he said. "The forged witness hand on the petition — upright, closed tail, careful to look younger. Renn's hand resembles it because she taught them both. She carried the second hand on the leaf for forty years. She wrote Aldous's name herself. She wanted to be found, but not by the Council. Not by Thorne."

Sol's back stayed to the window. Her shoulders were rigid. "She locked Renn in the dark to keep him away from the pegs. The line drew on him anyway. Three years. The gauge climbed from fourteen to seventeen."

"Three years from one man in eight days." Kael's voice was flat. "The draw does not stop on its own. Odo said so. The gauge reads seventeen now. It will read seventeen on Day Twenty-Eight unless someone goes below again."

"Or unless the line closes."

"Or unless the line closes."

She turned. Her face was pale in the grey light. The crease between her brows had deepened. "The formal demand at the Lower Reach is Day Twenty-Eight. The first attempt to close is Day Twenty-Nine. The order runs out Day Thirty."

"Thirteen days from the formal demand to the end of the order. One attempt to close before the Council can claim the custodian's pick failed."

"If the attempt fails."

"It will not fail."

"How do you know?"

Kael set his jaw. "Because the rope rig is set. The pegs are ready. Odo and Tessa have measured every cycle. The draw holds steady at eleven breaths, twelve with the east pulse. Mara's cold counts as a body at the east door. The gauge does not lie."

"And if the pick is named on Day Twenty-Six and stands at the plate on Day Twenty-Nine?"

"Then the pick takes the stored years. The line's price falls on the nearest living body after. That body will be me. I will stand closer."

"And if the pick is not named? If the custodian delays?"

"The order says the custodian will name a body on Day Twenty-Six. The order is law."

"Law bends. Malrik conceded the claim on Day Ten. The Council held this order sixteen days before serving it. Law bends when power presses it."

Kael said nothing. The room held the silence, the cold tea, the grey light thinning toward afternoon.

Sol moved to the table. Her fingers traced the ring his cup had left on the wood. "The steward asked to be given to no one until the line closes. She said it in ch.27 — wait. That is not yet. That is the scene that comes later."

She stopped. Her hand froze on the table.

Kael watched her. The moment stretched. The yard sounds drifted up: chalk on slate, the snap of a line, the low hiss of steam.

"What is it?" he asked.

Her head lifted. Her eyes were clear, the pale grey of the sky. "Nothing. A thought that arrived out of order. The steward's scene is not yet. She has not spoken her piece. We do not know what she will say."

"Then we wait for it."

"And in the meantime?"

"In the meantime we learn the room. We learn the rope rig. We learn the pegs. We measure the draw every cycle, every hour. Odo and Tessa will chart it. If the draw shifts, if the cold shifts, we know before the formal demand."

"Thirteen days."

"Thirteen days."

She nodded. The tension in her shoulders eased, a fraction. "Yelva will complain about the mud. Ansa's broth will be waiting. Mara will ask for her boots and a bridge to walk."

"Mara is awake. She is cross. She is asking."

"More than we had ten days ago."

The words echoed his own from the night before. Kael's mouth curved, a small thing, not quite a smile. "You remember."

"I remember everything."

The door knocked again. One rap this time. Softer.

Kael moved to it. The latch lifted. The door swung inward.

Tessa Rook stood on the landing. Her dark hair was pinned back with the gear-tooth clip. Chalk dust streaked her trousers. She held a slate in one hand, a stub of chalk in the other.

"Warden Ashworth. Lady Vane." Her voice was quick, efficient. "The gauge held at eleven. The east pulse added the twelfth. The draw is steady. Mara's log matches — one hour nightly, the cold at the forearms, climbing on nights after someone goes below. We charted it. Every cycle. Every hour."

She extended the slate. Kael took it. The chalk marks were precise, small, crowded. Columns of numbers. Eleven. Twelve. Eleven. Twelve. A notation in the margin: *Mara's cold counts as body at east door. Draw steady.*

"Good work," he said.

Tessa's chin lifted. "Odo says the rope rig is set. The ledge peg and the table peg — same mold, placed. Two pullers beyond the room's reach. You and me, or Odo and me. The plate is warm. Black iron. No keyhole. At the foot of the second stair."

She recited it like a lesson learned. Kael's gaze moved over the numbers, the notation, the small neat hand.

"Thank you, Tessa."

She inclined her head. "Thirteen days to the formal demand. The pick named on Day Twenty-Six. We will be ready." She turned. Her boots struck the stair. The sound faded.

The door swung shut. The latch caught.

Kael stood with the slate in his hand. The chalk dust smelled of hot metal and oil. The numbers blurred at the edges.

Sol came to his side. Her hand found his wrist, fingers warm on the pulse. She read the slate over his arm, her breath a faint stir against his sleeve.

"Eleven. Twelve. Holding." Her voice was quiet. "The draw does not stop on its own. But we know the rhythm now. We know the cold climbs on borrowed nights. We know the gauge reads seventeen and the stored sum is seventeen and the gap is seventeen and a half."

She said the last number aloud. The one he would not finish.

Kael's jaw tightened. The slate was warm in his hand, heated by the chalk and the room and the line running beneath them. "The gap is not the point. The point is the pick. Day Twenty-Six. The name in the sealed envelope."

"Or the envelope empty."

"Or the envelope empty."

He set the slate on the table, beside the cold tea cups. The grey light had deepened, the shadows lengthening across the floorboards. The stick by the door caught the last of it, the wood pale against the dark.

"Come," he said. "We have a room to learn. A rope rig to test. Pegs to measure."

She did not move. Her hand stayed on his wrist. The pulse beat against her fingers, steady, patient.

"Kael."

He looked at her. The pale eyes. The copper clasp. The crease between her brows that deepened when she was thinking, when she was angry, when she was afraid.

"Say it again," she said. "The rule. Like a prayer."

The request caught him off balance. The room held the cold tea, the slate, the order folded in his pocket, the count he would not finish. Fourteen days. Thirteen days. The pick named on Day Twenty-Six.

He drew a breath. The words came easier the second time.

"When you are burning, I stand beyond the reach of the room. The nearest living body pays. I will not be that body."

Sol's fingers tightened on his wrist. The pressure was a small anchor. A small vow.

"Good," she said. "Again tomorrow."

"Every day."

"Every day until the line closes."

He turned his hand, caught her fingers in his. Large, warm, callused. Her hand was smaller, cooler, the copper clasp brushing his knuckles.

"Every day," he agreed.

The grey light faded behind them as they moved toward the door. The stick waited by the jamb. Kael took it up. His left leg took the weight. The ache was there, the cold weight in the bone, but the knee held. The ankle held.

Sol opened the door. The stairwell yawned, dark and steep. The sounds of the yard rose up: chalk on slate, the snap of a line, the low heartbeat of the line measured in breaths.

They descended together. Two steps. Three. The rule held between them, spoken aloud, a prayer and a promise.

The pick would be named on Day Twenty-Six. Fourteen days from now. Thirteen from the formal demand.

Kael did not count the days again. He counted the steps. One. Two. Three. The rhythm steady. The left foot finding its place without hesitation.

Behind him, Sol made no sound. He could hear her not reaching. The absence of her hand on his elbow was louder than any touch would have been.

He felt it in the space between his shoulder blades. In the air that did not move when she did not step closer.

The rule held. The line waited. The pick would be named.
