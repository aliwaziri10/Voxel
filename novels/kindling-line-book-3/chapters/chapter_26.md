Grey light slipped through the cracked window of the room over the tar-shop. One lamp still burned, its flame thin and patient. The floorboards were cold. Kael sat on the edge of the bed and looked at his left foot as if it belonged to the room, not to him. The skin was pale. The toes did not move when he told them to. He reached for the wool sock folded on the blanket.

Sol took it from his hand before his fingers closed over the cuff. She knelt on the boards, the undyed coat brushing her ankles, and held the sock up between them.

"I know you can," she said.

He blinked. "I was only going to say the floor is not that cold."

"The floor is exactly that cold."

She turned the sock, found the heel, and began to work it over his toes. The wool caught on the rough skin of his heel. She tugged, adjusted, and the sock sat twisted, the ribbing spiralling up the side of his ankle.

"Wrong way round," he said.

"So it is."

She peeled it back down, smooth and unhurried, and started again. Her fingers knew the shape of him. The arch. The knob of the ankle. The long bones of the foot that had carried him down the second stair alone, that had called a name into the boiling-house cellar and watched the line take the woman who answered.

The foot had no opinion. It simply lay in her hand, warm and heavy and unresponsive. In the night it had been the colour of candle wax, and she had sat up with it for an hour, chafing it between her palms, saying nothing, until she was sure there was still blood in it. He had pretended to be asleep. She had known that too.

"The foot is the only honest one of the two of us," she said. "Since it never pretends to feel anything."

"That is a harsh review of my leg."

"It is a fair one."

"The other one is no better. It only lies more politely."

"Then you have two witnesses and no testimony."

He watched her hands. The knuckles pale. The nails trimmed short. The way her thumb pressed the wool into place at the top of the cuff. He could not feel the pressure. He could not feel the warmth of her palm against his instep. He saw the contact and his mind supplied the sensation, a memory of touch standing in for the thing itself.

"It is strange," he said. "To see it done and not know it is done."

She paused, the sock settled, her fingers still resting on his ankle. "That is most of what being cared for is. You do not feel it. You watch for it."

He went quiet. The lamp hissed. In the lane below, a door slammed, and a woman's voice rose and fell and was answered by a child's.

"I have spent my life on the other side of that sentence," he said.

Her hand did not move. She held his foot a moment longer than the sock needed. Then she shifted, rose on her knees, and laid her cheek against his knee. The wool of her coat brushed his thigh. His hand found her hair without his mind directing it. The strands were cool against his fingers, then warm. He curled them around the heel of his palm.

Neither of them said what the day was. The trial of the rig. The cords. The count. The plan that lived or died on two pullers staying where they were put.

"Stay five more minutes," he said. Lightly. As if it were a request about tea.

She lifted her head. Her eyes were grey in the grey light. "I have counted. There are four."

"Four then."

They took all four. His fingers in her hair. Her cheek against his knee. The lamp burning down. The cold floor. The foot that felt nothing and told the truth.

Then she sat back. The sock stayed on. The heel straight. The ribbing even.

"Odo's hut," she said. "You said."

"I said."

She rose. Brushed the knees of her coat. Offered him a hand. He took it and used it to lever himself up, and she did not make a show of bearing his weight, and he did not make a show of leaning. The stick waited in the corner. On level ground his left leg took the weight and the pins and needles had faded to a dull hum, and he could have crossed the room without it. He took it anyway. Level ground was one thing. The stair down to the lane was another, and he had agreed to be honest about the difference.

She watched him take it and said nothing. It was the nearest thing to praise she allowed herself before noon.

They went down the stair one tread at a time, the stick finding each step before his foot did, Sol a stair ahead of him with her hand loose on the rail. In the lane the day had the colour of old pewter. The boiling house stood at the end of it with its vents breathing steam, and between the lane and the yard the gate was open, and Joren stood in it with his back against the post and his arms folded and his eyes on nothing.

"Afternoon," Joren said. It was all he said. He watched them go past him into the yard and turned his head to keep them in view.

The yard of the boiling house lay quiet in the afternoon light. Odo's cords were laid out from a stand-in plate, a board with a lamp set on it and a sandbag lashed to its foot, through a door frame to the far end of the lane. Two lines. Two pullers. Odo at one. Tessa at the other, the chalk-line hanging at her belt, the reel bright against her coat. She was twenty-three and quick and she kept her eyes on the board as if it might try to get away.

Kael approached the rig. His square hand found the cord at Odo's station. The hemp was rough, the knots tight. He tested the tension. The cord did not give.

"Two pulls," Odo said. He did not look up from the stand-in lamp. He had trimmed the wick himself, and he had read the lamp's height against his rule, and then, because he read everything twice, he had read it again and written the figure in the log book on his knee. "On her count. In the trough."

Tessa stood at the far end of the lane, the cord coiled in her left hand, her right raised. She was counting under her breath, not the pull but the breaths that came before it: eleven for the charge, the twelfth for the second line, and then the small flat silence between twelve and one that the hut's gauge left open like a door. The lane held its breath with her.

"Now," Tessa called.

They pulled. The cords sang. The stand-in lamp dropped from the board and hung, and the sandbag shifted and settled. The breath held. The lamp stayed down.

"Again," Tessa said. She said it flatly, without asking Odo.

Odo reset the board with his own hands and checked the knot. He laid the lamp back on its mark with the care of a man replacing a sleeping child in its cot, and squared the sandbag against the edge, and stood a moment looking at the two cords where they ran through the door frame as though he were reading a sentence in a language he trusted. "Now."

They pulled. The lamp dropped. The same breath. The same silence.

Odo exhaled. He coiled his cord. "Twice. Same breath. The rig holds."

Tessa came down the lane at a trot, rewinding as she came, and stopped at Odo's elbow to look at the board. "The second cord ran a hair late the first time," she said. "I felt it in my wrist. It was not late the second."

"A hair," Odo agreed. He wrote it down. Then, because he wrote everything down, he wrote down that she had said it.

Kael watched the lamp where it hung. Both cords had come free of the door frame at once, with no more sound than a sheet drawn off a bed, and the thing they were drawing out had no more weight than a pair of pegs. It was a very quiet way to close a line. He found that he disliked it. He had always known what it was to pay, and it had always been loud.

His jaw tightened. "I should take your place at the second cord."

Odo stopped coiling. "No."

"Odo."

"A puller does not move until the count is done. You move."

"The stair is not the rig."

"Your left leg cannot brace. That is the first reason." Odo said it as he said everything, without ornament, the way a man reads off a number. "A cord taken at a run wants a foot that holds. Yours is in a sock."

Kael's hand fisted on the cord. "The second."

Odo looked at him then. The broad face unreadable. "You went down the second stair alone on the eleventh of Cinderveil. You called Mara in on the fourth. The habit is in you. When the line draws, you go to it. A puller stays."

"I know what a puller does."

"Then you know why it is not you." Odo set the coil on the board. "You gave the rule yourself, in the cellar. If a body stands nearer the plate than Sol when it closes, that body takes the seventeen. I am not guessing. I am saying your rule back."

Sol's voice came from the gate. She had stopped there beside Joren with her arms folded. "If you are at the rope and the rope fails, you will run in. You will be the nearest."

Kael turned. Her face was calm. Her eyes were not.

"That is not what I would do."

"It is what you have done."

He denied it. The word left his mouth before he could shape it into anything else. "No."

He knew he was lying a little. The knowledge sat in his chest, hot and cold at once. They were right. Both of them. The stair. The habit. The years of moving toward the sound of the Kindling instead of away from it. He had built a life on being the one who went in. The plan asked him to be the one who stayed out.

He saw it again, as he saw it whenever he let his mind go slack: the mouth of the cellar, the dark below, the thin cold air coming up the stair. He had called the name without thinking, a single word flung down into the black, the way a man shouts for his dog. And she had come, at a run, with her warden's hands out in front of her, and the line had taken her before she reached the bottom step. He had not planned any of it. That was the whole of the trouble. Nothing he had done had been a decision. It had all been a reflex wearing a decision's coat.

The anger rose. Not at them. At the rightness of it. At the fact that being right did not stop him wanting to be the one who pulled.

"Then the door of the passage," he said. "I will be at the door of the passage."

"Say it and stay there," Sol said.

He looked at her. The lamp on the stand-in plate had gone cold. The cords lay coiled. Tessa was winding her reel with great concentration, as a person does who has been told by her whole body to look at anything else. Odo watched him with the patience of a man who had measured the same thing twice and would measure it a third time if asked.

"The door of the passage," Kael said aloud. "I will be at the door of the passage. I will not move from it."

Sol held his gaze. The lane stretched between them. The gauge hut breathed its twelve beats. A bird called once from the roof of the boiling house, and fell silent.

"Good," she said, very quietly, and it was not a verdict. It was the word she had once used on a stair when he had said he would not say sorry, and it meant that she had heard what the sentence cost and would carry the other half of it.

He walked to the gate. Joren pushed off the post as Kael came up, and for a moment the two of them stood looking at the same stretch of lane.

"Passage door," Joren said. Not a question.

"Passage door. I will not move from it."

"I heard." Joren's eyes went to the yard, to the coiled cords and the cold lamp, and then back. His voice was dry and neither kind nor unkind. "A man who says where he will stand and then stands there is not a man I have to watch. I will watch you anyway. That is what a gate is for."

"Then you will hold me to it."

"I will."

Kael nodded. He could think of nothing else to say that would not be a request, and he had run out of requests. He went through the gate into the lane and, because he was going to the lane and because it was the rule and because the words had become a way of tying one knot before he trusted his weight to the next, he said, aloud, "Joren and Corren."

Behind him Joren made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "Aye," he said. "One of us is not here to hear it."

"He will be told."

"He will."

The lane was long and the stick tapped its way down it. His left leg held on the level and the stick took the strain at every rise. The tar-shop chimney smoked. The gauge hut's hatch stayed closed. The line breathed twelve beats, eleven for the charge and one for the second line, and between the twelfth and the first lay the trough where the pull would happen. He had learned to hear it the way a man learns the stairs in his own house in the dark. Eleven, and the twelfth, and the trough. He counted it twice, as Odo would have, and was ashamed to find his lips moving.

He thought of the sock. The wool warm on his foot. The heel straight. The ribbing even. Sol's fingers on his ankle. Her cheek against his knee. The four minutes she had counted and given. He had spent his life on the other side of that sentence. He had been the one who went in. The one who carried. The one who measured and decided and gave the order that sent others to the rope.

Now the plan asked him to stay at a door and not move. To be the one who watched. To trust Odo's broad shoulders and Tessa's quick hands and Sol's sixteen and a half years and the seventeen waiting in the gauge. To trust that the nearest living body would be her, and only her, when the pegs came free.

The anger had not left his chest. It had settled, a coal banked under ash. He was angry that they were right. Angrier that being right did not stop him wanting to be the one who pulled. The habit was in him. The second stair alone. The call into the cellar. The name that had brought Mara running. The line had taken her and kept drawing on her, wrist to forearm to elbow, night by night, because she had been the first body it took. The draw ended when the line closed. The plan closed the line.

He walked the length of the lane to its end, where it bent toward the wide stair, and the lamps under their violet glass flickered up one by one on every fourth riser. The light thinned. The river rushed beside the lane, grey-green and cold, with mist clinging at its edge. The cliffs rose black against the darkening sky. The boiling-house vents exhaled steam that curled and vanished.

He reached the door of the passage. The frame was iron, the hinges rusted but sound. The door itself stood open, a rectangle of darkness leading to the second stair and the iron door with the enamelled knot and the warm black plate at its foot. The plate. Not the anchor plate. The plate in the bare iron door, where Sol had already burned half a year and where she would sit when the count came.

He leaned against the jamb. Crossed his arms. Watched the lane. The mist off the river had crept up to the sill of the passage and lay there, patient as a dog, and the iron under his shoulder was cold enough to feel through the coat. He was glad of it. It was the first honest sensation his left side had offered him all day, and he took it as a gift.

The yard emptied behind him. Odo and Tessa coiled the last of the cords and carried them to the hut. The stand-in lamp was put out. The board and the sandbag were stacked against the wall. The hatch of the gauge hut opened, a rectangle of light spilling onto the packed earth, and Odo went in with his log book and Tessa followed, her chalk-line rewound. They spoke low. Odo wrote. Tessa watched. They did not look at the passage door.

Kael did not move.

At the gate, a long way off, a figure stood against the last of the light with its arms folded. It did not come nearer. It did not look away.

He had said where he would stand. He would stand there. He would watch the cords go taut and the pegs come free and the seventeen years find the woman who had put a sock on his foot and told him the floor was exactly that cold.

The lamp in the gauge hut went out. The yard fell into shadow. The lane held only the violet pools and the mist on the water.

He stayed.
