Grey light slipped through the cracked window of the room over the tar-shop. One lamp still burned, its flame thin and patient. The floorboards were cold. Kael sat on the edge of the bed and looked at his left foot as if it belonged to the room, not to him. The skin was pale. The toes did not move when he told them to. He reached for the wool sock folded on the blanket.

Sol took it from his hand before his fingers closed over the cuff. She knelt on the boards, the undyed coat brushing her ankles, and held the sock up between them.

"I know you can," she said.

He blinked. "I was only going to say the floor is not that cold."

"The floor is exactly that cold."

She turned the sock, found the heel, and began to work it over his toes. The wool caught on the rough skin of his heel. She tugged, adjusted, and the sock sat twisted, the ribbing spiralling up the side of his ankle.

"Wrong way round," he said.

"So it is."

She peeled it back down, smooth and unhurried, and started again. Her fingers knew the shape of him. The arch. The knob of the ankle. The long bones of the foot that had carried him down the second stair alone, that had called a name into the boiling-house cellar and watched the line take the woman who answered.

The foot had no opinion. It simply lay in her hand, warm and heavy and unresponsive.

"The foot is the only honest one of the two of us," she said. "Since it never pretends to feel anything."

"That is a harsh review of my leg."

"It is a fair one."

He watched her hands. The knuckles pale. The nails trimmed short. The way her thumb pressed the wool into place at the top of the cuff. He could not feel the pressure. He could not feel the warmth of her palm against his instep. He saw the contact and his mind supplied the sensation, a memory of touch standing in for the thing itself.

"It is strange," he said. "To see it done and not know it is done."

She paused, the sock settled, her fingers still resting on his ankle. "That is most of what being cared for is. You do not feel it. You watch for it."

He went quiet. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the lane below, a door slammed.

"I have spent my life on the other side of that sentence," he said.

Her hand did not move. She held his foot a moment longer than the sock needed. Then she shifted, rose on her knees, and laid her cheek against his knee. The wool of her coat brushed his thigh. His hand found her hair without his mind directing it. The strands were cool against his fingers, then warm. He curled them around the heel of his palm.

Neither of them said what the day was. The trial of the rig. The cords. The count. The plan that lived or died on two pullers staying where they were put.

"Stay five more minutes," he said. Lightly. As if it were a request about tea.

She lifted her head. Her eyes were grey in the grey light. "I have counted. There are four."

"Four then."

They took all four. His fingers in her hair. Her cheek against his knee. The lamp burning down. The cold floor. The foot that felt nothing and told the truth.

Then she sat back. The sock stayed on. The heel straight. The ribbing even.

"Odo's hut," she said. "You said."

"I said."

She rose. Brushed the knees of her coat. Offered him a hand. He took it, used it to lever himself up, the stick waiting in the corner. His left leg took the weight. The pins-and-needles sensation had faded to a dull hum on level ground. The stick stayed in the corner.

"Joren and Corren," he said.

"At the gate."

He nodded. Walked to the ladder. Descended. The trapdoor closed above him.

The yard of the boiling house lay quiet in the afternoon light. Odo's cords were laid out from a stand-in plate — a board, a lamp, a sandbag — through a door frame to the far end of the lane. Two lines. Two pullers. Odo at one. Tessa at the other. Her chalk-line hung at her belt, the reel bright against her coat.

Kael approached the rig. His square hand found the cord at Odo's station. The hemp was rough, the knots tight. He tested the tension. The cord did not give.

"Two pulls," Odo said. He did not look up from the stand-in lamp. "On her count. In the trough."

Tessa stood at the far end, the cord coiled in her left hand, her right raised. The lane held its breath. The gauge hut's hatch was closed, but Kael knew the rhythm. Eleven breaths for the charge. The twelfth for the second line. The trough between twelve and one.

"Now," Tessa called.

They pulled. The cords sang. The stand-in lamp dropped. The breath held. The lamp stayed down.

"Again," Tessa said.

"Now."

They pulled. The lamp dropped. The same breath. The same silence.

Odo exhaled. He coiled his cord. "Twice. Same breath. The rig holds."

Kael's jaw tightened. "I should take your place at the second cord."

Odo stopped coiling. "No."

"Odo —"

"A puller does not move until the count is done. You move."

"The stair —"

"Your left leg cannot brace. That is the first reason."

Kael's hand fisted on the cord. "The second."

Odo looked at him then. The broad face unreadable. "You went down the second stair alone on the eleventh of Cinderveil. You called Mara in on the fourth. The habit is in you. When the line draws, you go to it. A puller stays."

Sol's voice came from the gate. "If you are at the rope and the rope fails, you will run in. You will be the nearest."

Kael turned. She stood with her arms folded, the copper clasp catching the light. Drop, feather, flame. Her face was calm. Her eyes were not.

"That is not what I would do."

"It is what you have done."

He denied it. The word left his mouth before he could shape it differently. "No."

He knew he was lying a little. The knowledge sat in his chest, hot and cold at once. They were right. Both of them. The stair. The habit. The years of moving toward the sound of the Kindling instead of away. He had built a career on being the one who went in. The plan asked him to be the one who stayed out.

The anger rose. Not at them. At the rightness of it. At the fact that being right did not stop him wanting to be the one who pulled.

"Then the door of the passage," he said. "I will be at the door of the passage."

"Say it and stay there," Sol said.

He looked at her. The lamp in the stand-in plate had gone cold. The cords lay coiled. Tessa was winding her reel. Odo watched him with the patience of a man who had measured the same thing twice and would measure it a third time if asked.

"The door of the passage," Kael said aloud. "I will be at the door of the passage. I will not move from it."

Sol held his gaze. The lane stretched between them. The gauge hut breathed its twelve beats. Somewhere a bird called, once, and fell silent.

"Joren and Corren," he said.

He turned and walked toward the gate.

He reached the gate. Joren stood with his back to the wall, the scar across the back of his hand pale in the fading light. Corren leaned against the opposite post, his eyes on the lane. Neither moved as Kael approached.

"Passage door," Joren said. Not a question.

"Passage door," Kael confirmed. "I will not move from it."

Joren's gaze flicked to the yard, to the coiled cords and the cold stand-in lamp, then back. "The rig held."

"Twice. Same breath."

"Aye."

The word hung between them. Joren had been a warden longer than Kael had been alive. He had seen lines close and lines fail. He had pulled bodies from the boiling-house cellar and written the reports that followed. His scar had come from a rope that snapped on the third pull of a closing that went wrong. Kael had read the file. He had not asked the man about it.

"Two days," Corren said. "The custodian's pick."

Kael stopped. The lane dust settled on his boots. "Named in two days."

"Day Twenty-six," Joren said. "The order gives thirty. Today is twenty-four. The pick is named on twenty-six. The formal demand reads on twenty-eight. The first attempt to close is twenty-nine. The order runs out on thirty."

He spoke it like a tally. No weight. No opinion. The dates were the dates. The custodian had dated the order on Day Zero, the night of the petition, and served it on Day Sixteen. Sixteen days of silence. Now the clock moved again.

Kael's left leg throbhed, a dull deep pulse that had nothing to do with pins and needles. He shifted his weight. The stick stayed in the corner of the room over the tar-shop. He had walked the lane without it. He would walk back without it. The leg held on level ground.

"Bram carries the notes," Kael said. "He wrote two numbers on a slip in the gauge hut on Day Twenty-three. He left toward the Council road."

"Bram carries what he is given," Joren said. "The numbers were the count and the gauge. The pick's name is not a number."

"Who decides the pick?"

"The custodian. The Council confirms. The ward-takers on the roll wait." Joren's shoulders shifted. "You know the roll. You know the names. One of them will be told to stand at the Lower Reach on Day Twenty-eight and read the demand."

Kael did know the roll. He had memorised it three years past when the vacancy opened and the Council had delayed. He had not expected to need it again. The ward-takers were clerks and surveyors and one former auditor who had retired to the archives. None of them had stood at a plate. None of them had felt the Kindling draw. The custodian's order named the pick only as "one ward-taker of the Council's roll." The anonymity was deliberate. The pick would not know until the letter arrived.

"Two days," Kael repeated.

"Two days," Joren agreed. "The plan does not change. Odo and Tessa pull. You hold the door. Sol sits at the plate. The pegs come out in one breath. The seventeen goes to her. The line closes. Mara's cold stops climbing."

"And if the pick objects?"

"The pick reads the demand. The demand is the order. The order is the law. The plan does not change."

Corren pushed off the post. "We walk the perimeter at dusk. The lane, the yard, the gauge hut. The passage door. You will be there."

"I will be there."

"Good."

They fell into step beside him, Joren on his left, Corren on his right. The three of them moved down the lane toward the river, the boots striking earth in a rhythm that had no name. The tar-shop chimney smoked. Ansa's broth pot sent up a curl of steam. The gauge hut's hatch stayed closed. The line breathed twelve beats, eleven for the charge and one for the second line, the trough between twelve and one where the pull would happen.

Kael thought of the sock. The wool warm on his foot. The heel straight. The ribbing even. Sol's fingers on his ankle. Her cheek against his knee. The four minutes she had counted and given. He had spent his life on the other side of that sentence. He had been the one who went in. The one who carried. The one who measured and decided and gave the order that sent others to the rope.

Now the plan asked him to stay at a door and not move. To be the one who watched. To trust Odo's broad shoulders and Tessa's quick hands and Sol's sixteen and a half years and the seventeen waiting in the gauge. To trust that the nearest living body would be her, and only her, when the pegs came free.

The anger had not left his chest. It had settled, a coal banked under ash. He was angry that they were right. Angrier that being right did not stop him wanting to be the one who pulled. The habit was in him. The second stair alone. The call into the cellar. The name that had brought Mara running. The line had taken her and kept drawing on her, wrist to forearm to elbow, night by night, because she had been the first body it took. The draw ended when the line closed. The plan closed the line.

Two days. The pick named. The demand read. The attempt made. The order run out.

He would stand at the passage door. He would not move. He would watch the cords go taut and the pegs come free and the seventeen years find the woman who had put a sock on his foot and told him the floor was exactly that cold.

The lane widened. The river rushed beside them, grey-green and cold. Mist clung to the water's edge. The cliffs rose black against the darkening sky. The boiling-house vents exhaled steam that curled and vanished.

Joren stopped at the corner where the lane bent toward the wide stair. "Dusk watch set. Passage door covered. We relieve at midnight."

Kael nodded. "I will be there."

"See that you are."

They turned back toward the yard. Kael walked on alone, the passage door ahead of him, the gauge hut to his right, the tar-shop behind. The light thinned. The lamps under violet glass flickered to life at every fourth riser of the wide stair, pale pools against the stone.

He reached the door of the passage. The frame was iron, the hinges rusted but sound. The door itself stood open, a rectangle of darkness leading to the second stair and the iron door with the enamelled knot and the warm black plate at its foot. The plate. Not the anchor plate. The plate in the bare iron door. The one Sol had burned at for half a year on Day Thirteen. The one she would sit at when the count came.

He leaned against the jamb. Crossed his arms. Watched the lane.

The yard emptied. Odo and Tessa coiled the last of the cords and carried them to the hut. The stand-in lamp was extinguished. The board and sandbag were stacked against the wall. The gauge hut's hatch opened, a rectangle of light spilling onto the packed earth. Odo emerged with his log book. Tessa followed, her chalk-line rewound. They spoke low. Odo wrote. Tessa watched. They did not look at the passage door.

Kael did not move.

The stair was empty. The households of the Lower Reach kept to their yards. The word had passed that the Thorne steward had moved in the night. The word had passed that the custodian's order had been served. The word had passed that the gauge read seventeen and had not moved in days. No one followed. No one needed to. The plan was the plan. The dates were the dates.

A figure appeared at the top of the lane. Tall. Pale hair. The Thorne grey coat. Aldous Thorne walked down the wide stair and onto the packed earth, his stride measured, his face composed. He did not look at the passage door. He walked toward the gauge hut.

Kael watched him go. The boy had spoken against his house. Had forged his name as witness. Had brought the primer with the model letters leaning right and the name inked out on the last page. The works steward had taught him his letters so someone would know the hand. She had written his name on the witness line herself so a clerk would compare hands and ask who taught him. Wanting to be found. Not by the Council. Not by Thorne.

By Sol.

Aldous reached the hut. The hatch closed behind him. The light vanished.

Kael returned his gaze to the lane. The river. The cliffs. The darkening sky.

Two days.

The pick would be named. The demand would read. The attempt would come. The order would run out.

He would stand at the door. He would not move.

The lamp in the gauge hut went out. The yard fell into shadow. The lane held only the violet pools and the mist on the water.

Joren's voice carried from the gate, low and even. "The custodian's pick is to be named in two days."

Kael did not turn. He did not answer. The words settled in the space between the passage door and the gate, between the plate and the gauge, between the sixteen and a half and the seventeen and the thirty-three that waited on the other side of the pull.

Two days.

He stayed.
