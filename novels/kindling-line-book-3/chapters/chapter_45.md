Morning light slipped through the boards of the room over the tar-shop. The lane noise rose through the floor, cart wheels on cobbles, a voice calling an order, the hiss of a kettle on a pump. Ansa's tea sat in a pot on the table, steam curling into the grey air.

Sol woke with Kael's shoulder under her cheek. The mattress had held its shape through the night. No plan sat in the room beyond the two of them.

She lifted her head. His eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling beams.

"Thirty-three," she said.

He turned his face. The grey at his temples caught the light. "About thirty-three."

She smiled against his skin. "You still say it."

"Principle."

"Say it without."

A pause. His throat moved. "Thirty-three."

The word came out clean. He looked startled at it, as if the number had spoken itself.

Sol laughed, low and quiet. "The sock is the best thing I have ever knitted. And I did not knit it."

Kael's mouth curved. "I knew you did not."

"I bought it."

"I knew that too."

"You know everything and do nothing about it."

"I do the sock."

He moved then, swinging his legs over the side of the bed. No stick. He stood, weight even on both feet, and walked to the window. Twelve steps there. Twelve steps back. He did not mention the absence of the wood. She did not mention it. Neither of them could stop watching the other not mention it.

He stopped at the foot of the bed. The light fell across his face, the line of his jaw, the white strand above his ear.

"It was a very good walk," she said.

"It was twelve steps."

"I was not counting."

"You were."

Sol sat up. The blanket fell to her waist. She held out her arms. He came into them, standing between her knees, and she wrapped her hands around his waist, pressing her face against his chest. Wool and warmth and the steady beat beneath.

"What do you hear?" he asked. His hand rested on her hair, fingers slow.

"Slower."

"That is because you are not counting."

"I will go on."

The word hung between them, quiet and certain.

"Later," she said against his ribs.

"Hold me to it."

She lifted her head. Their eyes met. The promise that had been a vow made under threat had become a small shared joke about an ordinary day. The count had stopped being a clock. This was the fifth time. The first time it was only a joke.

---

The tea had gone cold in the pot. Sol poured two cups anyway. The steam rose thin and pale.

They sat at the table, shoulders touching. The lane carried on below, the rhythm of a morning that asked nothing of them.

"Thirty-three," she said.

"Thirty-three," he answered.

The way they said good night. The way they said good morning.

A knock came at the door. Three raps, measured, the second slightly heavier.

Kael set down his cup. "Enter."

Joren pushed the door open. The corridor beyond held the grey of the stairwell, the scent of mineral dust. Joren's face was drawn, the scar across the back of his hand pale in the morning light. He held a folded paper, edges crisp.

"From the Ashworth gate," Joren said. "Addressed to both names. A hand neither of you knows."

Kael took it. The paper was thick, the fold sharp. He passed it to Sol.

She broke the seal. The wax was red, unmarked by any house device. The script inside was neat, slanting right, the descenders closed.

*To Isolde Vane and Kael Ashworth,* she read aloud, her voice steady in the quiet room. *The family has opened a place for both of you at its table and a line in its ledger. I would like to be told, aloud and first, the day you come. Auda Ashworth.*

She lowered the paper. The words settled in the air between them.

Kael said nothing for a long moment. His fingers traced the edge of the table, the groove worn by generations of tar-shop keepers. The lamplight from the gallery mouth threw a blue stripe across the floorboards.

"I will say it first," he said.

Sol nodded. The tea sat forgotten. She stood, and he rose with her, the stick leaning against the wall where it had waited all night. He did not reach for it.

They went down the narrow stair to the lane. The blue lamp in the gallery mouth burned steady, its flame caught behind glass. The households of the Lower Reach moved through their day, carts and baskets and the clatter of copper on stone. Ansa stood at her pump, small and brown and knotted, a basket at her feet.

She looked up. Her eyes found them. One word.

"Eat."

Sol's throat tightened. Kael's hand found hers, fingers closing over her knuckles.

They walked past the tar-shop, past the gallery mouth, toward the boiling house. No rope coiled over a shoulder. No cords waiting at the turn of the stair. No one stood at the cellar mouth. The door of the passage hung open, a rectangle of dark at the head of the second stair.

The descent was familiar. The iron rungs cold under boots, then warm, then hot as they neared the turn. The hum rose, vibration in ribs and soles. The air thickened, mineral and old iron.

At the foot of the second stair, the bare iron door waited. The plate in its centre was black, unmarked. Sol laid her palm flat against it.

Cold. Ordinary iron. No surge. No hum beneath her skin. She left her hand there, counting the beats of her own pulse against the metal. Nothing happened. The plate did not wake.

Kael stood one step above her, the stick absent, his weight easy on the stone. His voice was quiet in the stairwell.

"Do you want me to wait?"

She turned her head. His face was calm, the grey at his temples catching the blue ghost of the lamp from above.

"No," she said. "Come here."

He descended the last step. His hand found her shoulder, thumb pressing the curve of muscle. They stood together in the dark, the plate cold beneath her palm, the second stair rising behind them into silence.

"Let us go up," he said.

They climbed.

They climbed. The iron rungs bit into her palms, cold at first, then warming where her skin pressed. The hum faded behind them, a vibration leaving the bones. The air thinned, the mineral scent giving way to the yeast-sour tang of the cellar, then the sharper bite of tar and coal dust from the boiling house above.

Kael moved beside her, his breath steady. No stick tapped the stone. No cords whipped against the shaft walls. The twin brass pegs lay where Odo and Tessa had left them, coiled rope and iron waiting for a summons that would not come. The sockets in the lintel and the sill were empty, dark holes in iron that had held the weight of seventeen years.

She reached the cellar floor first. Her boots struck the flagstones. The boiling house sprawled around them, vast and quiet, the great kettles dormant, the fire mouths black. Morning light cut through the high windows in grey shafts, dust motes dancing in the slant.

Kael landed behind her. She turned. His face was pale in the dust-light, the white strand at his temple caught in a beam. He looked at the iron door they had left behind, the bare plate set in its centre, then back at her.

"Done," he said.

The word hung in the space between them, smaller than it should have been for the thing it named.

Sol nodded. She looked at her own hand, the palm that had rested on the plate. The lines were ordinary, the callus from rope and the newer mark from the pegs. No glow lingered. No cold sank into the bone. The skin was warm, pink at the heel where the metal had touched.

She flexed her fingers. The motion was easy, the joints smooth.

"Your leg," she said.

He glanced down. The left leg, the one that had gone numb below the hip on the night of the swell, the one that had carried a stick through twenty-three chapters of stairs. He shifted weight onto it, testing. The boot heel struck the stone, a solid sound.

"Holds," he said.

They walked across the cellar toward the stair that led up to the yard. The Thorne guard would be at the gate, copper clasps dull in the day. The lane would be louder now, the carts heavier, the voices sharper. Ansa would be at her pump still, or back in her kitchen, the tea pot refilled.

The stair climbed. Stone treads, worn hollow by generations of boots. Sol went first. Kael followed, his hand light on the wall. No hum rose from below. The second stair, the one that dropped to the plate, yawned behind a closed door at the landing. The iron door of the passage. She did not look at it.

They emerged into the yard. The sky was a pale dome, clouds thinning to grey at the edges. The cliff face loomed, striated rock black against the light. The Thorne guard stood at the far gate, a line of grey and copper. One of them raised a hand in acknowledgement. Kael returned it, two fingers to his brow.

Mara's stool sat empty at the cellar mouth. The hard wood she had claimed for days, the nights she had counted aloud while the cold climbed her arms. The stool was just a stool now. A piece of furniture. No one was watching the dark.

Sol walked past it. Kael fell into step beside her. The gate opened before they reached it, a guardsman pulling the iron latch. The lane received them, the noise rising, the smell of tar and smoke and baking bread.

Ansa stood at her pump. The basket at her feet was full, greens and roots and a loaf wrapped in cloth. She watched them approach, her face folded with lines, the knotted hair escaping its pins. She did not smile. She did not speak until they stopped before her.

"Eat," she said.

The word was the same. The command was different. No urgency. No threat of cold. An invitation.

"We will," Sol said.

Ansa dipped her chin. She turned back to her pump, the handle rising and falling, water gurgling into a pail. The rhythm was old, older than the line, older than the counts.

They continued. The gallery mouth yawned ahead, the blue lamp still burning behind its glass, a steady flame in the day's grey. The households of the Lower Reach moved around them, carts and baskets, the clatter of copper, a child's shout, a dog barking. No one stopped. No one stared. The wardens Joren and Corren stood at the corner, scarred hand and straight spine, watching the flow. They nodded as Sol and Kael passed.

The room over the tar-shop waited. The narrow stair. The mattress with its straw tick. The pot of cold tea on the table.

Sol climbed the stair first. The boards creaked under her weight, familiar notes. Kael followed, his tread lighter than it had been, the stick left leaning against the wall below.

The room was as they had left it. The lamp on the bedside table, wick curled into ash. The window admitting the morning. The coats folded over the chair backs.

She went to the table. The tea was cold, a skin forming on the surface. She poured it out into the basin by the pump, the dark liquid swirling down the drain. Fresh water. New leaves. The kettle on the hook, the fire box below it.

Kael sat at the table. He watched her move, his elbows on the wood, his chin on his folded hands. The grey at his temples caught the light from the window. The white strand. The shadow under his cheekbone where the stick had pressed.

"Thirty-three," he said.

The number floated between them. Not a count. Not a clock. A statement of fact, spoken aloud in a room that asked nothing of them.

"Thirty-three," she agreed.

She set the tea to steep. The steam rose, curling into the grey air. The lane noise rose through the floor. The blue lamp burned at the gallery mouth. The iron door at the foot of the second stair sat cold and closed, its plate black and silent.

The iron was only iron.

She turned from the table. Kael held out his hand. She took it. The palm was warm, dry, alive. The callus on his index finger, the rope line, the newer mark from the pegs. Her own hand matched it, callus for callus, mark for mark.

They sat together in the morning light. The tea steeped. The city kept its count. The line was closed.

The iron was only iron.
