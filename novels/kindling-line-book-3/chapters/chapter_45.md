Morning light came through the boards of the room over the tar-shop in thin gold lines and laid itself across the blanket. The noise of the lane rose with it, cart wheels on cobbles, a voice calling an order, the hiss of a kettle set down on a pump. On the table a pot of Ansa's tea steamed gently in the cool air, and nothing else in the room wanted anything from anyone.

Sol woke with Kael's shoulder under her cheek. The mattress had held its shape through the night. There was no plan in the room. She noticed it the way one notices that a tooth has stopped aching, by the absence of a thing that had been there so long it had become the weather.

She lifted her head. His eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling beams, and the look on his face was the look of a man who had been awake for a while and had not minded.

"Thirty-three," she said.

He turned his face. The grey at his temples caught the light, and the white patch above his ear, which she no longer saw as a mark and only as a part of him. "About thirty-three."

She smiled against his skin. "You still say it."

"On principle."

"Say it without."

A pause. His throat moved. "Thirty-three."

It came out clean. He looked startled at it, as if the number had spoken itself and he had only been standing near.

Sol laughed, low and quiet, and the laugh shook the bed. "The sock is the best thing I have ever knitted. And I did not knit it."

His mouth curved. "I knew you did not."

"I bought it."

"I knew that too."

"You know everything and do nothing about it."

"I do the sock."

He moved then, swinging his legs over the side of the bed. No stick. He stood with his weight even on both feet and walked to the window, and she lay on her side and watched him go. Twelve steps there. At the window he looked down into the lane for a long moment, at nothing at all, at a woman with a basket and a boy chasing a hoop. Twelve steps back. He did not mention that he had left the wood in the corner. She did not mention it either. Neither of them could stop watching the other not mention it.

He stopped at the foot of the bed. The light fell across his face and the line of his jaw.

"It was a very good walk," she said.

"It was twelve steps."

"I was not counting."

"You were."

She sat up. The blanket fell to her waist. She held out her arms, and he came into them without a word, standing between her knees, and she wrapped her hands around his waist and pressed her face against his chest. Wool and warmth and, under both, the steady beat of him. She had listened to that beat in a great many places. In the dark, in the damp, on a stair, in a cellar with her ear against a door. She had not once listened to it with nothing waiting.

"What do you hear?" he asked. His hand rested on her hair, the fingers slow.

"Slower."

"That is because you are not counting."

"I will go on."

Her answer stayed in the quiet room, certain, and she felt him smile against the top of her head. "You have a cowlick," he said. "Here. It has been there since the first week. I have been waiting for the right moment to mention it."

"This is the right moment?"

"It is the only one I have found that does not involve a stair."

"Later," she said into his ribs.

"Hold me to it."

She lifted her head. Their eyes met. The words had begun as a vow made under threat, a thing said in the dark with both of them thinking of the cost, and she heard now that they had become another thing altogether. They were a small shared joke about an ordinary day. The count had stopped being a clock. It was the first time the sentence had been only a joke, and she found that she was grinning like a fool, and that he was too.

---

The tea had gone lukewarm in the pot. Sol poured two cups anyway. The steam rose thin and pale.

They sat at the table with their shoulders touching. The lane carried on below, the rhythm of a morning that asked nothing of them, and for a little while neither of them said anything at all.

She had spent months counting. First the days, then the years, then the gap between the years she had and the years she was owed. She had said the numbers aloud so often that they had stopped being arithmetic and become a prayer, a thing recited to keep the dark at the right distance. And she saw now, sitting at the table with a lukewarm cup in her hands and Kael's shoulder warm against hers, that she had stopped. Not the saying. The fear. She was no longer counting the days she had. She was counting, with him, the ones they would spend.

It was the same number. It was an entirely different sum.

"Thirty-three," she said.

"Thirty-three," he answered.

The way they said good night. The way they said good morning.

A knock came at the door. Three raps, measured, the second slightly heavier.

Kael set down his cup. "Enter."

Joren pushed the door open. The passage beyond held the grey of the stairwell and the smell of mineral dust. His face was weathered and calm, the scar across the back of his hand pale in the morning light. He held a folded paper, its edges crisp.

"From the Ashworth gate," Joren said. "Addressed to both names. In a hand neither of you knows."

Kael took it. The paper was thick, the fold sharp, and he turned it over once as a man does who has handled many sealed letters and none that he wished to open. He passed it to Sol.

She broke the seal. The wax was dark red and unmarked by any device. The script inside was neat and slanted to the right, the descenders closed, an old hand that had learned its letters when the world was slower. She read it aloud, her voice steady in the quiet room.

*To Isolde Vane and Kael Ashworth. The family has opened a place for both of you at its table and a line in its ledger. I would like to be told, aloud and first, the day you come. Auda Ashworth.*

She lowered the paper. The words settled in the air between them.

Kael said nothing for a long moment. His fingers moved along the edge of the table, over the groove worn into the wood by generations of tar-shop keepers. Joren had the grace to look at the window. The lane went on below, the cart wheels and the voices, and the paper in Sol's hand was very light.

"I will say it first," Kael said at last. His voice was not quite steady, and he did not try to make it so.

"She asks the way you do," Sol said, low. "Aloud, and first."

"I think that is where I learned it." He let out a breath that was half a laugh. "I did not know it was a family habit. I thought I had invented it."

Sol nodded. She folded the letter along its crease and set it on the table between the two cups, and Joren inclined his head, once, and went out, and his boots went down the stair without hurry.

Neither of them touched the tea again. She stood, and he rose with her. The stick leaned in the corner by the head of the stair, where it had waited all night, and he looked at it for the space of a breath.

"Stairs," he said.

She said nothing. It was the nearest thing to a joke the stick had ever been given. He took it, and they went down together. On the stair he went one tread ahead of her, the stick finding each step, and she watched his left leg take the weight and hold, and thought that she would never again see it do so without a small private thanks to whatever had decided, in the end, to let it.

The lane was bright. The blue lamp in the gallery mouth was lit, its flame steady behind its glass, a small cool light in the day's gold, and the households of the Lower Reach moved about their morning around it with baskets and carts and the clatter of copper on stone. A child shouted. A dog barked and was told to stop. A woman at the baker's window was arguing with a boy about the price of a loaf, cheerfully and at length, and a girl was walking a hoop along the gutter with her tongue between her teeth, and an old man had carried a stool out to sit by the lamp and was doing nothing at all, with great concentration. Nobody stopped to look at them. Nobody stared. It was a kindness so large and so ordinary that Sol almost did not recognize it, and then she did, and had to look at the cobbles for a moment.

Ansa stood at her pump, small and brown and knotted, with a basket at her feet. She looked up. Her eyes found them across the lane. She did not smile, and she did not speak until they stopped in front of her.

"Eat," she said.

The word was the same word. It was not the same command. There was no urgency in it and no cold behind it, only a woman who had spent a season feeding two thin people and had decided that she was not quite done.

"We will," Sol said.

Ansa pressed a heel of bread into each of their hands, still warm, and turned back to the pump. The handle rose and fell. Water gurgled into the pail. The rhythm was old, older than the line, older than any of their counts.

They ate as they walked. The bread was good and a little burnt on the bottom, and Kael ate his with the slow, serious pleasure he had learned to give to small things. The lane gave way to the yard of the boiling house, and the yard to the cellar mouth. The door stood open. Dark waited beyond it, ordinary and cool.

There was no rope coiled over her shoulder. There were no cords laid out along the passage floor. No one stood at the cellar mouth, and no one was waiting for a count. The yard was empty of everything but morning. A pigeon walked the length of the wall with the unhurried air of a landlord. The kettles in the boiling house stood cold behind their open doors, and a shaft of sun lay across the flagstones where, a season ago, a lamp had burned all night and a woman had sat on a hard stool and waited for one word to come up the stair. Nobody counted there now. The stool was a stool. Sol stopped on the threshold and breathed in the cold smell of old iron and wet stone, and it smelled, for the first time, only like a cellar.

"Together," Kael said.

"Together."

They went down.

The second stair was as it had always been, the treads worn shallow, the walls beaded with damp. His stick tapped on every step and her boots followed, and neither of them hurried. At the turn she glanced back and saw him there, one hand on the wall, the lamplight from above catching the white above his ear, and he met her eyes and lifted his brows, and she turned and went on down.

At the foot of the stair the bare iron door waited. The plate set into it was black and plain, a dull disc in a dull door. She had knelt at it. She had counted at it. She had thought, in the worst hour, that she would never walk away from it as she was.

She had expected, on the long way down, to feel relief at this moment, or fear, or the thin cold ghost of the old dread. She felt only the plain curiosity of a person looking at a door she had once been unable to look away from. It was a very ordinary door. It had a few rust stains along the lower edge and a scratch where a heavy thing had been dragged across it once, and she wondered, with a tenderness that surprised her, who had done the dragging.

She laid her palm flat against it.

Cold. Ordinary iron. No surge, no hum beneath her skin. She left her hand there and counted the beats of her own pulse against the metal, slow and even, and nothing happened. The plate did not wake. It was a piece of iron in a door in a cellar, and it was exactly as warm as the air.

Behind her Kael stopped on the last step. When he spoke his voice was quiet in the stairwell.

"Do you want me to wait?"

She turned her head. His face was calm, and the lamp from above laid a pale line along his cheekbone.

"No," she said. "Come here."

He came down the last step. His hand found her shoulder, the thumb pressing the curve of muscle, and for a moment they stood together in the dark with the plate cold under her palm and the second stair rising behind them into silence.

Then she took her hand away, and he took hers, and they went up. The stick tapped once on the stone and was quiet. Halfway to the turn she looked back, just once, over her shoulder.

The iron was only iron.
