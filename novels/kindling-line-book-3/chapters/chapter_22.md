The lamp flame steadied in the still air of the room over the tar shop. Ansa's soup pot sat on the floor between them, steam curling from the rim. Sol leaned back against Kael's shins, her weight settled on the good leg's side, the wool of her undyed coat rough against his trousers. She held the wooden spoon and offered him the pot.

"The spoon is mine," she said. "You can have the pot."

"That is an unfair division of labour."

She turned her head, the corner of her mouth lifting. "Since when do you care about fairness?"

"Since you made the pot sound like a privilege."

He took the spoon. The broth was thick with barley and something green, salted deep. He ate, the warmth spreading through his chest. She watched him, the lamp light catching the copper clasp at her throat, the line of her jaw.

The gauge-book page lay on his knee, the pencil moving in his square hand. Her initials still sat in the margin from the old ledger, I.V., the letters upright, the descenders open. He wrote the figures beneath them.

Sixteen and a half. Thirty-four. Seventeen and a half.

He said the gap aloud. "Seventeen and a half."

The gauge in the hut below read seventeen. He noticed. He said nothing about it.

Sol took the spoon back. She scraped the bottom of the pot, the wood knocking against iron. "You are counting the half."

"I am counting what is there."

"The half is not there. The gauge reads seventeen. The gap reads seventeen and a half. The half lives in your arithmetic, not in the line."

"The line does not care about halves. The nearest living body pays. A fraction stays with the wielder. That is the rule."

"Then why write it?"

"Because the gap is what hurts."

She went still. The spoon hovered over the pot. The lamp flame threw her shadow long across the wall.

"You think the gap is what hurts."

"Is it not?"

She set the spoon down. The pot rocked on the floorboards. Her fingers found his knee through the wool, the pressure light, deliberate.

"When no one stands near," she said, "I pay all of it. When you stand near, you pay for me. Your leaving is only me paying."

The pencil stopped. The graphite tip pressed into the paper, a dark dot blooming.

"I could go," he said. "A winter. The Reach would hold. The line would hold. The gap would be yours again, clean."

"Clean." She repeated the word. "You would be three hundred miles away. The cost would fall on me every time the line burns. That is not clean. That is you deciding what I can bear."

"I am offering—"

"You are deciding." Her hand slid up his thigh, came to rest on his hip. The table peg pressed against her palm through the pocket wool. "I want you where I can count you. Here. In the room. At the plate. At the rope's end. Where the rule puts you when the cost comes due."

He stopped writing. The pencil lay flat across the page. The figures blurred at the edges.

Her head tipped back against his knee. She looked up at him, the lamp light catching her eyes, grey-green in the gold. The copper clasp threw a dull warm glint onto her cheek.

"You did it again," she said quietly.

"What."

"Decided for me. You offered to leave before I could tell you what I needed. You made the calculation in your head and presented the answer. The old habit."

His throat moved. The stick leaned against the wall by the door, forgotten. His left leg held its weight without tremor.

"Tell me first, every time," he said. The words came slow, shaped by the memory of a quarrel on a stair. "That was the bargain."

She smiled then, small and tired. "Thank you for saying it first."

His hand left the pencil. It found her hair, fingers threading through the dark strands, the pads of his fingertips pressing against her scalp. She closed her eyes. The lamp flame did not waver.

The gauge-book page stared up at them, the numbers stark in his hand. Sixteen and a half. Thirty-four. Seventeen and a half. The gauge read seventeen. The half a year between them sat in the arithmetic like a stone in a shoe.

He thought of the seventeen stored in the dark below. He thought of the neatness of it. Sixteen and a half plus seventeen. Thirty-three and a half. Thirty-four minus half. The symmetry that would let them grow old together, equal, the way the old count had promised.

He refused to finish the addition in his head. He saw how neat it would be. He hated that it was neat.

The pick would be named on Day Twenty-Six. Six days. The custodian's order, dated the night of the petition, served on Day Sixteen, gave thirty days total. The pick would stand where the custodian said. Whoever stood nearest the line would take the stored years.

The cost rule was absolute. The nearest living body paid most. A fraction stayed with the wielder. Transfer was always involuntary. No chosen buffer. No ward-binding.

Sol's fingers tightened on his hip. Her eyes opened. The grey-green held his.

"Then we say it out loud," she said. "Where they can hear."

He did not ask who they were. The wind rose outside, rattling the window pane. The chalk-line snapped somewhere in the yard, sharp as a whip crack. The gauge held twelve. Twelve. Twelve.

The room held its breath.

The wind rattled the window pane again. The chalk-line snapped a second time, farther away now, moving toward the gauge hut. The gauge held twelve. Twelve. Twelve.

Kael's hand stayed in Sol's hair. The strands were cool against his fingertips, the ends brushing the wool of his coat. He could feel the pulse at her temple, steady and slow. His own pulse matched it, or near enough.

The gauge-book page lay between them, the pencil still flat across the figures. Sixteen and a half. Thirty-four. Seventeen and a half. The half a year that lived only in arithmetic.

"Day Twenty-Six," he said. The words came quiet, not a question. "The pick is named on Day Twenty-Six. The order was dated the night of the petition. Served on Day Sixteen. Thirty days total."

Sol's eyes stayed open. She did not look away. "Six days."

"Six days to learn the room. The peg locations. The rope rig. The puller positions. The signal code. The timing."

"Odo and Tessa pull in one breath. Beyond the room's reach. Thirty feet."

"The margin we measured."

"The margin that is fear. The margin that is the rule."

He nodded. His thumb traced a slow line along her hairline, the skin there fine and warm. The lamp flame threw their shadows against the wall, two dark shapes merged at the shoulders.

"Mara's cold climbed to the forearms on Day Eight," Sol said. "Renn said the draw rises on nights after someone has gone below. We went below yesterday. The anchor plate. The spiral stair. Two hundred and eleven steps."

"The draw ends when the line closes."

"The line closes when the pegs pull. The pegs pull when Odo and Tessa haul the rope rig in one breath. Simultaneous. If the timing fails by a heartbeat, the pegs bind. The line does not close. The stored sum stays. The order runs out. The Council sends another pick. Another attempt. Another year."

"We have six days to learn the room. Fourteen to the end of the order."

"Thirteen to the formal demand. Day Twenty-Eight. The Lower Reach. The custodian reads the order. The pick stands at the plate."

Kael's jaw tightened. The stick leaned against the wall, the wood worn smooth by his grip. His left leg held its weight without tremor, the numbness a dull presence he had learned to ignore on level ground.

"Renn's map comes tomorrow," he said. "Aldous said noon. The room over the tar shop. The peg locations. The rope rig. The puller positions. The signal code. The timing."

"Everything," Sol said.

"Everything."

She shifted against his shins, the movement small, settling deeper. The copper clasp at her throat caught the lamp light again, threw it back dull and warm. Her fingers on his hip pressed once, a brief squeeze.

"I am tired," she said.

He let out a breath he had not known he was holding. "Sleep here. The floor is hard but the soup pot is warm. Ansa's bread is on the table."

"I do not want to sleep. I want to stop thinking."

"Then stop."

She laughed, low and rough. "You make it sound simple."

"It is simple. You decide to stop. The thinking does not stop. But you stop following it."

"Since when are you a philosopher?"

"Since I learned from the worst student I have ever had."

Her hand left his hip. She reached up, fingers closing over his where it rested in her hair. The contact was light, brief. She let go.

"Stay," she said. "Just stay. Here. In the room. At the plate. At the rope's end."

"I am not going anywhere."

"Say it."

"I am not going anywhere."

"Again."

"I am not going anywhere."

She closed her eyes. The lamp flame did not waver. The wind rose and fell outside. The chalk-line did not snap again. The gauge held twelve. Twelve. Twelve.

Kael watched her face in the gold light. The line of her jaw. The dark sweep of her lashes against her cheek. The copper clasp dull at her throat. The oilcloth bundle on the table, the three papers inside — the leaf, Renn's sheet, the diagram — the dotted line pointing east toward the cliff, toward the Lower Reach, toward the boiling house where the second line breathed twelve beats and stored seventeen years.

He thought of the works steward, small and upright, grey hair pinned flat, copper clasp of three knotted strands. Forty years of carrying on the order of a dead man. Teaching Aldous his letters so someone would know the hand. Writing Aldous's name on the witness line herself. Crossing out her own name the day she began. Asking to be given to no one until the line closed.

He thought of Renn, lean and forty, ink in the creases of every knuckle, locked in the third gallery, the line drawing on him for three years while the gauge climbed from fourteen to seventeen. Renn's last chalk on the second stair: THIS ONE HAS NO BOTTOM I HAVE FOUND.

He thought of Mara, twenty years' service, cold climbing from wrists to forearms to elbows, the draw ending only when the pegs pulled.

He thought of the pick, unnamed, waiting on Day Twenty-Six. Whoever stood nearest the line would take the stored years. The cost would fall on the next nearest. Thirty feet away. The margin they had measured.

He thought of the neatness he had refused to finish. Sixteen and a half plus seventeen. Thirty-three and a half. Thirty-four minus half. The symmetry that would let them grow old together, equal.

He did not finish the addition. He hated that it was neat.

Sol's breathing slowed. Her hand relaxed in his hair, fingers loosening, palm cupping the back of his head. The weight of her against his shins grew heavier, trust given without words.

Kael did not move. The pencil lay flat on the gauge-book page. The figures stared up at him, stark in his square hand. Sixteen and a half. Thirty-four. Seventeen and a half. Seventeen on the gauge. The half a year between them.

He would learn the room in six days. He would learn the pegs, the rope rig, the pullers, the signals, the contingencies. He would stand where the rule put him when the cost came due. He would tell her first, every time.

The lamp flame burned steady. The room held its breath.
