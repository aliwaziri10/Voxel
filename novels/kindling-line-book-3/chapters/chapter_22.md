Ansa knocked once, three even raps, and her boots were already going down the stair when Sol reached the door. On the boards outside sat a pot with a cloth tied over the lid and a wooden spoon laid across the knot. Steam came out from under the cloth in a thin line.

"Eat it before it sets," Ansa's voice came up from the landing, "or I will know."

Sol carried the pot in against her chest and shut the door with her heel. The window had gone the orange of the hour before dusk, and the lamp on the table was lit low. Kael sat in the chair beside it with the gauge-book open on his knee and a pencil in his square hand. His left leg stretched out in front of him. The stick leaned against the wall by the door, where he had put it down on coming in and had not looked at since.

She did not take the other chair. She set the pot on the floor beside his foot, lowered herself to the boards, and leaned back against his shins, so that her shoulders came to rest just below his knees. Her weight settled against the right leg, the one that told him she was there. The wool of her undyed coat was rough through his trousers. He felt her breathe out, long, the way a person does on putting down a load.

"That is the good side," he said.

"I know which side it is."

She untied the cloth. Barley and leek and a salted bone, and the smell came up warm and thick into the cold of the room. She lifted the spoon and held it where he could see it.

"The spoon is mine," she said. "You can have the pot."

"That is an unfair division of labour."

She turned her head, and the corner of her mouth lifted. "Since when do you care about fairness?"

"Since you made the pot sound like a privilege."

"It is the heavier of the two. I am being generous." She ate a mouthful, blew on the next, and tipped it up over her shoulder without looking, the spoon held out at the end of her arm. He leaned forward and took it. The broth was hot and salted deep, and the barley had gone soft the way Ansa cooked it, past the point where it was a grain and into plain warmth.

"Well?" she said.

"It is the best thing I have eaten since the last time Ansa cooked."

"That is the only compliment she accepts."

They went on like that, the spoon over her shoulder and back, a share and a share. It was a small business and neither of them hurried it. Twice her fingers closed round his wrist to steady the spoon, and twice he found that he had stopped reading the page on his knee. For ten years he had sat across a table from other people's figures and taken them down without once being fed while he did it. He had not known the two things could be done together. It turned out that they could, and that the figures got no worse for it.

Down in the yard a boy called to another boy about a bucket, and a cart went by in the lane with a slow iron rattle, and then the quiet came back in. Sol set the spoon in the pot and left it there. She let her head fall back against his knee. He looked down at the crown of her head, at the dark hair coming loose from its pin, and he thought that he could have sat there until the lamp burned dry and called it a good evening's work.

Then he picked up the pencil, because he was who he was.

He turned the gauge-book page and wrote on the back of it in his square hand. Her initials still stood in the margin from the old ledger, *I.V.*, the letters upright, the descenders open. He set the figures under them.

Sixteen and a half. Thirty-four. Seventeen and a half.

"The gap," he said aloud. "Seventeen and a half."

At Odo's afternoon reading the gauge had stood at seventeen. He had written that at the head of the page that morning, and it lay there now, half a year short of the line beneath it. He looked at the two numbers one above the other. He said nothing about it. The half year was nobody's fault. It was only that the world had not been built to come out even.

Sol took the spoon from the pot and scraped the bottom, wood knocking on iron. "You are counting the half."

"I am counting what is there."

"The gauge says seventeen. The gap says seventeen and a half. The half lives in your pencil, not in the line."

"The line does not care about halves. I know that."

"Then why write it?"

"Because the gap is what hurts."

She went still. The spoon hung over the pot. The lamp threw her shadow long across the wall, and it did not move.

"You think the gap is what hurts."

"Is it not? You have sixteen and a half. I have about thirty-four. When I am gone you will have a great many years of being the one left, and I am the cause of them." He heard how level his voice was. It was the voice he used for reading a total aloud. "I could go. A winter. The Reach would hold, and the line would hold. The gap would be yours again, clean."

Sol set the spoon down. The pot rocked on the boards. She said nothing for as long as it takes to draw a slow breath, and then she turned, still sitting, until her shoulder lay against his knee and she could see his face.

"When no one stands near," she said, "I pay all of it. When you stand near, you pay for me. That is the rule, and you know it better than I do. If you go, you have not taken one day off the cost. You have only moved it to me, and made me pay it alone."

"I meant it as a kindness."

"I know you did."

"Then it was a poor one."

"It was a clean one," she said, "and I do not want clean. I want you where I can count you. Here, in the room, where the rule puts you when the bill comes due." Her hand found his knee through the wool and stayed there, the pressure light and deliberate. "I would rather pay with you in the room than be spared with you gone."

The pencil stopped. The graphite tip pressed into the paper and a dark dot bloomed under it and spread.

She tipped her head back against his knee again and looked up at him. The lamp caught her eyes, grey-green in the gold. He put the pencil down flat across the figures, and his hand went to her hair of its own accord. His fingers threaded into the dark strands, and the pads of them pressed against her scalp, and she closed her eyes.

He heard what he had said then. He heard it all at once, the way a man hears a figure that is wrong only after he has read the whole column. He had sat there with her whole future spread on the back of a gauge-book page, and he had worked out in his head, in the tidy hand he kept for sums, what she could bear. Then he had offered her the answer already finished. It was the old habit in a better coat. It had passed for kindness only because it was so well ruled.

"I did it again," he said.

Her eyes opened.

"I decided for you. I did the sum alone and handed you the total, and called it an offer." His thumb moved once along her hairline, slow. "Tell me first, every time. That was the bargain, and I broke it with a pencil. I should have asked you what you could bear before I told you."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she smiled, small and tired and entirely real.

"Thank you for saying it first," she said.

"You would have said it."

"I would have. It is better that you did." She turned her cheek into his palm, and he felt the warmth of the soup still in her skin. "That is the part I did not think you could do. Not the leg. That."

"It is a very small amount."

"It is more than you had at the start of the month."

He did not answer, because it was true. The stick stood where he had left it by the door. His left leg lay out in front of him and held its own weight without any tremor, and he did not look at it.

She reached up without looking and took the pencil from the page. He let her. She turned it over in her fingers and read the figures upside down, her lips moving, and then she set the point under the line that said seventeen and a half and drew it slowly along beneath the numbers, once, from one end to the other.

"You write it like a debt," she said.

"It is the only way I know to write it."

"Write it like a distance, then. A debt is what you pay down until it is gone. Seventeen and a half years is a road, and a road is what two people walk side by side, one on the near edge and one on the far, until the ground gives out under both of them at the same pace."

He took the pencil back. He did not change the figure. But he looked at it again, the seventeen and the half, and it seemed to him a stretch of ground with two people standing on it, and the page had more room in it than it had had a moment before.

"Ansa told me once that a line does not owe you its length," he said. "It has the length it has, and you use it."

"Ansa says a great deal about rope."

"It turns out to be about most things."

Sol laughed against his knee, low, and her hair slid across the back of his hand. She reached for the pot, found the last of the barley with the spoon, and held it up over her shoulder again without turning. "Finish it. You have not eaten properly since the stair."

He finished it. The broth had gone lukewarm and it tasted of salt and bone and the long afternoon, and he ate every grain, because she was watching the lamp and pretending not to watch him. The flame had burned down a finger's width since she first sat, and neither of them had once thought of the hour.

There was another sum on the page that he had not written, and he would not write it. It lay under the figures the way a floor lies under a rug. Her count and the stored years, set side by side, came out so evenly that his whole ledger-trained mind leaned toward it like a plant to a window. Equal. The two of them growing old at the same pace, to the same day, the way the old count had once promised and then denied. He could see how neat it would be. He hated that it was neat. In ten years of ledgers, a total that came out that evenly had always turned out to have been cooked.

He left it unwritten and let the lamp burn.

"The custodian names the pick on Day Twenty-Six," he said. It was the only date in the order that mattered tonight. He said it once, flat, the way he would have read a due date into a record. "Six days."

"Whoever the custodian names will stand where the custodian says." Sol kept her cheek in his hand. "And whoever stands nearest the line takes what it holds."

"Yes."

"Then they will stand nearest to me."

He had been about to say that they would not, and he found that the sentence would not form. He did not tell her it would be fine. He did not tell her the pick would be a stranger who would never come near, or that there was a way to arrange the room so that the cost fell on no one. Every one of those sentences was a finished sum. He held them all in his mouth and let each one go unspoken.

"I do not know yet," he said. "I will not pretend to."

"Good." Her hand tightened on his knee, one firm press, and relaxed. "Then we say it out loud. Where they can hear."

He did not ask who they were.

Outside, the cart had gone, and the boy in the yard had gone in to his supper. The lamp stood steady in the still air. The pot on the floor had stopped steaming, and neither of them reached for the spoon. Kael kept his hand in her hair and the pencil lay flat on the page, and between the two of them, in the square hand and the lamplight, the figures went on saying what they said.
