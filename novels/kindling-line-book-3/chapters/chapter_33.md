The stick was too tall. The ferrule caught on the third step down and Sol swore at it, a quiet hiss that bounced off the wet stone and came back sounding like a stranger's voice. She adjusted her grip. The wood was warm still, or warming, the grain worn smooth in a band three fingers wide where his palm had settled night after night. Her hand did not fit the groove. Her fingers were shorter, the calluses in different places, and the stick wanted to pivot in a way that made her wrist ache.

"Too tall," she said to it. "Too smooth. Too opinionated."

The stair drank the words. Two hundred and eleven steps, the count she knew in her knees before she knew it in her head. She had climbed it once with him, the first time, and he had counted aloud and she had matched him breath for breath. The rhythm had held them together when the air grew thin and the mineral tang thickened until she could taste it on her tongue.

She began to count now, the numbers falling into the dark.

"One. Two. Three."

Her voice was thin at first, swallowed by the spiral. She steadied it.

"Four. Five. Six."

The stick tapped a rhythm beside her, ferrule on stone, wood on iron rung where the stair narrowed. The lantern she had brought hung from a hook at her belt, its light a pale circle that moved with her. The flame did not flicker. The air down here did not move unless she moved it.

"Seventeen. Eighteen. Nineteen."

At twenty she felt the old habit settle in her shoulders. The count was a rope she could hold. At forty it was a prayer. At sixty she was smiling, a crooked thing that hurt her cheeks, because he had laughed at sixty the first time and said, "Only sixty? I counted eighty before my knees gave out," and she had said, "Your knees gave out at forty-two," and he had not argued.

"Eighty-nine. Ninety. Ninety-one."

The wall was wet under her free hand, cold slick stone that left a film on her fingertips. She trailed her fingers along it, counting by touch as much as by voice. The light caught mineral veins in the rock, silver threads in grey, and she thought of the diagram in its oilcloth, the dotted line running east off the paper toward the cliff.

"One hundred. One hundred one. One hundred two."

Halfway. The landing where the stair turned back on itself was a memory she had not reached yet. She paused, breathing, the stick vertical beside her. The lantern light showed the turn, the next flight dropping away into shadow.

"One hundred seventeen," she said, and then, in a voice that was not hers, "One hundred seventeen and a half."

She stopped. The echo returned the half-number, clear and mocking.

"Bad impression," she told the stair. "He is a bad liar. He would never say 'and a half.' He would say 'one seventeen point five' and look at me like I should have known."

The stair said nothing back. The lantern flame burned steady. She laughed, a short bark that surprised her, and then the laugh died in her throat because the silence after it was shaped exactly like him not answering.

"One hundred eighteen," she said. "One hundred nineteen. One hundred twenty."

The steps grew steeper. The air grew colder. Her thighs burned, a clean fire she welcomed. The stick took more of her weight with every flight, the ferrule finding purchase on stone worn hollow by generations of boots. Her mother's boots. Her own. The groove in the last step of the anchor chamber was deep enough to hold a heel, or a knee.

"Two hundred. Two hundred one. Two hundred two."

She said the numbers like she was placing stones in a wall. Each one solid. Each one final.

"Two hundred nine. Two hundred ten. Two hundred eleven."

The anchor chamber opened around her, a cylinder of black iron and wet stone. The plate sat at the north end, a disc of black iron set in the floor, the size of a wagon wheel. It did not glow. It did not hum. It waited.

She did not go to it.

She leaned the stick against the curved wall, the ferrule in the groove where the floor met the iron. Her hand stayed on the wood a moment longer than she meant, the warmth of it seeping into her palm. Then she let go and pressed her hand flat against the stone beside it, fingers spread, the cold biting into her palm, the wet soaking through skin.

She held it there. The stone did not warm. Her hand did not cool. The mark she left was a pale print on dark grey, already fading at the edges.

"Kael," she said.

The chamber swallowed the name. No echo came back, not even a ghost of one. The iron ate sound.

"Kael."

She said it again, louder this time, forcing the syllables into the air. They hung there, two beats of silence, then fell flat.

About thirty-three. She had said it herself on the stair, carrying his stick up behind him, flat as a figure in a ledger. Sixteen and a half. About thirty-three. Seventeen stored. The arithmetic that governed everything and explained nothing.

"About thirty-three," she said aloud, trying for his tone. The words came out wobbling, uncertain, a question she had not meant to ask. She tried again. "About thirty-three."

Worse. The number refused to be a figure. It curled around her tongue like a name.

She tried the other half of it, the way it was done at night. "Sixteen and a half," she said, and her own number came out steady, because it was hers and no one had ever asked it to be anything else. Then she waited, as she waited every night, for the second number to come back to her across the dark. None came. The chamber kept it.

She slid down the wall until she sat on the last step, the one with the groove worn deep. The stone was cold through her trousers. The plate faced her across the chamber, inert and patient. She had sat like this as a child in her mother's records room, small legs dangling from a stool too tall for her, watching her mother work and never once being told to leave. The plate had that same patience now, and the same refusal to look up.

The lantern at her belt threw her shadow long across the floor, a thin dark shape stretching toward the plate. She watched it, and watched the plate, and said the thing she had come down two hundred eleven steps to say.

"When the year tore out of you, for one heartbeat I was glad it was not me."

The words did not echo. They simply existed, spoken into the cold air, heavy as the stone beneath her.

"I am ashamed of that. I will not be less ashamed tomorrow. I said it to the room because the room will not leave. I said it to you because you are not here to forgive me."

She drew a breath. The mineral tang filled her lungs.

"One of the seventeen is my mother's. I decided to count it as mine. The leaf does not prove it. The carried hand leans right, and her hand leaned forward, and I decided anyway. I will not let you pay for me again. I cannot pull two pegs alone. I would not if I could."

The confession sat in the chamber with her and did not ask to be forgiven.

"The public step. I have been afraid since then that I would use you up. That the arithmetic would eat you and I would stand there counting heartbeats while you burned."

Her voice cracked on the last word. She pressed her hand harder against the stone, the cold sharp enough to cut.

"The plan failed because I relied on your habit being different. The fifth page said what you would do. It did not say what you would not. The only thing that works is to ask you aloud, in the room, and wait for your answer."

She did not know how to ask. She did not know what the answer would be. She knew only that the order lapsed when this day ended, and the gauge read seventeen, and the draw on Mara continued, and she was sitting on the last step of her mother's stair talking to a man who was not there.

A sound from above. The clank of a pot lid, the scrape of iron on stone, far up the stair. Ansa's pot, set down on a landing and left there. The smell of broth came down on air that did not move, carried by cold and habit.

She did not climb. She pulled her knees up, rested her forearms on them, and waited. The lantern flame leaned toward the plate, a slow bend in the still air. The stick leaned against the wall beside her, patient, warm, worn to the shape of a hand that was not hers.

She would eat on the stairs. She would not hurry. The day would end while she was down here, and she knew it.

She climbed twenty steps before the smell of broth won. The stone was hard under her palms, the iron rungs cold against her boots, and she stopped on a landing wide enough to sit. The pot waited there against the wall, out of the way of boots, steam rising thin and straight from under the lid. A bracket driven into the mortar generations back took the lantern hook, and light pooled around her, a small circle in the dark.

Ansa had not stayed. The old splicer knew better than to follow when Sol went to the anchor plate. She knew the count. She knew the habit. She had set the pot and gone, the way she had set food outside the records room door when Sol's mother worked past midnight and forgot to eat. "Eat," Ansa had said in the kitchen once, and had not said it twice. Sol heard it now in the weight of the lid.

She unstoppered the waterskin at her belt. The water was flat, tasting of the tin cup she had filled yesterday. She drank half and capped it, then lifted the lid with careful fingers. Thick, grey-brown, the fat risen in yellow eyes. Barley and root vegetables and what might have been dried mushroom. Ansa's hand. No meat. Meat cost years she did not have.

She drank from the pot, tilting it to her mouth because she had not brought a spoon. The heat spread through her chest, a small fire in the cold. She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand and set the pot down on the step beside her.

She knew what the day was. Bram had read the demand aloud under the burned ropewalk, and the custodian had added the rest, and she had not needed either of them twice: the order ran out when the day ended, and the pick would attend at the line. She had not seen the parchment. She had heard its words in the cold, with the pick named as a title only.

One ward-taker of the Council's roll.

The words meant nothing and everything. A body to stand at the line. A body to pay if the pegs pulled while Sol stood at the plate. A body that was not Kael and not her.

She pulled her knees up again. The broth settled warm in her stomach. The lantern flame burned steady. The stick leaned against the wall twenty steps below, patient, waiting.

She thought of the fifth page in his coat pocket. One line in his square hand, written eleven times before the words were right: that when he wanted to step in between her and the plate he would say so aloud first, and wait for her answer. She had read it unhanded and given it back.

He had not said it first. He had stepped in. The year had torn out of him before the word could form. His body had moved before his mind, before his honour, before the rule he had written in his own hand.

She understood. She had always understood. The body moved. The promise broke. The arithmetic shifted.

But the asking was the part that held her. Not the command. Not the order. The asking.

*I am at my mother's plate. Do not follow. I will be back. I am asking.*

She had asked him to stay. He had stayed. Joren had delivered the message. Yelva had tended the leg. The night had passed. The order's last day had dawned grey and cold over the Lower Reach.

She should go up. She should climb the two hundred and eleven steps and the lane and the gallery mouth and the cliff path. She should stand at the plate with the rope at her waist, and ask him aloud, and wait for his answer. She should hold the count and watch the plate wake and let the stored seventeen find her.

She did not move.

The pot sat beside her, cooling slowly. The broth skin thickened. The steam stopped rising.

She thought of the leaf, and of the two hands on it, one leaning forward and one leaning right, and of the choice she had made between them. She had said it aloud already. It did not weigh less for that.

Sixteen and a half. About thirty-three. Seventeen stored.

The stored seventeen would go to the nearest living body when the pegs were pulled. If the pull came while Kael was nearest, the years would flood into him. Thirty-three plus seventeen. Fifty. A number no body was made to carry.

She did not want fifty for him. She wanted the close to happen while she was nearest. She wanted the years, whatever they were, to find her and not him.

She wanted to stand at the plate and have him at the door and have the rope hold and the rig hold and the pegs pull clean and the line close with both of them whole.

She tried to picture his face when she asked, and got his left foot instead, in the sock she had turned the right way round for him, and the way he had watched it for a feeling that did not come. That was the face she would be asking. It would listen with its whole attention and still not know what its body meant to do.

The lantern flame stood straight in the still air. The mineral tang thickened on her tongue. The stone beneath her was cold and wet and eternal.

She ate the last of the broth from the pot, tilting it up, draining the dregs. Barley grains stuck to the metal. She licked them off her fingers.

The pot was empty. She set it down on the step. The metal rang faintly against stone.

She did not climb. She leaned her head back against the wall, the stone cold through her hair, and watched the lantern flame dance.

The order ran out when the day ended. She was already below when it did. She knew it and did not hurry.

On the stair the pot was still warm, and there was no one there but her.
