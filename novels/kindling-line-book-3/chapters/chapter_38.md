Morning light lay across the floorboards in a long bar. The pot of Ansa's tea had gone cold. Steam rose thin and straight from the spout and did not curl.

The trapdoor creaked.

Sol turned. Ansa stood at the top of the ladder, a wooden bowl in her hands, steam rising thick and fragrant. Her face was calm. Her eyes were on Sol.

"Eat," Ansa said.

Sol took the bowl. The broth was hot, salty, rich. She ate. The warmth spread through her chest, down her arms, into her fingers.

"It is good," she said.

---

The room over the tar-shop held the chill of early morning. Grey light stretched across bare boards. No table. No chairs. The hearth had gone cold hours ago. Renn's page lay folded on the floor where he had left it, the charcoal lines facing down.

Sol sat with her back against the wall. Her legs folded beneath her. Her hands rested on her knees, palms down, fingers loose. She wore her undyed coat, the collar turned up against the draught that slipped under the door.

Kael watched her from the doorway. His stick leaned against the jamb. His left leg throbhed with the dull ache that had become familiar, the bone-deep reminder of stairs and cold stone. He did not reach for the stick. He crossed the room in three slow steps and lowered himself against the opposite wall, his spine straight, his boots flat on the boards.

The space between them measured the length of the room.

"This is the plate," Sol said. Her voice was quiet. It did not echo. "One person. This much floor."

Kael tilted his head back against the plaster. "And this is the door. The far wall. The place where the map says I wait."

"That is much too far."

He smiled. The expression did not reach his eyes. "Is it."

"You look like a man waiting for a ferry. The kind that runs once a week and leaves without you."

His smile widened. A fraction. "Then I had better move."

He shifted. One boot heel dragged on the boards. He stopped. "Here?"

She studied him. Her head tilted. The light caught the bridge of her nose, the curve of her upper lip. "No."

He dragged the heel again. A hand's breadth. "Here?"

"No."

Again. The same measured distance. "Here?"

"No."

He paused. His breath came slow. The boards were cold through his trousers. "And here?"

She was silent for a long moment. Her eyes moved over his face, his shoulders, the line of his thighs pressed to the floor. "That is the other kind of far."

He frowned. "Which kind is that."

"The kind where you are almost near enough to be nearest. And the kind you are not allowed."

The words hung in the cold air. Kael felt them settle in his chest, heavy and quiet. He looked at the space between his boots and her knees. Three floorboards. A hand's breadth of dust.

"I will stop where the map says," he said. "And not a step nearer."

She nodded once. "I know. And I want you to say it with your body now. So you can feel where the line is."

He did not move. He held the position. The muscles in his thighs began to tremble, a fine vibration he could not stop. The cold from the floor seeped upward. His left leg ached, a sharp spike that travelled from hip to ankle. He breathed through it. The tremor spread to his hands, braced on his knees.

It was hard in a way he had not expected. Like holding a hand over a candle. The burn that comes not from flame but from the knowledge of it.

Sol watched. She did not reach for him. She did not speak. Her eyes tracked the shake in his fingers, the tightness in his jaw, the way his breath hitched on the seventh count.

He held it. He held it until the tremor became a constant hum, until the cold was the only thing he could feel, until the space between them became a measure he carried in his bones.

Then she moved.

She crawled across the boards on her hands and knees. The coat bunched at her shoulders. Her hair fell forward, a dark curtain. She stopped with her head on his knee, her cheek pressing into the wool of his trousers.

"Now nobody is nearer," she said.

The words vibrated against his leg. Kael's hand found her hair without thought. His fingers sank into the thick strands. The strands were cool. His palm was warm. He rested there, the weight of his hand holding her down, holding himself still.

They stayed that way. The light shifted. The boards warmed where their bodies touched. The hum from the passage below rose and fell, a low vibration felt in the ribs.

He would like to be the person who sits in the right place because he has been told, he said. Not because he has been made.

She lifted her head. Her eyes were bright in the grey light. "That is the only reason it counts."

---

The trapdoor creaked again. Renn's head appeared, his face pale, the ink stains on his knuckles dark against the grey light. Ulla followed, her movements careful, one hand on the ladder rail, the other on his good shoulder.

Kael did not move his hand from Sol's hair. She did not lift her head.

"Morning," Renn said. His voice was rough. The cracked wrist was bound in grey cloth, the fingers curled stiff against his chest. "The map is ready."

Sol slid back to her place against the wall. She settled her hands on her knees again. "Show us."

Renn reached the floor. Ulla steadied him. He crossed to the center of the room and unfolded the page. The paper was thick, the edges frayed. The back was covered in his small level hand, lines and angles and measurements pressed deep into the fibre.

He laid it flat on the boards. The charcoal did not smudge.

Kael rose. The stick was in his hand before he thought. He planted it, the ferrule biting the boards. His left leg took the weight. The ache flared and settled.

He looked down at the map.

The second stair. The turn. The bare iron door. The plate. Two sockets, one set in the lintel above the plate and one in the sill below it, a man's height apart. The cords ran up through the lintel and the sill to the turn of the stair and on to the pullers.

"The foot of the stair has room for one person to sit at the plate and no more," Renn said. His good hand traced the line. "The next nearest place is three steps up. The pullers' place is above the turn, further than any person could reach the plate."

Kael studied the drawing. The precision of it. The care. "The map is made so that nobody but the one who sits can be nearest."

"Correct."

Renn's finger tapped the sockets. "The sockets take the pegs only if both are set in the same breath. And each peg is pulled by the cord and not by the hand."

Odo and Tessa were not in the room. The map would go to them next. Kael felt the certainty settle in his shoulders, the same certainty that had held him on the floor boards.

"The door of the passage at the head of the stair," he said. "That is my place. Further from the plate than Sol on any count."

He spoke aloud, to the room, to the map, to the woman against the wall. "I will be there. And I will not move till she asks."

Renn rolled the page. The charcoal lines disappeared into the fold. His good hand closed on the cylinder. "Now nobody can be nearer. Unless they are carried in."

Renn tucked the cylinder under his bound wrist. Ulla's hand stayed on his shoulder, her thumb pressing into the cloth of his sleeve.

"Odo and Tessa need to see it," Sol said.

Renn nodded. "They are in the yard. With the rig."

Kael moved toward the trapdoor. His stick tapped the boards. The rhythm was different now — slower, deliberate, each placement measured. He paused at the ladder. Sol fell into step behind him. The trapdoor stood open, the grey light from the yard spilling down the rungs.

They descended into the boiling-house cellar. The air was warm, mineral-thick, the hum louder here, a physical pressure against the eardrums. The Upper Iron Door stood at the far end, its enamelled knot catching the lamplight from the wall brackets. Kael had not been this close to it since the night Mara fell. The memory pressed at his ribs — the swell, the cry, the cold that had taken her wrists in hours.

He did not stop at the door. He continued past it, down the second stair. The steps were iron, bolted to the cliff face, each tread worn smooth by generations of boots. The wall wept moisture. The air grew warmer with every step.

The turn came at the twenty-third step. Kael counted them without meaning to. The stair bent left, opening onto a narrow landing. The bare iron door sat there, the plate set into its face, black and warm. Two sockets — one in the lintel above, one in the sill below. A man's height apart. Exactly as the map showed.

Odo and Tessa waited at the landing. They had come down while Kael and Sol were on the floor above. Odo's broad frame filled the turn. Tessa leaned against the cliff wall, her chalk-line reel silent at her belt.

Odo unfolded the map. His fingers were careful on the paper. He read. He folded it. He unfolded it again. He read a second time. His eyes moved from the drawing to the sockets, to the plate, to the turn of the stair behind him.

"Clean," he said. "The geometry holds."

Tessa took the map from him. She read once. She read again. Her finger traced the cord path from the sockets up through the lintel and sill, along the stair wall to the turn, onward to the pullers' place above.

"The cords run clean," she said. "No snag points. The pegs seat in the same breath or they do not seat at all."

"Both pegs pulled by the cord," Odo said. "Not by the hand. That is the rule."

"The rule is the map," Tessa said. She handed the cylinder back to Renn, who had followed them down, Ulla a step behind.

Renn did not speak. He took the map. His good hand closed on it.

Kael looked at the plate. The black iron was warm. He could feel the heat radiating from it, a low pulse that matched the hum in the walls. The sockets were dark holes in the iron, the lintel one higher than his head, the sill one at his shin.

"The cords," he said. "They run from the sockets to the turn. The pullers stand above the turn."

"Correct," Odo said. "The eyes are not set yet. We set them now. On your instruction."

Kael looked at the stair wall. The iron was pitted, streaked with mineral deposits. He traced the path the cords would take — up from the lintel socket, across the landing ceiling, down the stair wall to the turn, then up the passage wall to the pullers' anchor points.

"Two eyes on the landing ceiling," he said. "One for each cord. Two eyes on the stair wall at the turn. One for each cord. The cords must not cross. They must not rub."

Odo nodded. He uncoiled a length of rope from his pack. Tessa produced a hand drill, the bit blackened with use.

They worked in silence. The drill bit sang against iron. Dust fell. Odo set the eyes, each one driven deep, the threads biting into metal that had held for generations. Tessa fed the cords through, her fingers quick, the chalk-line marking each pass.

Kael watched. His stick rested against the cliff wall. His left leg throbhed, a steady beat that matched the hum. He did not reach for the stick. He stood at the turn, his weight balanced, his breath slow.

This was easier than the floor. He knew where to stand because the map told him. The map told him the pullers' place was above the turn, further than any person could reach the plate. The map told him the foot of the stair held room for one person at the plate and no more. The map told him the next nearest place was three steps up.

His body had argued on the floorboards. His muscles had trembled, his breath had hitched, his mind had counted the distance between his boots and Sol's knees. Here, on the landing, his body knew. The map had settled the argument before it began.

He was the puller. He stood at the turn. The plate was twenty-three steps below him, behind the bare iron door. The cords ran from the sockets to the eyes he watched Odo set. The pegs — the ledge peg and the table peg — would seat in those sockets. The pull would come from here, from above the turn, from a place further than any person could reach the plate.

Sol would sit at the plate. She would be the nearest living body.

The thought did not tremble in him. It settled.

"Done," Odo said. He coiled the excess rope. The cords ran clean, taut lines from the sockets to the turn. "The eyes are set. The cords are run. The rig above is ready."

Tessa capped her chalk. "The count is Sol's. The pull is ours. We know the rhythm."

Kael looked down the stair. The plate was a dark warmth in the lamplight. The sockets were black holes. The cords vanished into the ceiling eyes.

"Good," he said.

They climbed back up. The twenty-three steps. The Upper Iron Door. The cellar. The trapdoor. The room over the tar-shop.

Ansa was gone. The hearth was cold. The tea pot sat empty on the floor.

Ulla sat at the table. She had a ledger open, her pen moving slow across the page. She looked up as they entered.

"The surveyors' commission," she said. "One line in the record. The map is entered. The sockets are noted. The nearest-body test is documented."

She did not look at Renn. She did not look at Sol. Her eyes stayed on the page.

"Thank you," Sol said.

Ulla's pen stopped. She capped it. "The record is not for the Council. It is not for Thorne. It is for the line."

She rose. Her chair scraped. She crossed to Renn, her hand finding his shoulder again.

"Come," she said. "The wrist needs looking at."

Renn did not argue. He rose slow, the cylinder tucked against his chest. He paused at the door, his good hand on the jamb.

"The map stays with you," he said to Kael. "The sockets are set. The cords are run. The pegs are ready. The pullers are placed. The nearest body is defined."

He left. Ulla followed. The trapdoor creaked shut behind them.

Sol and Kael stood alone in the room. The grey light had shifted, shorter now, the bar across the floorboards narrowed.

Kael planted his stick. He looked at the space where they had sat, the floorboards worn by knees and palms. He looked at the trapdoor. He looked at Sol.

"It is easier," he said. "To stand where the map says. Than where the body says."

She nodded. "The map does not tremble."

"No. It does not."

He waited. The silence stretched. The hum from the passage rose and fell.

"Will you stay?" he asked.

She was quiet. One heartbeat. Two. Three.

"Yes."

He opened his door. She followed him in.
