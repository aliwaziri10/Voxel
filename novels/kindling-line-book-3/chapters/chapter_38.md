Early light lay across the floorboards of the room over the tar-shop in a long grey bar. The pot of Ansa's tea had gone cold on the table, and the window held the first thin noises of the lane waking: a cart, a door, the knock of a mallet in the shop below. Sol had pushed the table against the wall and stacked the stools beneath it, so that the floor lay bare from the bed to the door.

Kael stood in the doorway with his stick leaning against the jamb. His left leg throbbed with the dull ache that had become familiar, the bone-deep reminder of stairs and cold stone. He had watched her clear the room without asking why, and he had not asked, because she had told him the night before that she wished to know what a map would feel like before she saw one.

Sol sat on the boards with her back against the wall. Her legs were folded beneath her. Her hands rested on her knees, palms down, fingers loose. She wore her undyed coat with the collar turned up against the draught that slipped under the door.

He did not reach for the stick. He crossed the room in three slow steps and lowered himself against the opposite wall, his spine straight, his boots flat on the boards.

The space between them measured the length of the room.

"This is the plate," Sol said. Her voice was quiet. It did not echo. "One person. This much floor."

Kael tilted his head back against the plaster. "And this is the door. The far wall. The place where the map says I wait."

"That is much too far."

He smiled. The expression did not reach his eyes. "Is it."

"You look like a man waiting for a ferry. The one that runs once a week and leaves without you."

His smile widened. A fraction. "Then I had better move."

He shifted. One boot heel dragged on the boards. He stopped. "Here?"

She studied him. Her head tilted. The light caught the bridge of her nose, the curve of her upper lip. "No."

He dragged the heel again. A hand's breadth. "Here?"

"No."

Again. The same measured distance. "Here?"

"No."

He paused. His breath came slow. The boards were cold through his trousers. "And here?"

She was silent for a long moment. Her eyes moved over his face, his shoulders, the line of his thighs pressed to the floor. "That is the other far."

He frowned. "Which is that."

"The one where you are almost near enough to be nearest. And not allowed."

The words hung in the cold air. Kael felt them settle in his chest, heavy and quiet. He looked at the space between his boots and her knees. Three floorboards. A hand's breadth of dust.

"I will stop where the map says," he said. "And not a step nearer."

She nodded once. "I know. And I want you to say it with your body now. So you can feel where the line is."

He did not move. He held the position. The muscles in his thighs began to tremble, a fine vibration he could not stop. The cold from the floor seeped upward. His left leg ached, a sharp spike that travelled from hip to ankle. He breathed through it. The tremor spread to his hands, braced on his knees.

It was hard in a way he had not expected. Like holding a hand over a candle. The burn that comes not from the flame but from the knowledge of it.

Sol watched. She did not reach for him. She did not speak. Her eyes tracked the shake in his fingers, the tightness in his jaw, the way his breath hitched on the seventh count.

He held it. He held it until the tremor became a constant hum in his own body, until the cold was the only thing he could feel, until the space between them became a measure he carried in his bones.

Then she moved.

She crawled across the boards on her hands and knees. The coat bunched at her shoulders. Her hair fell forward, a dark curtain. She stopped with her head on his knee, her cheek pressing into the wool of his trousers.

"Now nobody is nearer," she said.

The words vibrated against his leg. Kael's hand found her hair without thought. His fingers sank into the thick strands. The strands were cool. His palm was warm. He rested there, the weight of his hand holding her down, holding himself still.

They stayed that way. The light shifted. The boards warmed where their bodies touched. Down in the shop the mallet knocked at hemp and stopped.

"I would like to be the person who sits in the right place because I have been told," he said. "Not because I have been made."

She lifted her head. Her eyes were bright in the grey light. "That is the only reason it counts."

---

Feet sounded on the stair, one pair slow and one light. At the sixth tread the board gave, and a man's voice said a word under its breath. Kael did not move his hand from Sol's hair. She did not lift her head until the knock came, three raps, and Ulla's voice said, "It is us."

"Come in," Sol said, and sat up, and Kael took his hand away and laid it on his knee.

Renn came in first, his face pale, the ink dark in the creases of his knuckles. Ulla followed with one hand on his good shoulder. The cracked wrist was bound in grey cloth, the fingers curled stiff against his chest, and under the bound arm he carried the page, rolled.

"Morning," Renn said. His voice was rough. "The map is ready."

"Show us," Sol said.

Ulla steadied him as he lowered himself to the boards beside the bare floor where they had played at distance. He unrolled the page and laid it flat. The paper was thick and its edges frayed. The back was covered in his small level hand, lines and angles and measurements pressed deep into the fiber. He weighted the corners with a boot, a cup, the heel of Kael's stick, and the charcoal did not smudge.

Kael rose. The stick was in his hand before he thought of it. His left leg took the weight, the ache flared and settled, and he looked down at the map.

The second stair. The turn. The bare iron door. The plate. Two sockets, one set in the lintel above the plate and one in the sill below it, a man's height apart. The cords ran up through the lintel and the sill to the turn of the stair and on to the pullers.

"The foot of the stair has room for one person to sit at the plate and no more," Renn said. His good hand traced the line. "The next nearest place is three steps up. The pullers' place is above the turn, further than any person could reach the plate."

Kael studied the drawing. The precision of it. The care. "The map is made so that nobody but the one who sits can be nearest."

"Correct."

Renn's finger tapped the sockets. "I will say the one thing plainly. The sockets take the pegs only if both are set in the same breath. And each peg is pulled by the cord and not by the hand."

Sol looked at the two small circles for a long moment. She did not say that she had a ledge peg and he a table peg, and that the whole of it came to this: two hands that had carried them, and two cords that would do what the hands must not. She laid her palm flat on the boards beside the page and did not touch it.

Feet sounded again on the stair, heavier, and the board gave at the sixth tread. Odo came in ducking the lintel, with Tessa at his shoulder and a coil of cord over his arm. Corren stood behind them on the landing and did not come in. "Joren has the gate," he said, to nobody, and went down again.

Odo read the map. He read it twice. He did not ask to hold it. He went down on one knee beside the boards, with his broad face unreadable, and moved his eyes from the sockets to the turn to the pullers' place and back. Tessa read it once, and then again, her lips moving a little, her finger a hand's breadth above the charcoal tracing the path of each cord.

"The cords run clean," she said. "Nothing crosses. Nothing rubs."

"Both pulled by the cord," Odo said. "Not by the hand. That is the rule, and the map makes it a place instead of a rule." He sat back on his heel. "A puller does not move until the count is done. I said it in the yard. It is easier to keep a rule when the floor agrees with it."

"The door of the passage at the head of the stair," Kael said. "That is my place. Further from the plate than Sol on any count."

He spoke aloud, to the room, to the map, to the woman who had sat against the wall and put her head on his knee. "I will be there. And I will not move until she asks."

Sol looked at him. She did not answer at once, and he saw that she was giving him the three heartbeats he had given her, and that she was giving them to be kind.

"Then we go and look at it," she said. "All of us who are to stand there. Renn, you will stay."

"I will stay," Renn said. "I was promised." His eyes went briefly to Ulla and back. "I have drawn it from the sealed page and from what I remember. I would not like to see how close I came."

"Nobody will make you," said Sol.

---

They went down by the cellar of the boiling house: Sol first with the lamp, because she had asked to carry it and he had said, aloud, that he would prefer to carry it himself, and had then let her. Kael came after her with the stick, Odo behind him, Tessa last with the cord. The air grew warm and mineral-thick. The hum came up against the ears as a pressure and not a sound. The upper iron door stood at the end of the cellar with its enameled knot catching the lamplight, and Kael did not look at it for longer than a step. He had not been this close since the night Mara was taken. The memory pressed at his ribs: the call, the cold, the line closing over a woman who had run to a name.

He did not stop. He went down the second stair behind the lamp, the stick finding each tread before his foot did. The steps were iron bolted to the cliff, worn smooth by generations of boots. The wall wept moisture. The air grew warmer with every step.

The turn came where the stair bent left and the wall widened to a narrow landing. Below it the stair ran on down to its foot, and at the foot, in the lamplight, the bare iron door stood with the plate set at its base, black and warm. It was all there as the page had drawn it. The lintel above the plate. The sill beneath it. Between them a space for one person to sit, and no more.

Kael stopped at the turn. He did not go further, though the stair did. He stood with his weight on the stick and his breath slow, and let himself look at it.

Sol came to stand at his left side, where she had learned to walk. She held the lamp low so that its light fell on the stair and not on his face. "Would you like to go down one step further?" she asked. "You may say no."

He felt the answer rise in him before she had finished the sentence, and felt it for what it was, an old habit with its boots already on. He let it stand there. One heartbeat. Two. Three.

"No," he said. "I would like to look at it from here. This is where the map puts me, near enough, and I would like to know what it feels like to stay."

"And what does it feel like?"

"Like a very long breath held in the right place."

She nodded and did not say that it was good. She stood beside him a while longer with the lamp, and then she said, in a voice he would not have heard from the stair above, "Thank you for waiting."

"Two eyes on the landing wall," he said at last. "One for each cord. Two more at the turn, one for each. The cords must not cross. They must not rub."

"On your word," Odo said. He uncoiled a length of rope from his pack. Tessa produced a hand drill, the bit blackened with use, and they went to the wall together.

They worked in silence. The drill sang against the iron. Dust fell and was blown away by the draught that came up from the foot. Odo set each eye and tried it with his full weight, and said "Clean" twice, once for each trying. Tessa fed the cords through, her fingers quick, the chalk-line marking each pass on the wall in a small neat tick.

Kael watched. His body, which had argued on the bare boards of the room that morning, was quiet here. He knew where he stood because the map had told him. Above the turn was the pullers' place. Above that, at the head of the stair, was the door of the passage, and that door was his, and it was further from the plate than Sol would ever be. The map had settled the argument before it began.

It was easier. He had not expected that. His legs had trembled on the floor for a woman who sat three boards away and asked nothing. Here, where the plate itself lay in plain sight at the foot of the stair, he felt the want arrive, and the map answered it, and the want went and sat down.

Sol would sit at the plate. She would be the nearest living body. The thought did not tremble in him. It settled.

"Done," Odo said. He coiled the excess. The cords ran taut from the eyes up the stair wall toward the turn. "The eyes are set. The cords are run. The rig is ready."

Tessa capped her chalk. "The count is Sol's. The pull is ours. We know the rhythm."

Kael looked down the stair once more. The plate was a dark warmth in the lamplight, and the lintel and the sill showed as two black edges. Then he turned and climbed. The stick found each tread, and Sol went ahead with the lamp, and Odo and Tessa came last, talking low about the cords as people talk who have a good day's work behind them and do not wish to say so. At the top of the cellar stair Odo stopped to read the gauge in the wall of the hut, and read it again, and said "Seventeen. Fixed," to the air.

---

The room was as they had left it. Renn sat on the boards beside the page with his back against the leg of the table, and Ulla beside him with a ledger open on her knee and a pen in her hand. She looked up as they came in. The grey bar of light across the floor had shortened and gone pale.

"One line in the record," she said. She did not look at Renn, or at Sol. "The surveyors' commission has the map. It is entered. It is not for the Council. It is not for Thorne. It is for the line."

"Thank you," said Sol.

Odo set his coil on the table, and Tessa wiped the chalk from her palms onto her coat. Neither of them said what all four had seen at the foot of that stair: a room that held one person and no more, and the whole of the line waiting in it.

Ulla capped the pen. "He will want to rest the wrist."

Renn did not argue. He gathered the page from the boards with his good hand, slow and careful, and rolled it against his chest. The charcoal lines went into the fold one by one: the stair, the turn, the door, the two sockets. He looked at Sol, and then at Kael, and then at the place on the floor where the two of them had sat.

"Now nobody can be nearer," he said. "Unless they are carried in."
