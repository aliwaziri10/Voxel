The wall kept the last of the day's heat in its stones, and Sol sat on it with her legs hanging over the boiling-house yard and the low sun on her back. Below her the stand-in frame leaned where the techs had left it, the board and the lamp and the sandbag all quiet. At the edge of the yard Tessa was coiling a cord over her knee, winding it in even turns, the chalk-line swinging at her belt. Across the far side the Thorne guard stood in their line, grey cloaks, straight backs, facing the east approach. Nobody over there had spoken since morning. Nobody had touched anybody.

Joren had sent word at midday that Mara meant to walk to the yard before dark, and that Yelva had stopped saying no only because she had run out of ways to say it. So they had come out to wait on the wall. Kael sat beside her with his stick laid across the stones at his hip. When Tessa slung the coil over her shoulder and went out by the lane gate with a nod for both of them, he leaned back on his hands until his weight settled against Sol's knees.

"They have not moved all day," she said, with her chin toward the far line.

"They have been told to stand," Kael said. "It is a skill. I would not like to have to learn it."

"You stand very well."

"I stand where there is a rule. They stand where there is a gate. It is different."

She found tar-dust in his hair. She said so.

"It lives there now," he said.

"Then I will evict it. May I?"

She asked it aloud, because the rule held for small things too. He counted three heartbeats before he said, "Yes," and she could hear that he had enjoyed the count.

She shifted along the wall until she sat behind him with a knee on either side of his hips, and took the first strand between finger and thumb. The dust came away in black grains that she let fall on the stone. One strand, then the next. His hair was dark where the sun had it and grey where it did not, and the tar had got into both.

"This is the first time," he said, "that anyone has looked for anything in my hair that was not grey."

"There is plenty of grey. I am sorting it."

"And how does the sorting go?"

"The grey is coming in handsomely on the left."

He turned his head far enough that she saw the edge of his smile, and the white thread above his ear. "It is the left side of my head that has been listening to you."

"That is not how listening works."

"It is how I work."

"Do not ask me to leave any in. You would only count them."

"I would count them twice," he said, "and report."

She laughed before she could stop it, and the laugh went out across the yard. The nearest man of the Thorne line did not turn his head, but she thought his shoulders eased a little. She bent to the next strand. For a while there was only the small work of it, the grit between her fingers, the warm stone under her knees, his back loosening by degrees against her. The yard smelled of hot tar going cold and of dust, and the sky over the cliff had turned the pale brass it turned before it gave up. Her fingers came to the white thread above his ear, and she stopped there. It had no dust in it. She did not comb it, and she did not ask herself why she had stopped. She laid it back where it lay and went on to the dark hair behind.

"I want to ask you one thing," she said. "I want you to be very honest with me, and I will wait after, the full count."

"Ask."

"On the last night. Do you want to be at the door? Or are you agreeing to the door because it is your place?"

He did not answer. She kept her fingers in his hair and counted, one, two, three, and let the count run on to a fourth because the waiting was harder than she had told him it would be. A swallow went over the yard, low, and up the cliff.

"I do not want to be at the door," Kael said. His voice was level, and he did not turn round. "I want to be at the plate. I know exactly why I cannot. The door is the thing I will do."

She had thought he might say that it was his place. She had braced for it, and the plain answer went past her guard and struck low under the ribs. She laid her cheek against the back of his neck. His skin was warm and smelled of tar and wool and the cold stair.

"That is the true answer," she said. "It is also a terrible one."

"Yes."

"I am glad you said it."

"I am not glad. But I said it."

"I will not ask you to say it again," she said. "I will not pretend I did not hear it, either."

"I did not want you to pretend."

He lifted his head a little. "Mara will come with a thing to say to me. I think I know what it is. I would like to hear it standing."

"Then stand. I will be on your left."

"You are always on my left."

"It is where you listen best."

Her arms went round his shoulders and her chin came down on top of his head, and his hands came up and closed over her wrists, slowly, the rough palms steady. He did not hold hard. He held the way a man holds a rail on a stair he does not trust, to know where it is. The yard was darkening. The stones began to give up their warmth. Beyond the wall a bell or a gate rang once, a single flat note, and did not ring again.

"I would like to spend the whole next season," she said into his hair, "combing things out of this."

His thumbs moved over the inside of her wrists, back and forth. "I would like to spend it letting you."

There was a step on the cobbles by the lane gate, and then a second step, with the tap of a stick between them. Neither of them moved for one more breath. Then Sol lifted her chin, and Kael let go of her wrists, and she slid down from the wall into the dusk.

---

Mara came through the gate between Joren and Corren, one on each side of her, and she did not lean on either of them. Corren kept a hand an inch from her elbow and was not allowed to close it. She carried a stick of her own in her free hand, a plain length of ash, and set it down ahead of every step as if she meant to make the cobbles answer for it. She had put on the Ashworth coat. Her back was straight and her chin was level. Her face was grey, not pale but grey, like the cloaks at the far side of the yard, the skin drawn close over the bone. The sleeves were buttoned to the wrist, and Sol knew without asking that they were buttoned so nobody would see how far the grey had climbed.

"She walked from Yelva's door," Corren said to Sol, low, as he passed. "The whole way. We did not carry her. She would not have it."

Yelva came a pace behind with her bag on her shoulder and her mouth set flat.

"I told her to stay on the bed," she said, to Sol, to the wall, to the sky. "I told her twice, and a third time at the door of my own house. I have said it. I will not say it again."

"No," Mara said. "You will not. Thank you, Yelva."

Yelva folded her arms and stopped where she was, three paces back, and did not say it again.

Mara halted on the cobbles below the wall. Her eyes went once across the yard to the cellar stair and the dark of its mouth, and came back. The low light lay along the ash stick and showed every flaw in the grain, the knots and the long scar of an old split bound with wire. Close to, Sol could see the cold in her. The knuckles round the stick were the color of old candle wax, and the hand that held it shook once, very finely, and was still. Joren and Corren stood to either side and looked at nothing. Kael had got his stick and stood, and Sol came to his left, where she had learned to walk, and the three of them faced one another in the last of the light.

"Tell me the day the line will close," Mara said.

Sol had promised to tell her, and had not known how it would feel to say it to a face. "Four nights from now," she said.

Mara nodded once, a small, controlled motion. "Then I will be at the cellar mouth. Not at the door of the passage. I do not wish to be carried."

Sol wanted to say that nobody would carry her. She said, "At the cellar mouth," and gave Mara her own words back whole.

Mara turned her head. "Kael."

He stood with both hands on the stick. "Mara."

"I have wanted to say a thing to you since the fourth. I would have run for your voice again." She waited for his face to change, and when it did not, she went on. "You called. I ran. That is what being called is. It is not a thing to forgive."

Sol watched him. She had seen him argue with a map and with a rule and with his own two legs, and she had braced for it now, the quick denial, the guilt put back on his own shoulders where he kept it. He did not argue. He stood with his eyes on Mara's face and let the words reach him. Nothing in him moved but the muscle of his throat. After a long moment he bowed his head, once, the way a man bows to take a weight he has stopped trying to set down, and he said nothing at all.

"He hears you," Sol said.

"I know he does," said Mara. "That is why I said it to his face and not to yours."

Mara looked at him a breath longer. Then she looked at Sol. "May I see the clasp again?"

Sol put her hand inside her coat and drew the clasp out on its cord. Drop, feather, flame, the three strands worn smooth. She laid it on her open palm and held it out, and Mara took it and closed her fingers over it and did not speak.

The light went on going. At the far gate a guard lit a lamp and set it on the ground, and the line stood about it without a word, grey against the grey of the cliff. Yelva shifted her bag. She was waiting too, three paces back, but her waiting had an edge to it, like a knife kept in a sleeve. Mara only stood, with the clasp shut in her fist and her weight on the ash stick, and the stillness of her was not the stillness of a woman who had run out of strength. It was a thing she knew how to do. Sol thought of twenty years of being put where she was needed and staying put, at a gate, at a stair foot, at the mouth of a cellar. Mara could wait the way a post waits, with her whole length. Sol could not. She paced rooms and counted stairs and made every hour carry a load. She had counted the cord turns on Tessa's knee that afternoon, and the heartbeats under her own ribs on the wall, and now she stood in a yard with her hands open at her sides and nothing in it to count, and she felt the lack like a draught from an open door. Mara had no such draught in her. Whatever the cold had taken from her, it had not taken that. She did not reach for the clasp. She let her hands hang and learned, for the length of that long silence, how little she had practiced.

Mara opened her hand. The clasp lay on her palm, and her thumb lifted from the middle strand.

"My hands are warmer when you are near," she said.

That was all. She did not say what it meant, and Sol did not ask. It was not offered as a rule. It was a line, said once, and left lying in the air between them. Sol had not known she had stepped closer until she saw that the half pace of cobble between them was gone. She took the clasp and its cord and put them back inside her coat, where they lay against her breastbone, warm from Mara's hand.

Kael had not moved. His shoulder was a finger's width from hers. She felt him breathe out.

Joren and Corren closed in on either side, and Mara turned to go. "Joren and Corren," Kael said, aloud, to the air between them, and Joren, without turning his head, said, "We have her."

Yelva fell in behind them, her eyes on Mara's grey face. The four of them crossed the cobbles toward the lane gate, the tap of the ash stick keeping its slow time. Sol listened to it go. Every third step the stick lagged by a breath, and Mara waited out the lag without a pause in her face, and set it down again. The Thorne line did not turn its head to watch them pass.

At the gate Mara stopped, her hand on Joren's arm, and looked back over her shoulder. Her voice carried the length of the yard.

"Tell me when."

"Four nights," Sol said. "I will tell you."
