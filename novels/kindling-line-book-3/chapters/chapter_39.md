The wall held the last of the day's heat in its stones. Sol sat with her legs dangling, the boiling-house yard spread below them — Odo and Tessa gone quiet with the cords, the stand-in frame still against the dust, the chalk-line coiled at Tessa's belt. The air smelled of tar and mineral dust and the yeast-sour tang of Ansa's bread from the morning. Long shadows stretched across the cobbles. The Thorne guard stood in line at the far gate, grey cloaks, straight spines, the copper clasp of the works steward catching the low light.

Kael leaned back. His weight settled against her knees. The stick rested across the wall beside him, forgotten.

Sol found tar-dust in his hair. She said so.

"It lives there now," he said.

She shifted. Sat behind him with her knees either side of his hips. Her fingers found the first strand, dark against grey, and worked the dust free. One strand. Then another. The granules fell to the wall, black specks on warm stone.

"This is the first time," he said, "anyone has looked for anything in my hair that was not grey."

"There is plenty of grey," she said. "I am sorting it."

"The grey is coming in handsomely on the left."

He turned his head. The profile caught the dusk — the line of his jaw, the shadow under his cheekbone where the stick had pressed the night before, the white strand threading above his ear. "It is the left side of my head that has been listening to you."

"That is not how listening works."

"It is how I work."

Her fingers paused on a strand at his temple. The humour settled, warm and quiet. She combed the dust free. The strand slipped through her fingers, clean.

"One thing," she said. "I would like you to be very honest with me about one thing. Whether you want to be at the door on the last night, or whether you are only agreeing because it is your place."

He went still. The wall held them both. The yard noise had faded to the hum from the passage below, a low vibration felt in the ribs.

"I do not want to be at the door," he said. "I want to be at the plate. And I know exactly why I cannot. The door is the thing I will do."

The words landed. Sol felt them in her own chest, heavy and true. She laid her cheek on the back of his neck. The skin was warm. The hair at his nape was soft, fine, grey at the roots.

"That is the true answer," she said. "And it is also a terrible one."

Her arms came round his shoulders. Her chin rested on his head. His hands found her wrists, fingers closing slow, the palms rough, the grip steady. The yard darkened. The Thorne guard did not move. The gate was silent.

Somewhere a bell rang once. Or the gate. It did not repeat.

"I would like to spend the whole next season combing things out of your hair," she said.

His thumbs traced slow arcs on the inside of her wrists. "I would like to spend it letting you."

A footstep on the stones. Neither of them moved for one more breath.

---

Mara came through the gate on Joren's arm and Corren's, upright, grey in the face, a stick of her own in her free hand. Yelva followed, her bag across her shoulder, her mouth set.

The Thorne guard did not shift. The works steward's copper clasp caught the last light, three knotted strands, and did not move.

Mara stopped at the edge of the cobbles where the east approach began. The passage mouth yawned behind her, dark and breathing. She looked at Sol. Her eyes were clear, the colour of the river in winter.

"Tell me the day the line will close," Mara said.

Sol's fingers tightened on Kael's wrists. She felt him go still beneath her hands.

"Four nights from now," Sol said.

Mara nodded. The motion was small, controlled. "I will be at the cellar mouth. Not the door of the passage. I do not want to be carried."

She turned to Kael. Her face did not soften.

"I have wanted to say to you since the fourth that I would have run for your voice again. You called. I ran. That is what being called is. It is not a thing to forgive."

Kael did not speak. His hands stayed on Sol's wrists. His throat moved. The stick lay forgotten on the wall.

Mara held his gaze a moment longer. Then she looked at Sol. "May I see the clasp again?"

Sol released Kael. She reached into her coat, found the leather cord, drew it free. The clasp lay in her palm — drop, feather, flame, the strands worn smooth. She held it out.

Mara took it. Her fingers closed. She held it a long moment, the grey light catching the metal. Her thumb traced the feather strand.

"Your hands are warmer when you are near," she said.

Sol did not answer. The words sat in the cooling air, neither mechanics nor comfort, only her line.

Mara opened her hand. The clasp rested on her palm. She gave it back.

Sol took it. The metal was warm. She fastened the cord at her throat, the drop settling in the hollow.

Mara turned at the gate on Joren's arm. The Thorne guard fell in behind her, a wall of grey. Yelva walked at her shoulder, the bag swinging.

"Tell me when," Mara said. Her voice carried back across the cobbles.

Sol's hand found Kael's wrist again. Her fingers settled on the pulse.

"Four nights," she said. "I will tell you."

The words hung between them. The Thorne guard marched. Mara's stick tapped the cobbles, a slow rhythm, each step measured. Joren and Corren moved with her, one on each side, their pace matched to hers. Yelva walked at the rear, her bag swinging, her eyes on Mara's face.

Sol watched until the grey cloaks disappeared through the gate. The yard swallowed them. The hum from the passage rose, fell, rose again.

She did not move. Her hand stayed on Kael's wrist. The pulse beneath her fingers was steady, slower than her own.

"He did not argue," she said.

Kael turned his head. His profile cut the dusk. "No."

"She gave you the thing you have carried since the fourth. And you let it land."

"It was not mine to deflect."

The wall was cold against her thighs. The stone had given up its heat. She shifted, the muscles in her legs stiff from stillness.

"Waiting," she said. "She is good at it. Twenty years at the work. Standing at the cellar mouth. Standing at the door. Standing wherever she is placed."

Kael was quiet. His thumb stroked her wrist, a slow back-and-forth.

"You are not," he said.

"No." The admission came out flat. "I pace. I count. I find ways to make the time do work."

"She made the time do work too. She held the line at the cellar mouth. She held the cold at her wrists, her forearms, her elbows. She held it by standing there."

Sol looked at the gate. The cobbles were empty. The Thorne guard's copper clasps were gone. The works steward's three knotted strands had vanished into the dusk.

"Four nights," she said. "She has four nights to hold out. And the pick will come."

"The pick waits at the yard gate. The custodian's order lapsed on Day 30. The pick has been waiting since Day 31."

"Five days."

"Five days."

She leaned her forehead against his shoulder. The wool was rough, warm from his body. The stick lay on the wall beside them, forgotten by both.

"Yelva said her piece," Sol said. "Once. At the cellar mouth. She told Mara not to walk. Mara walked anyway. Yelva gave up."

"Yelva knows when speaking changes nothing."

"She follows anyway."

"She follows."

The yard was fully dark now. Lamps flickered in the windows of the boiling house. The stand-in frame stood silent against the wall. The cords were coiled. Odo and Tessa had gone.

"She asked about the clasp," Kael said. "Not the map. Not the pegs. Not the cords. The clasp."

"She has seen it before. In the healer's back room. The night you brought her back."

"She held it a long moment."

"Her thumb traced the feather strand."

"The feather is the middle strand. The second of three."

"I know the order."

"Drop, feather, flame."

"She said her hands are warmer when I am near."

The words sat in the cooling air. Sol felt the weight of them, not mechanics, not comfort, only Mara's line.

"Is it true?" Kael asked.

"I do not know. She believes it. The draw ends at the close. She said so. The cold climbs on nights after someone has gone below. It has not climbed tonight. No one has gone below since the map was set."

"Renn went below."

"Renn went below days ago. The cold climbed at the elbows by Day 27. Today is Day 36. She is past the elbows. Yelva said so."

Kael's hand tightened on her wrist. "She will be at the cellar mouth. Not the door of the passage. She does not want to be carried."

"She said she would have run for your voice again."

"She said it is not a thing to forgive."

"She said you called. She ran. That is what being called is."

The hum from the passage pulsed. A low vibration felt in the ribs, in the soles of the feet, in the bones of the wall.

"I would have run too," Sol said.

"I know."

"For you. For her. For anyone who called."

"I know."

She lifted her head. The dusk had deepened to night. Stars pricked the sky above the cliff, cold and distant. The boiling-house yard was a pool of shadow.

"Four nights," she said again. "Then the descent. Day 38. The pick arrives Day 39. The close Day 40."

"The map is set. The sockets are set. The cords are run. The pegs are ready. The pullers are placed. The nearest body is defined."

"Renn's words."

"Renn's map."

She slid from the wall. Her boots hit the cobbles. The cold shot up through the leather. She stretched, her spine popping.

Kael reached for his stick. His left leg bore the weight as he rose. The ache was a familiar shape now. He did not grimace. He planted the ferrule. The rhythm was slower, deliberate, each placement measured.

"Come," he said. "The room over the tar-shop. Ansa's broth will be cold again."

"Ansa's broth is never cold. She brings it hot."

"She brought it hot this morning. It went cold while we sat on the floor."

"She will bring it hot again."

"She will."

They walked across the yard. The trapdoor waited, the grey light from the room above spilling down the rungs. The Upper Iron Door stood at the far end of the cellar, its enamelled knot catching the lamplight. The second stair descended behind it, twenty-three steps to the landing, the bare iron door, the plate, the sockets, the cords.

Sol did not look at the door. She did not look at the stair. She walked beside Kael, her step matching his, her hand finding his wrist again as they climbed the ladder into the room.

The hearth was cold. The tea pot sat empty. Ulla's ledger was closed on the table.

Sol went to the window. The yard lay below, empty, quiet. The Thorne guard was gone. The gate was shut. The works steward's copper clasp was a memory in the dark.

"He will come for her," she said to the glass. "The pick. On Day 39. He will come for her at the cellar mouth."

"She will be there."

"She will not be carried."

"She will not."

Sol pressed her palm to the glass. The cold bit her fingers. Her reflection stared back, pale, dark-eyed, the undyed coat a smudge against the night.

"I am not good at waiting," she said to the reflection. "But I will wait for this. Four nights. Three descents. One close."

Behind her, Kael moved. The stick tapped. A chair scraped. He sat. The sound of a spoon in a bowl.

She turned. The room was warm. The broth steamed. Kael held a wooden bowl, the broth dark and rich. He held out a second bowl.

"Ansa left it on the hearth," he said. "Covered. Still hot."

She took the bowl. The warmth spread through her fingers, up her arms, into her chest.

"Eat," he said.

She ate. The broth was salty, rich, good. The warmth spread.

Outside, the night held its breath. Four nights. Three descents. One close.

She would tell Mara when.
