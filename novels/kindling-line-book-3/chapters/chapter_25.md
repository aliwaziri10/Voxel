The tar-shop door framed Ansa's kitchen, the smell of broth and pine smoke curling into the lane. Morning light lay pale across the packed earth. Kael stood with his weight on his good leg, the stick hooked over one arm. His coat was fastened. His collar sat straight. He looked rested in the way that meant he had not slept much but had decided not to show it.

"Odo's hut," he said. "I will be there until the pull is rigged. Then the passage end."

Sol folded her arms. "The still-room at the Thorne house. Aldous sent word. The works steward's room is not bare."

They had spoken at the same moment. The words collided in the air between them. Kael's mouth quirked. Sol's eyebrow rose.

"You first," they said together.

A pause. Then Kael snorted, a short breath through his nose. "You first."

"Lady Vane first," Sol said. "Alphabetical."

"Actuarial," Kael corrected. "Seniority of risk."

Ansa's voice drifted from the kitchen, dry and unimpressed. "If I have to listen to this every morning, I will charge rent. By the hour. With interest."

Sol turned her head toward the doorway. Ansa stood at the table, ladle in hand, steam rising from the pot. Tam sat beside her, mending a net. Neither looked up.

"Rent noted," Sol called back. "We will keep it brief."

"Brief was three mornings ago," Ansa said. "This is a habit."

Kael reached into his coat and withdrew the lantern. He held it out. "Take it."

Sol glanced at the sky. The clouds were high and thin. The lane held no shadow deep enough to swallow a fingertip. "It is day."

"Take it anyway."

"I do not need it."

"That is not the reason."

She studied his face. The set of his jaw. The way his thumb rested on the lantern's cold metal. The reason was the same reason he had walked the lane to the boiling-house yard without the stick yesterday and would walk it again today: the habit of preparation, the refusal to leave a variable unaccounted. She took the lantern. The metal warmed against her palm.

"Thank you," she said.

His hand moved before she could pull away. Fingers brushed her throat, adjusting the cord of her coat where it had twisted beneath the copper clasp. The clasp caught the light: three strands knotted, drop and feather and flame. His knuckles grazed her jaw. She did not step back. She did not flinch. The contact lasted a heartbeat longer than necessity.

"Later," she said.

His hand fell. "Hold me to it."

The words settled in the space between them. The fourth time. The fourth morning. Each one had carried the same weight, the same quiet insistence, and each one had sounded less like a promise and more like a vow.

She turned then. Walked down the lane without looking back. The effort cost her, a physical pull at the base of her ribs. Her boots struck the earth. The lantern swung at her side. At the corner, where the lane bent toward the wide stair, she stopped. She looked back.

Kael had not moved. He stood at the tar-shop door, the stick hooked over his arm, the morning light outlining his shoulders. Ansa's ladle clinked against the pot. Tam's needle flashed. Kael raised one hand, a small gesture, fingers lifting from the wrist.

Sol did not wave. She did not smile. She held his gaze until the bend in the lane took her from sight, and then she climbed.

The wide shallow stair rose from the river's edge, each step worn smooth by generations. Lamps burned under violet glass at every fourth riser, their glow pale against the morning. The air grew cooler with each flight, the scent of tar giving way to stone dust and the faint mineral tang of the boiling-house vents far below.

Sol counted the landings. Seven. The Thorne house occupied the eighth, its façade severe and pale, the Thorne grey uniform across every lintel and sill. The front door stood closed. A narrow man in violet cuffs opened it at her approach. His face was smooth, unlined, the kind that revealed nothing. He said nothing. He stepped aside.

The interior was cool. Corridors ran straight and windowless, lit by the same violet lamps. Her boots made no sound on the stone. She knew the way. The still-room lay at the end of the east corridor, past the archive and the map chamber. Aldous waited in the doorway.

He was tall, his grandfather's height, but his shoulders lacked Malrik's breadth. His hair was pale, his face composed. His coat hung straight. When he moved, it was with a deliberation that suggested he had rehearsed every gesture.

"Lady Vane," he said. "The room is this way."

He led her past the still-room's copper vessels, the condenser coils, the ledgers stacked in precise rows. A door stood ajar beside the far wall. The small room beyond held a bed, a table, a chair. The bed was made. The table cleared. On the table, a square of cloth folded once, its edges crisp.

On the cloth lay a copper clasp.

Three strands knotted. Drop. Feather. Flame.

Sol's breath caught. The clasp at her own throat grew warm, sudden, as if recognizing its twin. She did not reach for it. She did not touch the one on the table. Her eyes lifted to the note beneath it.

The handwriting leaned right. The descenders closed. The tail of the 'y' curled open. She had seen this hand on the leaf, under the word carried. She had seen it in the primer, on the model lines Aldous had brought five days past.

The note read: *I will come to Lady Vane, not to the house and not to the Council.*

One sentence. No name. No date visible at first glance. The ink was dark, the strokes deliberate.

Aldous stood in the doorway behind her. He asked nothing. He offered nothing. His hands rested at his sides. His face was blank.

Sol picked up the clasp. The copper was cool, the knots tight. She picked up the note. The paper was thin, folded once. She slipped both into her coat pocket, her fingers closing over them.

"Thank you," she said.

Aldous inclined his head. "She was here in the night. The room was bare on Day Eighteen. It was not bare this morning."

"She came alone?"

"As far as I know."

"Will you tell your grandfather?"

Aldous paused. The violet lamps threw shadows across his cheekbones. "He did not ask. I will not volunteer."

Sol nodded. She turned toward the door. Her hand brushed the table's edge. The wood was smooth, unmarked. No dust. No trace of occupation beyond the bed and the clasp and the note.

She walked back through the still-room. Aldous fell into step behind her. The corridor stretched. The violet lamps burned steady. At the front door, the narrow steward held it open. The morning air hit her face, sharp with river and pine.

She descended the stair. The lamps at every fourth step threw pools of light. Her boots struck stone. The clasp in her pocket pressed against her thigh through the fabric. The note's paper crinkled.

Halfway down, she stopped. She drew the note out. Unfolded it. The right-leaning hand stared back at her. She read the sentence again. *I will come to Lady Vane, not to the house and not to the Council.*

Her eyes dropped to the bottom of the paper. A single word, written smaller, squeezed into the margin.

*Tonight.*

The word sat alone. No preamble. No explanation. The ink was the same. The hand was the same.

Sol folded the note. Returned it to her pocket. Her palm pressed flat against the copper clasp beneath the wool.

She went home to wait.

The descent took her past the violet lamps one by one, each pool of light swallowing her and releasing her. The stair was empty. The households of the Lower Reach kept to their yards and their work; the word had passed that the Thorne steward had moved in the night, but no one followed. No one needed to. The clasp in her pocket was proof enough.

Her boots knew the count. Two hundred and eleven steps to the anchor plate, but only eighty-seven to the tar-shop door. She had climbed them often enough in darkness, in rain, in the grey mornings after sleepless nights. Today the morning held a particular clarity, the kind that came after a night of speaking truths aloud. The public step. The counts. The seventeen years waiting in the gauge.

The note burned against her thigh through the wool. *Tonight.* One word. No hour specified. No condition. The writer had not said *if* or *when able* or *when the house sleeps*. She had written *tonight* and left it under the clasp, folded once, edges crisp, as if the act of folding sealed a contract.

Sol's hand remained pressed to her pocket. The copper warmed against her palm, twin to the one at her throat. Drop, feather, flame. The order never varied. The works steward had tied those knots forty years ago, or had them tied, and every clasp since had followed the same pattern. A language without words. A signature.

She reached the bottom landing. The river rushed beside the lane, grey-green and cold. Mist clung to the water's edge, burning off slow. The tar-shop chimney smoked. Ansa's broth pot would be bubbling. Tam would be at the net. Renn and Ulla by the fire. The gauge hut breathing its twelve beats.

*Tonight.*

The word implied the steward knew the cycle. Knew the trough between twelve and one. Knew the cords were rigged, the pegs waiting, the pullers measured. Or she knew enough to know that Sol would be at the plate when the breath came. That the stored years would find her. That seventeen years would land on sixteen and a half and make thirty-three.

Thirty-three. The number Kael had spoken aloud to the yard. The number he had promised her, stone by stone, in the gauge hut's cold light. The number that meant growing old together.

Sol stopped at the corner where the lane widened into the yard. The tar-shop door stood open. Ansa's ladle clinked. Tam's needle flashed. Renn sat propped against the wall, Ulla's fingers gentle on his splint. Corren and Joren at the gate. Odo and Tessa by the gauge hut, chalk lines coiled.

Kael was not in sight. Odo's hut, he had said. The passage end.

She walked across the packed earth. The clasp in her pocket clicked against the copper at her throat, a small sharp sound no one else would hear. *Tonight.* The steward would come tonight. Not to the house. Not to the Council. To Lady Vane.

The title felt strange in her mouth, stranger in the note. The steward had never called her that. Had never spoken to her at all, not in twenty years of Sol's life. The woman who had carried the years for Thorne, who had written the second hand on the leaf, who had taught Aldous his letters so the hand would be recognized — she had chosen Sol. Not Malrik. Not the Chancellor. Not the custodian's pick.

*I will come to Lady Vane.*

A decision. Not a summons. Not a plea. The steward had walked out of the Thorne house in the night, left the clasp and the note, and would return tonight to the woman who held her mother's plate and her lover's promise and the gauge's seventeen years.

Sol wanted to hear her. Wanted the woman to say whatever she had carried for forty years, the account she had kept in the dark, the reason she had begun and the reason she had continued and the reason she had stopped writing *carried* on the leaf. Wanted it before the Council decided what the steward was. Before Malrik decided. Before the custodian's pick — whoever that was — decided.

The account belonged to the woman who lived it. Sol would not let it be taken.

She stepped onto the tar-shop threshold. Ansa looked up from the pot. Tam glanced from his net. Renn's eyes found her, sharp despite the pallor.

"Well?" Ansa said. "The Thorne house still stands?"

"Still stands." Sol crossed to the ladder. "The works steward was there. In the night. She left a clasp and a note."

"She came to you?"

"She will come tonight. To me."

Ansa's ladle stilled. "To you."

"To Lady Vane. Her words."

Tam's needle paused. "That is a strange way to put it. If you are already Lady Vane."

"She wrote it deliberate."

"Deliberate is good," Renn said from the fireside. His voice was thin, tired. "Deliberate means she knows what she risks. The custodian's order names a ward-taker of the Council's roll. If the steward comes to you, she avoids the roll. She avoids the house. She avoids the Chancellor." He paused. "She trusts you."

"Or she trusts the plate," Sol said. She climbed the ladder. "The anchor plate. My mother's plate. The one that never burns."

The trapdoor gave under her hands. She pulled herself up into the room. The tea cups still sat on the table. The broth pot cold. The bread board empty. Morning light through the cracked window, pale and thin.

She set the lantern on the table. Unfolded the note again. *I will come to Lady Vane, not to the house and not to the Council.* The word *tonight* in the margin, small and squeezed.

She read it a third time. A fourth. The right lean. The closed descenders. The open tail on the *y*. The same hand that had written *carried* under her mother's row on the leaf. The same hand that had written the model letters in the primer, the name inked out, the date forty years past.

Forty years. The steward had carried the years for forty years. On the order of Malrik's father, unnamed, dead. Alone. She had written every second hand herself. She had taught Aldous his letters so someone would know. She had written Aldous's name on the witness line herself so a clerk would compare hands and ask who taught him — wanting to be found, but not by the Council, not by Thorne.

She had locked Renn in the third gallery to keep him alive and away from the pegs, not knowing the line would draw on him.

Malrik did not know.

Sol folded the note. Slipped it back into her pocket beside the clasp. Her fingers closed over both, copper and paper, warm and cool.

She sat at the table. The window showed the lane, the river, the cliffs. The gauge hut's hatch stayed closed. The line breathed twelve beats.

*Tonight.*

She would wait. She would listen. She would let the woman speak her account, whatever it held, before anyone decided what the steward was — thief, savior, traitor, tool. The account belonged to the woman who had lived it.

Sol leaned back in the chair. The copper clasp at her throat caught the light. The one in her pocket pressed against her palm. Twin knots. Drop, feather, flame.

She closed her eyes. Counted the beats. Eleven. Twelve. The trough between.

*Tonight.*
