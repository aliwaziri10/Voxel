Morning came up grey and mild over the lane. The tar shop was already at its work, the smell of pitch rolling out through the open door and a mallet knocking on hemp inside, and the broth Ansa had put on before light sent its own thin steam into the cold.

Kael stood on the bottom step with the stick hooked over his forearm and his coat done up to the neck. He had the look of a man who had not slept much and had decided not to mention it. Neither of them had slept much. They had lain in the room over the shop with the count running between them in the dark, unspoken, the way a second heartbeat runs under the first, and had risen in the grey with nothing left over to say.

"Odo's hut," he said. "I will be there all morning. If you want me, that is where I am."

"The Thorne house," Sol said, in the same breath. "Aldous sent word that the steward's room is not bare. I will go up now and come straight back."

They both stopped. It took her a moment to see why. Neither of them had asked. There had been a time when the words between them were a demand, said across a table with the lamp turned low: tell me now, and tell me first, every time. This morning nobody had demanded anything. He had said where he would be and she had said where she was going, and the whole business had taken two breaths, and it fit like a coat that had been cut for her.

"You told me first," Kael said.

"So did you."

"I noticed." The corner of his mouth moved. "I thought I would not say so, in case it stopped."

"It will not stop because you said it."

He took the lantern down from its nail inside the door and held it out to her. "Take this."

She looked at the sky. High thin cloud, and a lane with no shadow deep enough to hide a fingertip. "It is day."

"Take it anyway."

"I do not need it."

"That is not why I am giving it to you."

She studied his face, the set of his jaw, his thumb resting on the cold ring of the lantern. He was not offering her light. He was offering her a thing of his to carry into a house where he would not be. She took it. The ring was warm from his hand, and the glass was clouded with the print of his thumb, and her fingers closed over his on the ring and neither of them let go at once.

"Later," she said.

"Hold me to it."

They were the oldest private words they had, older than the public step and every number said aloud since, and they carried what they had always carried: a conversation, or a night, that was not finished and would be.

She went down the lane without looking back, because looking back would have cost more than going. At the corner where the lane bent toward the wide stair she looked back anyway. He had not moved. He stood on the step with the stick over his arm and the morning on his shoulders, and he raised one hand from the wrist. She did not wave. She held his eyes until the bend took her, and then she climbed.

---

The wide stair rose from the river's edge in shallow flights, each tread worn into a dip by generations of boots. A lamp burned under violet glass at every fourth riser, pale against the morning, and the air cooled as she went up, the smell of tar giving way to stone dust and the faint mineral breath of the boiling-house vents far below. Sol counted the lamps without meaning to, lost the number twice, and began again. It was a habit of the plate. Count a thing, and the fear sat still.

She had climbed this stair before, in other weeks and other weather, with Kael's pace beside her and the Council's business waiting at the top. He could not have managed it this morning without the stick, and she had not asked him to climb it with the stick; Aldous had sent word to her, and she meant to hear it alone. Today she climbed it by herself, with a lantern she did not need and two words of Aldous's in her head. Not bare.

The anchor stair had gone down into the dark and ended in iron. This one went up into the light and ended in a door, and she found she trusted it less. A stair that went down told you plainly what was at the bottom. A stair that went up into a house only told you that a person had been expecting you.

The house stood pale and severe against the cliff, grey stone and grey shutters, as if the family had wanted to be mistaken for the rock it was built on. The front door opened before she reached it. The steward of the house stood in the gap, a narrow man in grey with violet at his cuffs and a face designed for not being remembered. He said nothing. He stepped aside and kept his eyes on the floor, and she went past him into the cool.

The hall was wide and high and very clean, with a long table down the middle of it that looked as if nobody had eaten at it in years and a stair at the far end going up into shadow. Aldous was waiting beside the table, a long pale figure in a grey coat, standing with his hands at his sides as if they had been issued to him. He had his grandfather's height and none of his ease, and it was plain from his face that he had not slept either.

"Lady Vane," he said. "Thank you for coming."

"Is your grandfather in the house?"

"Asleep. He came home late." He said it as a fact about the house and not as a warning, one more fact among the others. "This way."

She had heard him describe the house at Ansa's table, and the description had been exact. Her boots made no sound on the stone, and neither did his. The corridors ran straight, and the violet lamps burned in their niches, and nothing in the house made a noise it did not have to. A boy had grown up in this, Sol thought, and then she put the thought away, because it would not help her and it would not help him.

The still-room opened off the east corridor, a long low room with a scrubbed table down its middle, a rack of slates on one wall, and shelves of stoppered jars along the other. It smelled of cold copper and old herbs. This was the room where a six-year-old had copied model letters from a grey primer with a woman beside him, her sleeves pushed up. Sol did not look at the slate rack for long.

Beside the still-room, in the angle of the wall, was a narrow door standing a hand's breadth open. Aldous stopped in front of it.

"Five days ago I told you what was in here," he said. "Nothing." He pushed the door wide. "This is not nothing."

It was a small room, and it was the same bare room he had described. The shelf over the bed was still empty, with no dust where books had been, because a hand had wiped the wood. The pale square was still on the wall where a small frame had hung. The peg beside it still held nothing. But the bed, which he had found stripped to the boards, was made, a grey blanket pulled square and a pillow set at its head, and the table under the narrow window had been cleared and wiped. It was like seeing a house with one tooth put back.

Sol crossed to the bed and laid her palm flat on the blanket. It was cold, and it had been cold for hours. She touched the peg by the pale square, and it was smooth from use and colder than the wall. Through the narrow window she could see the cliff, and the river below it, and the thin haze that rose from the boiling-house vents, and she thought that whoever had lived in this room had looked at that view every morning for a very long time and had taken every trace of herself away from it.

On the table lay a square of cloth, folded once, its edges crisp. On the cloth lay a copper clasp.

Sol knew it before she bent to it. Three strands knotted: drop, feather, flame, in that order and no other. It was the only thing in the room that shone. She heard Mara's voice from the cot in the healer's back room, thirsty and cross and entirely certain. The clasp was the only thing that shone. I would know it again.

She did not touch it yet. Under the cloth's edge a sheet of thin paper had been folded once, and she lifted that instead, and opened it.

It was one sentence, in brown ink, in a hand that leaned to the right.

*I will come to Lady Vane, not to the house and not to the Council.*

The strokes were even and the descenders closed, and every letter walked into the same wind. Lady Vane. The title sat on the page the way a name sits on a parcel addressed to a stranger, correct and without warmth. Nobody who loved her had ever called her that. The woman had chosen the word the Council would have used, and then written that she would not go to the Council, and Sol turned that over once and found no edge on it.

Sol knew that lean. She had seen it on the leaf in her oilcloth bundle, in the darker hand under the word carried, and on the model alphabet Aldous had put into her hands in Ansa's room. She did not say so. She did not let her face say so either; she had learned that much at the plate. A lean was a lean, and half the clerks in the Reach leaned right, and she would not let the thought finish until it had a reason to.

Behind her, Aldous stood in the doorway and did not come in. He asked nothing. His eyes were on the clasp and not on her, and she understood that he had stood in this spot a good while that morning before he sent for her, and had not touched it.

"It was not here five days ago," she said.

"No."

"So she was in the house in the night."

For a while neither of them spoke. Far down the corridor a door closed, softly, the way doors close in a house that has learned to be quiet, and Aldous's shoulders rose and fell once. He did not answer her, and she had not meant it as a question. The house had a hundred doors, and not one of them had been forced, and the steward of the house had been awake at the front of it all night, or had not, and none of that was hers to ask. She was not here to search a house. She was here to read a sentence.

She picked up the clasp. The copper was cool and heavier than it looked, and the knots were tight and neat, the work of a patient hand. She turned it over. The pin was straight and the back of it was worn bright along one edge, where a thumb had rubbed it every morning for longer than she had been alive to count. It was made to sit at the throat of a coat, where an eye went first. She laid the note against it and held the two together in her palm.

"I would like to take these," she said.

"Take them. They are not mine." He said it on a long breath, like a man setting down a bucket.

"And I would ask you to say nothing of them to anyone in the house until I have heard what she means to tell me."

"I will say nothing."

He did not ask why. He did not ask what she meant to do, or what she believed the note meant, or whether she thought the woman was coming to confess or to run or to ask for help. He stood in the doorway of a bare room with his pale hands at his sides, and agreed, and waited to be told whether he was needed any further. Sol thought of the tray set down without good evening, and the lamps lit by a boy who did not look up.

"Thank you, Aldous," she said, and found that she meant it more than the words could hold.

"You need not thank me." His mouth moved, almost a smile, and went before it arrived. "I would like to be asked to be of use, now and then."

"Then I am asking."

"Then I am glad to be asked," said Aldous, and bent his head to her, a short stiff bow, the way a boy bows who was taught it before he understood what it was for.

He walked her back through the still-room and along the corridor. At the front door the narrow steward held it open, and his eyes met hers for the length of a breath and dropped to the floor again. The morning air came in sharp with river and pine. Sol put the clasp and the note into her coat pocket, where they lay against her thigh, and stepped out.

She went down the stair slowly. The lamps at every fourth riser threw their pools of violet light across the stone, and she passed through each one and out again. The households of the Lower Reach were at their work far below, the smoke of their chimneys standing straight up in the still air. Nobody was following her. Nobody needed to. She had the only thing there was to follow.

Halfway down she stopped beside a lamp and stood with her hand on the cold rail. A hooded figure had stood on a rope bridge with a clasp like this one bright at the throat, and had watched them. Mara had been taken in a cellar, and Renn had been lost for more than a month, and Kael's leg still would not take a stair without help, and nobody had yet laid those on one line. Sol did not lay them on one line now. She found she was not afraid of what the woman would say. She was afraid of how quickly the Council would find a word for her. Sol knew what names did. She had a plate that was her mother's and a title she had not asked for, and she had watched how fast a name closed over a person, like water over a dropped stone. By the end of this week the woman who left a clasp on a folded cloth would be a thief, or a traitor, or a tool, whichever the Council found most useful, and the account she had come to give would be lost under the word.

Sol wanted to hear it first. Whatever it was. She wanted the woman allowed to say it, in her own voice, to one person who had not decided yet, before anyone told her what she was.

She drew the note out again. She had read it once in the room, only the sentence. Now she unfolded it fully, to read it as slowly as a letter she meant to answer, and the paper lay open on her palm, and the sentence was the same.

At the foot of the page, small, squeezed against the margin, the same brown ink had written one word more.

*Tonight.*

The writer had not said when she was able, or when the house slept. She had set the day down at the bottom of the page the way a clerk dates a receipt, and left it under the clasp, folded once, with its edges crisp. It was not a hope. It was an appointment.

Sol folded the note along its crease and put it back beside the copper. Her hand stayed over the pocket for a moment.

She would tell Kael. First, every time: she had said so at the door and meant it. But she would tell him with the paper in her hand and the whole of it in front of him, not as a report called across a yard, and not before she had stood still long enough to know what she thought. Across the roofs below her she could see the grey back of the gauge hut with a thread of smoke beside it. She lifted two fingers toward it, in case he was looking, and he was not, and she did it anyway.

Then she went down the rest of the stair with the lantern knocking unlit against her leg and the print of his thumb still clouding the glass, to wait.
