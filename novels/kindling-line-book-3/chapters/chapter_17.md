The lamp burned low on the table, its flame caught in the curve of the glass. Boots sat by the door, leather dark with the day's oil and dust. A pot of soup had gone cold between them, the fat risen in a pale skin across the surface.

Sol pulled her legs up on the narrow cot. The wool of her coat still held the heat of the climb, the two hundred and eleven steps, the weight of Kael's arm against her shoulder. She watched him pour tea into two tin cups, his movements unhurried. The stick leaned against the wall, forgotten.

He brought a cup to her. The steam rose between their faces.

"I am going to be glad for one whole hour," she said.

Kael set his own cup down. He pulled the chair closer, straddling it backwards, arms folded on the backrest. His square hand rested on his forearm, the knuckles pale.

"One hour," he repeated. "I will keep time."

"Your ledger voice."

"It is the only voice I have for counting."

She blew across the tea. The surface rippled. "Half an hour from now, you will call it."

"I will."

She sipped. The tea was bitter, over-steeped. "Yelva will say we let it go cold on purpose."

"Yelva says the soup is thin when it is thick. She says the bread is hard when it is soft. She said my boots tracked mud that was not there three days ago."

"Did they?"

"She invented it. She admitted it when I asked."

Sol's mouth curved. "Ansa's soup is never thin. She puts a ham bone in the pot on the first day and does not take it out until the broth remembers the pig."

"Mara will complain about the cot. The blankets. The light through the window. The way the stove ticks."

"She complained about the cot in the healer's room. She complained about the broth. She complained about Joren's snoring, and Joren does not snore."

"She complained about the boots Yelva dried for her."

"She will complain about these boots tomorrow."

Kael's shoulder shifted, a small huff of breath. "She is awake. She is cross. She is asking for her boots and a bridge to walk. That is more than we had ten days ago."

The words settled in the room. The lamp threw their shadows long against the wall, two shapes leaning toward each other, merging at the edges.

Sol's laugh came sudden, surprised. It cracked on the second note. Her eyes burned.

She pressed the heels of her palms against them. "I did not expect that."

Kael did not reach for her. He did not move the chair closer. He sat, present and still, letting the silence hold its shape.

The tears were hot on her cheeks. She let them fall. Her breath came uneven.

"Gladness is not a loan," she said, the words thick. "You do not pay it back with interest."

Kael's jaw worked. He looked at the table, at the grain of the wood, at the ring the cup had left.

"I am glad too," he said. "And afraid to say it. In case saying it costs something."

"It is not a loan," she repeated.

His head lifted. The corners of his mouth moved, not quite a smile. "That is the nicest thing anyone has ever said about my habits."

"It was an insult."

"It was both."

She reached across the space between them. Her hands found his face, palms cupping his jaw, thumbs brushing the line of his cheekbones. The stubble rasped soft against her skin. She held him there, looking, searching. The grey eyes, the crease between his brows, the faint scar at his hairline from a fall in the third gallery when he was twelve — she knew the story, knew the stitches, knew the way he had refused the healer's poppy and walked out with his head high.

He let her. His eyelids lowered. His breath slowed. The tension in his shoulders released, fraction by fraction.

Her thumbs traced the bone beneath his eyes. The skin was thin there, translucent almost, the veins a blue map of the years he had carried.

"You are here," she said. Quiet. "You are whole. The leg holds. The climb held."

"The climb held because you were at my elbow."

"I was at the rail. I chose the rail."

"You chose to let me do it."

"I chose to trust you."

His hands came up, covered hers where they rested on his face. Large, warm, callused at the base of the fingers from the stick and the rope and the tally pegs. He turned his mouth into her palm, pressed a kiss there. The whisper of lips against skin.

The lamp guttered. The wick needed trimming.

Sol drew her hands back. The loss of contact was a small ache.

"One hour," Kael said. He picked up his cup. Drank. Set it down. "Half."

She smiled through the wet on her cheeks. "Half."

He rose. The chair scraped the floor. He moved to the lamp, turned the wick down until the flame shrank to a blue point, the room folding into shadow. The outline of him at the door, the latch lifted, the pause.

"Come back to me," she said.

The door closed. The latch caught.

She listened to his boots on the stair, the rhythm steady, the left foot finding its place without hesitation. The sound faded. The room held the shape of him in the air, in the cooling tea, in the dent on the pillow beside her.

Sol lay back. The wool blanket smelled of tar and oil and the faint sweetness of Ansa's broth. She pulled it to her chin. Closed her eyes.

The hour would not last. The gladness would not stay unearned. But for now, the dark was warm.

Morning found her at the east gauge hut before the sun cleared the ridge. The boiling-house yard spread below, a confusion of pipes and tanks and the long skeleton of the second line running east toward the cliff. Steam rose in columns, white against the grey stone. The air tasted of mineral and heat.

Odo Marl stood at the gauge board, broad shoulders filling the doorway. He held a chalk slate in one hand, a stub of chalk in the other. His eyes moved from the gauge to the slate, from the slate to the gauge. He wrote. He checked. He wrote again.

Tessa Rook crouched by the base of the instrument, her chalk-line snapping against the metal flank. She was twenty-three, quick as a struck match, her dark hair pinned back with a clip shaped like a gear tooth. She looked up as Sol approached.

"Mara's log," Tessa said. "One hour nightly. The cold draws on her like a body standing at the east door."

Odo did not turn. "The gauge reads eleven breaths per cycle. The second line adds a twelfth. The draw holds steady at the eleventh."

"Eleven breaths," Sol repeated. "The cycle B2 recorded at twenty-seven and forty-five."

"Nine and eleven breaths," Odo said. "Averaging ten. The east pulse adds one. Eleven now. The second line makes twelve."

He tapped the slate. "The draw rises on nights after someone goes below. Renn. Eight days. Three years. The gauge climbed from fourteen to seventeen. The cold at Mara's forearms climbed with it."

Tessa stood, wiping chalk dust on her trousers. "Yelva's temper held. Ansa's soup held. The cot held. The draw did not stop."

Sol leaned against the doorframe. The metal was warm under her palm, radiating the heat of the line beneath. "The draw does not stop on its own."

"No," Odo said. "It does not."

"Thirteen days to Day Twenty-Eight. The formal demand at the Lower Reach. The first attempt to close on Day Twenty-Nine."

"Thirteen days," Odo agreed. "The rope rig is set. The twin brass pegs are ready. The ledge peg and the table peg. Same mold. Placed."

"Two pullers beyond the room's reach."

"You and Tessa. Odo and I."

Sol's gaze drifted to the east, where the line vanished into the cliff face. The diagram in the oilcloth showed a dotted line running from the anchor's south edge toward the Lower Reach, off the paper, into the unknown.

"On the gauge," Tessa said, her voice level, "Mara's cold counts as a body standing at the east door."

The words hung in the steam-thick air. Odo's chalk stopped mid-stroke.

Sol pushed off the doorframe. Her boots found the first rung of the ladder down to the yard.

"Then we measure the draw with her in the count," she said. "And we close the line before it takes the rest."

Sol's boot found the rung. The metal was warm, the heat rising from the line below. She descended rung by rung, the steel cold against her palms where the chalk dust had not reached.

The yard opened around her. Steam columns rose from the tanks, white plumes dissolving into the grey morning. The second line ran east, a long spine of iron and ceramic, its joints wrapped in tarred rope. The pulse in the ground was a low vibration she felt through the soles of her boots, a slow heartbeat measured in breaths.

Odo's voice carried from the hut, muffled by the walls. Tessa's answer, sharper. The chalk slate scratched.

Sol walked the length of the gauge board. The needle sat steady at the eleventh mark, trembling only when the east pulse hit. Twelve breaths per cycle now. The second line adding its weight to the first.

She stopped at the junction where the east line met the main. A valve wheel stood frozen in its housing, the brass green with age. The diagram in the oilcloth had shown this point — the dotted line running from the anchor's south edge, toward the cliff, off the paper.

Mara's cold. One hour nightly. Counted as a body at the east door.

The gauge did not lie. The draw held steady at the eleventh breath. But the hours matched Yelva's log. Every night after someone went below, the cold climbed. Renn. Eight days. Three years. The gauge had climbed from fourteen to seventeen.

Three years from one man locked in the dark. The steward had not known. She had locked him away from the pegs, not from the line.

Sol's hand rested on the valve wheel. The metal bit into her palm.

Thirteen days to Day Twenty-Eight. The formal demand at the Lower Reach. The custodian's pick named on Day Twenty-Six. The order dated Day Zero, served Day Sixteen. Sixteen days the Council had held it before telling Kael.

She had not asked Kael how that felt. She had not asked herself.

The steam shifted. A gust of wind drove it low across the yard, obscuring the line, the tanks, the hut. When it cleared, the gauge needle had not moved.

Eleven breaths. Twelve with the second line. The draw steady. The cold at Mara's forearms, climbing on borrowed nights.

Sol turned back toward the hut. The ladder waited. The stove in the tar shop would be hot. Ansa's broth would be waiting. Yelva would complain about the mud, real or invented.

She climbed the rungs. The hut door was open. Odo looked up from his slate. Tessa snapped her chalk-line, the sound clean in the steam-thick air.

"Eleven," Odo said. "Holding."

"Twelve with the east," Tessa said. "Holding."

Sol stepped inside. The warmth wrapped her, the smell of oil and hot metal and chalk.

"Chart it," she said. "Every cycle. Every hour. If the draw shifts, if the cold shifts, we know before the formal demand."

Odo nodded. His chalk moved. Tessa crouched again, her chalk-line ready.

The gauge needle breathed. Eleven. Twelve. Eleven. Twelve.

Sol watched it, her back to the door, her face to the line that ran east toward the cliff, toward the Lower Reach, toward the place where the dotted line fell off the paper.

Thirteen days.

She would not waste them.
