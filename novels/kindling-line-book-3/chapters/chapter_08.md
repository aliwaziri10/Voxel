Morning light found them on the lane above the tannery terraces, the path narrow between the cliff face and the drop where steam curled up from the vats below. Wind came off the water, cold and smelling of salt and lime and the slow rot of hides. Scree shifted under their boots. The east-break stones waited ahead, eleven of them in a ragged line where the Kindling's pulse met the cliff.

Kael walked a half pace behind Sol. His stick tapped the stone, steady. His left leg had held through the night without waking him, a rarity he had not dared name aloud. The grey of his coat caught the light. The violet cuffs were turned back. His square hand rested on the stick's grip, the scar across the back of it pale.

He had been rehearsing.

He stepped back. Stopped. Looked down at his own boots. Stepped back again. Silent. The motion was deliberate, measured, the way a man might practice a knife throw or a knot he meant to tie in darkness.

Sol's stride did not falter. She glanced over her shoulder, her undyed coat brushing the cliff wall. Her grey eyes caught him.

"What are you doing?"

Kael lifted his chin. The wind tugged at the hair at his temple. "Practising."

"Practising what?"

"Standing back."

Sol stopped. She turned fully, one boot on a loose shale that shifted. Her mouth twitched. Then she laughed, a sharp bark of it that startled a gull from the cliff face. She laughed until she had to brace a hand on her knee, until her breath came uneven.

Kael watched her. The stick felt solid in his grip. "It is harder than it looks."

"It looks ridiculous."

He could not argue. He had chosen the words, the rhythm, the silence between steps. He had chosen to make a performance of the thing he had failed at once already.

Sol straightened. The laughter faded, leaving her face open, the corners of her eyes crinkled. "I do not want you to practise."

"No?"

"I want you to do it when it counts. Not as a performance. Not as a rehearsal. When I ask, or when the plate demands it, you go. Before you argue. Before you think. You go."

Kael's jaw tightened. The wind moved between them, carrying the scent of the tannery vats. "That is how I fail. By doing it only when it counts. By waiting for the moment to decide."

She studied him. The gull circled overhead, crying. The scree settled.

"You are right," she said. "I see it."

She stepped closer. Her hand rose, fingers closing on the wool of his sleeve at the forearm. She gripped and turned him, a small force, enough to bring him fully around to face her. The stick shifted in his other hand. The wind lifted the hair at her cheek.

"If I ask," she said, her voice lowered, stripped of the humour, "I need you to go before you argue."

Kael met her eyes. The grey flecks in her irises. The pulse at her throat. "Yes."

"You said yes at the tar shop and then argued."

"I argued in my head."

Her thumb pressed into the fabric of his sleeve, a small pressure. "That counts as arguing."

The wind gusted. Her hair blew across her mouth. She did not brush it away. Her hand stayed on his sleeve, the grip neither tightening nor releasing. A breath. Two. The scree shifted under his boots.

Kael thought of the night in the cellar room. The tea. The leaf. The way she had come to the window and leaned her shoulder against his arm, the first touch she had initiated in days. The way her fingers had curled into his sleeve then, too. How rarely she was the one who reached first. How he noticed every time.

He did not move his arm. He did not pull away. The wool was rough under her fingers. The wind tasted of iron and salt.

"Good," she said. "Then we understand each other."

She released him. Turned back toward the path. Her coat swung. The east-break stones loomed closer, dark shapes against the cliff.

Kael followed. The stand-back promise had become a thing they could joke about. He did not know whether that made it easier to keep or harder to break.

---

The eleven ward-stones stood in a line where the terrace path widened, each one a rough pillar of black iron set into the living rock. Copper wire wound their bases, bright against the grey. Gauge glass crowned each stone, the fluid inside catching the morning light. Some were still. Some pulsed slow. One, the third from the east end, throbbed with a rhythm Kael felt in his teeth.

Odo Marl stood two paces from that stone. Broad, fifty, his bad knee bent slightly. He held a slate, pencil poised. He took every reading twice. That was what he did.

Tessa Rook crouched at the stone's base, her chalk line at her belt, her quick fingers adjusting the gauge reader. Twenty-three. She read fast and wrote faster.

Tam held the lamp. Ansa's nephew. The boy who had signed the standard on the tenth of Cinderveil and withdrawn on the fifteenth and still worked the tar. His face was pale in the lamplight, the flame throwing shadows up the cliff face.

Sol approached the stone. Her undyed coat moved in the wind. She did not look at Kael.

He planted his stick. He stepped back. Two paces. Three. He did not wait to be asked.

The echo. She noticed. He saw her shoulders shift, the almost imperceptible pause in her step. Neither said so.

"Gauge reads four," Tessa called. Her voice cut the wind. "Four years stored. Cycle steady at twelve breaths."

Odo marked the slate. Scratched the pencil. "Four. Confirmed."

Sol laid her palm flat on the cold iron. The copper wire bit into her wrist. Her fingers spread. The pulse of the stone met the pulse in her hand. Twelve beats. She counted them in the way only she could, the rhythm lived in her chest.

The gauge fluid dropped. The light in the glass dimmed. The stone's hum faded to silence.

Odo sucked in a breath. His hand flew to his knee. The fingers dug into wool and flesh beneath. His face went slack with something that was not pain.

"Odo," Tessa said. Sharp. "Odo, your knee."

Odo straightened. Slow. The pencil fell from his fingers, clattering on the shale. He tested the joint. Bent it. Straightened it. Bent it again. The grimace that had lived in his face for years, the one Kael had seen a hundred times at the rope rig and the tar vats and the cliff count, smoothed away.

"Quiet," Odo said. The word came out rough. "It's gone quiet. Like a week come back." He stopped. Looked at his knee. Looked at the stone. Looked at Sol. "Like it never hurt."

Tessa's slate hovered. Her chalk waited. "The gauge reads zero. The four years are gone."

"Where did they go?" Tam asked. The lamp trembled in his grip. "Four years don't vanish."

Kael stepped forward. The stick tapped once. The wind carried his voice flat, precise, the ledger voice he used for manifests and tallies.

"A closed line gives what it stored to the nearest living body. The same way cost goes. The nearest living body pays. A closed line pays back."

He said it once. Plainly. No ritual. No repetition.

Tessa's chalk touched the slate. Scratched. "The east gauge does not go down at night."

The words hung in the wind. The gull cried. The tannery vats steamed below.

Kael looked at the stone. At the dark glass. At Odo standing straight for the first time in years, his hand still resting on a knee that had forgotten how to ache.

Standing back had been harder than paying. He had not known that until the moment he did it unasked, until he felt Sol notice and not speak. The stakes were simpler at the plate: one body, one cost, one choice. Here, closing a line paid someone. Closing the east line near strangers was not safe.

Odo laughed. A short, broken sound. He pressed his palm flat against the iron pillar. "Four years. Four years of grinding bone. Gone because I stood two paces from a ward stone when a girl closed it."

"Two paces," Tessa said. She wrote. "Nearest living body. The rule holds."

Sol's hand fell from the stone. She turned. Her face gave nothing away. The wind lifted her hair. She looked at Kael. Her eyes held the question she would not voice: what happens when the line we mean to close holds seventeen years, and the nearest living body is not a stranger's bad knee?

Kael met her gaze. The rope between them was not hemp today. It was the carried lines on a ledger leaf. The seventeen years on a gauge that had not moved in forty years until a surveyor was locked in the dark.

He did not answer. He could not. The pegs waited in his pocket and her pack.

Tessa capped her chalk. "We move to the next stone. Three paces west."

Odo picked up his pencil. He moved without the limp. The slate clicked against the clipboard. "Lead on."

Tam adjusted the lamp. The flame steadied. "Ansa said eat. She didn't say stop."

Sol fell into step beside Kael. The path narrowed again. The cliff pressed close. The wind carried the scent of hot mineral water and old iron from the boiling house vents ahead.

Kael's stick tapped. His left leg held. The promise they had made on the lane, the joke that was not a joke, settled into his stride. He did not step back. He did not need to. Not yet.

Sol's hand brushed Kael's knuckles as they walked. A touch. Light. Brief. The wool of his glove rough against her fingers.

He did not look down. He did not pull away. The contact burned cleaner than the Kindling, hotter than the plate, quieter than the twelve beats in his chest.

She had touched him first twice in one morning. He noticed. He would not stop noticing.

The path curved west, hugging the cliff face where the rock wept mineral water into shallow grooves. Stone four stood at a bend where the wind funneled hard, the copper wire at its base green with verdigris. Tessa crouched without being told. Her chalk moved over the gauge reader, quick as a breath drawn.

"Six years," she said. "Cycle steady at twelve. The second line adds its breath on the upstroke."

Odo marked the slate. His pencil moved smooth, the limp gone from his step as he shifted weight to test the knee. "Six. Confirmed. The joint holds."

Sol did not look at Kael. She approached the stone, her palm finding the iron, the copper biting her wrist. Twelve beats. The gauge fluid dropped. The light dimmed. The hum faded.

This time the nearest living body was Tam. The lamp wavered in his grip as the six years hit him. He gasped, a sharp intake that echoed off the cliff. His shoulders straightened. The fine tremor in his hands, the one Kael had seen at the tar vats when the boy poured hot pitch, stilled. His breath came even. He looked at his fingers, turned them over, flexed them slow.

"Steady," Tam said. The word held wonder. "My hands. They're steady."

Tessa's chalk scratched. "Gauge reads zero. Six years transferred. Nearest living body: Tam, nephew of Ansa, tar worker."

"Third stone paid Odo's knee," Kael said. His voice carried flat on the wind. "Fourth stone pays Tam's tremor. The rule holds."

Sol's hand fell from the iron. She did not turn. Her profile was sharp against the cliff, the line of her jaw set. The wind lifted the hair at her temple.

"Stone five," Tessa called. "Two paces further west."

They moved. The pattern settled into rhythm. Stone five held three years, paid to a scout whose cracked ribs knit silent beneath his coat. Stone six held two, paid to Corren's wrist where an old fracture had ached damp mornings. Stone seven held five, paid to a second scout whose shoulder had frozen since a fall from the cliff shelf three winters back. Each time Sol touched the iron. Each time the gauge dropped. Each time the nearest body took what the stone had stored.

Kael watched the faces. The surprise. The disbelief smoothing into something quieter, harder to name. A scout pressed his palm to his ribs, breathing deep, laughing low. Corren rotated his wrist, the scar on his hand white against the skin, and said nothing. The second scout rolled his shoulder, the joint popping, and wiped a sleeve across his eyes.

Odo moved between them all, his slate filling, his pencil never pausing. He wrote the gauge readings. He wrote the recipients. He wrote the years transferred. He did not write the rule. Kael had spoken it once. That was the agreement. Once, plainly, no ritual.

Stone eight. One year. Paid to Tessa herself, a faint scar on her forearm fading to white. She did not flinch. She wrote the reading. She wrote the transfer. Her hand did not shake.

Stone nine. Eight years. The gauge glass thick, the fluid dark. Sol's hand on the iron. The pulse met her palm. Twelve beats. The fluid dropped.

The nearest living body was Odo again.

He had moved three paces west to mark the slate. The eight years hit him square. His breath left him in a rush. He braced a hand on the cliff face, the other on his chest. The skin there, Kael saw, had held a mottled purple-grey pattern, the mark of a heart that labored. The pattern faded. The color normalized. Odo drew a breath full and clean, the sound of it different, deeper. He stood straight. He looked at his hands. He looked at the stone. He looked at Sol.

"Eight," he said. "Eight years on a heart that forgot how to beat clean."

Tessa's chalk moved. "Gauge reads zero. Eight years transferred. Nearest living body: Odo Marl, tech, fifty."

Sol turned then. Her face gave nothing away, but her eyes held the question again, louder now. Nine stones. The rule held every time. The stored years did not vanish. They moved.

Kael met her gaze. The wind carried the scent of hot mineral water from the boiling house vents ahead. The path dropped away, winding down toward the cellar mouth and the iron door with the enamelled knot.

"Two stones remain," Tessa said. She capped her chalk. The slate clicked shut. "Stone ten and stone eleven. Then the main tap."

"The metering chamber," Odo said. His voice was steady. The limp was gone. "Ansa's dark gallery lamp."

"Ansa's lamp is the metering chamber at the main tap," Kael said. "Not a small line. The gauge there reads the sum."

"Seventeen," Tessa said. "Unchanged since Renn was found."

"Unchanged since the nearest living body stopped standing near the plate."

The words laid themselves out between them. The eleven ticks on Renn's sheet. The twelve breaths of the charge cycle. The second line adding its breath. The works steward's hand leaning right. The carried lines on the leaf. The name Ashworth in the newest row.

Sol's hand found Kael's sleeve again. Third touch. She did not grip. She did not turn him. Her fingers rested on the wool at his forearm, light as the chalk line at Tessa's belt. The wind gusted, cold off the water.

Kael covered her hand with his own. The square palm warm. The fingers closing slow. He did not look down. He did not pull away.

The promise held: if she asked, he would go before he argued. Even the argument in his head.

He walked, and she walked beside him, and the wind moved between them carrying the scent of iron and salt and the slow rot of hides. The rope between them was not hemp. It was the carried lines on a ledger leaf. The seventeen years on a gauge that did not go down at night. The pegs waiting.

He knew the answer to the question she would not voice. What happens when the line we mean to close holds seventeen years, and the nearest living body is not a stranger's bad knee?

The answer walked beside him, her hand on his sleeve, her breath matching twelve beats in the wind.

The answer was her.
