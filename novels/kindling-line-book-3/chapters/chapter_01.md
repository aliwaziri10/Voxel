The lamp had been trimmed, the wick even, the oil fresh. Kael turned it a fraction. Then he turned it back. The stick stood propped against the wall where he had set it, and he moved it two inches to the left. The page on the table — his fourth page, folded and refolded until the creases had softened to cloth — he picked it up, smoothed it, set it down, picked it up again.

Sol sat on the narrow cot with her boots off, her back against the cold plaster. The room above the tar-shop held the smell of pine pitch and old bread, and the quiet that comes after the longest day. Ansa had left the loaf on the shelf with a cloth over it, and they had eaten standing at the window, tearing crusts in the dark while the Lower Reach settled below them.

"You wrote it down," she said, "so you could not change your mind halfway through saying it."

Kael stopped with the page in his hand. The lamplight caught the silver at his throat, the plain clasp Thorne grey. "That is exactly why."

He held it out to her. She did not take it at once. She watched him standing there on the leg that still remembered the boiling house, the needles he had described climbing from ankle to hip, the way he swung it from the hip on stairs as though it belonged to another man.

"Sit," she said. "Read it to me. Or I will read it myself while you stand there and shake."

He sat. The cot dipped under his weight, the frame protesting. He placed the page on his knee and did not open it.

"You read it," he said. "I have read it enough."

She took it. The ink was dark, his hand upright, the descenders closed and careful. She read slowly on purpose, letting each line settle. The lamp threw their shadows long against the wall behind them.

*I will not let the count be spoken by one voice. I will not let the warrant be sealed by one hand. I will not let the room be entered by one body while the other waits outside.*

She turned the page. A line had been crossed through, the ink pressed hard enough to bite the fibre. Under it, another line in the same hand.

She traced the crossed-out words with a fingertip. "Read this one to me."

His jaw tightened. He took the page back, held it closer to the flame.

"I would spend my years first," he read, low. "Every one of them. Before a single one of yours is touched."

He paused. The next line was not crossed out.

"I do not know how to want you safe without standing in front of you."

The words hung in the small room. The tar-shop below them creaked as the timbers cooled.

Sol did not reach for him. She did not soften her voice. "That is the whole problem, and you wrote it down."

He set the page on the table. His hand stayed on it, the square palm, the fingers scarred from the wheel.

"I wrote it down because it is true," he said. "Because I have tried to unwrite it in my head for six weeks, and it will not unwrite."

She took the pen from the table. The nib caught the light. She turned the page to the blank space beneath his words, pressed the nib to the fibre, and wrote.

*Then we say the numbers aloud. Every time. No silent counts.*

She signed it I.V. Her hand leaned the other way from his, the letters slanting left where his stood straight. He watched her write as if it were the first time anyone had written on his page.

When she set the pen down, he turned his hand over on the table, palm up. An invitation. She placed her fingers in his, the touch light, deliberate.

"Come here," she said.

They moved to the window sill together, shoulder to shoulder, the lamp behind them throwing a single shadow on the glass. The Lower Reach spread out below, the ropewalk lines strung between the roofs, the tar kettles dark for the night. Somewhere a bell struck, low and slow.

She leaned into him. He did not pull away. His thigh pressed against hers, warm through the wool of his trousers and the linen of her shift.

"I want to say it," she said. "My count. Out loud. For the first time since the Reckoning. And I want you to hear it."

"Tell me now."

She smiled, the corner of her mouth lifting. "Ask me again in an hour."

"I will."

He did not joke. He did not bargain. He sat beside her in the lamplight, his hand still in hers, and waited.

The hour passed in the way hours do when nothing is required of them. The bell struck again. The tar-shop settled. Kael shifted once, the stick scraping the floor as he adjusted his weight, and Sol counted the breaths between the strikes in her head, the way she always did.

When the third strike faded, he turned his head. "An hour."

She laughed, quiet and surprised. "You actually counted."

"I said I would."

"Then listen."

She turned to face him, drawing her legs up on the sill. "Eighteen. Eighteen years left. That is the number I carried out of the Reckoning chamber, and it has not changed since."

He did not flinch. He did not look away. His thumb moved over her knuckles, once.

"Eighteen," he repeated. "And I am at thirty-four. Or near enough."

"Thirty-four," she said. "Sixteen between us."

"Sixteen," he agreed. "And we say them aloud. Every time."

"Every time," she said. "That is the promise."

The lamp flame guttered. The shadow on the glass trembled.

Then Sol went still. Her hand tightened on his. The air in the room shifted, a pressure at the base of her skull, a vibration in the iron bolts that held the window frame to the wall. She knew that pressure. She had felt it in the flooded passage, in the stair chamber, in the palm pressed to the trunk line.

The Kindling was stirring. The pulse from the east had arrived.

"Now," she said.

She rose from the sill. Kael rose with her, the stick finding the floor automatically. She moved to the ladder that led to the roof hatch, her bare feet silent on the rungs. He followed, close enough that she felt the heat of him at her back.

The night air hit her face, cold and smelling of river and coal smoke. The roofs of the Lower Reach stretched out in grey ridges, the ropewalk lines invisible in the dark. She walked to the parapet and laid her palm flat on the iron strap that ran the length of the roof's spine.

The pulse came up through the metal. Eleven breaths. She had counted it at the stair. She counted it now.

In four. Hold six. Out eight.

She let the Kindling answer. A small flare, deliberate, controlled. She counted the burn in heartbeats. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.

Six heartbeats. Half a year.

The cost settled in her chest, a familiar weight. She breathed through it, the way she had been taught, the way she had taught herself.

Kael stood at the parapet beside her, within arm's reach. She felt him take the edge of it, the small cost jumping the gap between them. He did not flinch. He did not step back. His face in the starlight showed nothing but the habit she had watched him build, the instinct to stand in front.

"Stand back," she said. "Well outside the room's reach."

He looked at her. The wind caught the white hair at his temple. "Yes."

The word came before the argument. She saw it in the set of his jaw, the way his shoulders shifted. He opened his mouth to speak, to negotiate, to tell her why he could not.

Then he closed it. He stepped back. Two steps. Three. Until the parapet iron lay between them and the roof hatch yawned dark behind him.

She watched him go. The flare faded. The pulse in the iron continued, steady as a heartbeat.

She took her ledger from her coat pocket, found the next clean line, and wrote: *30 Cinderveil night. Flare test. Six heartbeats. Half year. Count: eighteen.*

The ink dried black on the page. She closed the book.

The iron strap under her palm gave the eleventh breath half a beat early, as if answering.

The iron strap under her palm gave the eleventh breath half a beat early, as if answering.

The vibration travelled up her arm, not sharp but insistent, a nudge at the bone. She had felt the second line before, in the stair chamber, in the flooded passage, but never like this — never as a direct reply to her own flare. The Kindling in the anchor plate had always been a echo. This was a conversation.

Kael stood where she had put him, a dark shape against the roofline. The wind moved his coat. He had not reached for his stick. He was watching her, she knew, the way he watched the wheel when the count approached the trough.

"Did you feel that?" she asked. Her voice came out lower than she meant, swallowed by the night.

He did not answer at once. The ropewalk lines hummed somewhere below, a sound like a held breath. Then: "The early beat."

"Yes."

"Eleven and a half."

"Eleven and a half." She kept her palm on the strap. The metal was cold, colder than the air, and the pulse ran through it steady as a second heart. "The second line answered me."

Kael took a step forward. Then stopped. The parapet iron gleamed between them, the boundary she had drawn. "It answered the flare."

"It answered the cost." She turned her hand over, pressing her fingers to the metal. "Half a year. I paid it. And the line — the east line — it knew."

The wind shifted, carrying the scent of wet stone and something sharper, mineral, rising from the cliff face. The boiling house lay that way, buried under generations of soot and silence. Renn's chalk had marked the second stair: *THIS ONE HAS NO BOTTOM I HAVE FOUND*. Sol had read those words in the lamplight of the research wing and felt the weight of them settle in her ribs.

"Renn said it had no bottom," she said. "He wrote it on the stair riser. Chalk, his own hand."

Kael's shoulders shifted. "He also said the east pulse adds a breath to every cycle. Ten becomes eleven. Eleven becomes twelve." He paused. "You counted twelve at the stair."

"I counted eleven at the stair. The wheel closed on eleven. The pulse came after." She lifted her hand from the strap. The vibration lingered in her fingertips. "But just now, when I flared — the eleventh breath came early. Half a beat. As if the line was waiting for me to finish."

"Waiting."

"Listening." The word surprised her. She had not meant to say it. "The Kindling doesn't listen. It draws. It takes. That's the rule."

"Nearest living body pays." Kael's voice was flat, reciting. "A fraction stays with the wielder. Transfer is involuntary."

"Then what was that?"

He did not answer. The bell in the Lower Reach struck again, four slow notes spaced wide. Between the third and fourth, the iron strap pulsed once more — eleven, twelve, a thirteenth that faded before it could be counted.

Sol breathed out. "The gauge reads seventeen. Renn said it was fourteen when he went down. Three years rose while he was locked in the third gallery. He was the nearest living body."

"Renn," Kael said. "The surveyor. The one who — "

"Who vanished the night Mara was taken. The night you went into the cellar." She turned to face him fully. The starlight caught the white hair at his temple, the line of his jaw. "You never told me what you saw down there. Only that the swell took your leg. That you called for Mara. That she ran in."

"I didn't see the gauge. I didn't know about the stored years." He spoke quietly. "I knew the line had taken her. I knew the cold would climb. Yelva said it would rise on nights after someone went below. The wrists on the fourth. Forearms by Day 8. Elbows by Day 27." He counted on his fingers, the gesture old and unconscious. "Today is Day 0. The thirtieth of Cinderveil. Her wrists are cold tonight."

Sol felt the number settle in her chest. Day 0. The petition entered. The Deed set aside by nine of twelve houses on the twenty-eighth. Aldous Thorne to be questioned tomorrow about who taught him his letters. The Chancellor's question hanging in the air: *If the hand you find belongs to a house we have both trusted, what will you do then?*

And underneath it all, the second line breathing twelve breaths to a cycle, storing seventeen years, waiting for the twin brass pegs to be pulled in one breath by two pullers beyond the room's reach.

"Odo and Tessa," she said. "The techs. They're the pullers. Renn's map will show where the pegs sit." She touched the ledger in her pocket. "Renn's map. Ulla has it. Renn's wife. She gave me a folded survey of the east, one cellar marked warm."

"Ulla." Kael tested the name. "Renn's wife. You met her."

"Tonight. Before the Council. She came to the research wing. She knew where to find me." Sol's thumb traced the edge of the ledger through her coat. "She said Renn would want me to have it. She said he'd want someone to use it."

"Someone who knows how to count."

"Someone who knows how to wait." She looked at the roof hatch, the dark square gap leading back down to the room, the lamp, the page with his crossed-out line and her added promise. "We say the numbers aloud now. Every time. That was the promise."

"Every time," he agreed.

The bell struck its final note. The vibration hung in the air, in the iron, in the space between them.

"Come down," she said. "The ledger's not finished. I need to write the rest of tonight. The petition. The count. The flare. The early beat." She paused. "And I need you to hear the next number when I say it. Not in an hour. Tomorrow. After Aldous."

Kael moved then, closing the distance in three long strides, the stick forgotten against the parapet. He stopped at the edge of the iron strap, close enough that she felt the warmth of him, the familiar scent of wool and metal and something underneath, something that smelled like the stair chamber and the wheel and the night they had first counted together.

"Tomorrow," he said. "After Aldous. I'll be there."

"Good."

She turned for the hatch. He followed, one step behind, the way he always did when she led. The ladder rungs were cold under her palms. She descended into the dark, the lamp's glow rising to meet her, and behind her the roof breathed with the pulse of a line that had just learned her name.
