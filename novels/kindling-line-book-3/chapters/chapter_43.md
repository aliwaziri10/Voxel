Grey light filled the yard of the boiling house. The Thorne guard stood in a line at the far gate, grey on grey, and did not shift a boot. Mara sat on her stool at the cellar mouth with her stick across her knees and her face turned toward the stair. The air carried the smell of tar and mineral dust and the faint yeast-sour tang that rose from below.

Sol stood beside Kael at the head of the stair. Her undyed coat was folded over her arm. The lantern hung from a hook on the wall, its glass clean, its wick trimmed.

"Pass me my coat," she said.

Kael waited. A count of three passed, marked by the hum that rose from the stairwell. Eleven breaths and a twelfth.

"Yes," he said. He took the coat from her arm and held it out, the wool heavy in his hands.

She slipped her arms into the sleeves. The fabric settled across her shoulders. She did not fasten the front.

"Hold the lantern," she said.

Another count of three. The wind off the cliff stirred the flame behind the glass.

"Yes." Kael lifted the lantern from the hook. The light shifted across the yard, across the guard, across Mara on her stool.

"If you are going to do it in front of me, you might do it faster," Mara said, without turning her head. "This stool is hard."

Sol smiled. "We are almost done."

"Take my left hand on the stair," she said to Kael.

The third count. The hum rose and fell.

"Yes." His fingers closed around hers, warm and rough, the callus from the rope pressing into her palm.

Odo's voice came up from the turn of the stair. "I take everything twice."

Sol leaned over the stair mouth and found him on the landing below, his broad shoulders filling it. Tessa stood beside him, smaller and quicker, the chalk-line at her belt and a coil of cord over her arm.

"Then I love you twice," Sol called down.

Odo's deep voice rolled up the stone. "Once is plenty. I will take the second as read."

Tessa cupped a hand to her mouth. "I want it on record."

"The record is my coat," Sol said.

Kael's hand tightened on hers. His face went still, the way it did when a list was forming behind his eyes. He opened his mouth. "I wish I could do it."

He stopped. He had heard it as she had. The rest of the sentence stood between them unsaid, and she watched him choose.

"I wish I could be there," he said.

Sol's thumb traced the knuckle of his index finger. "That is the true sentence. Do not say the other one. Not even in your head. Not today."

He held her gaze. The grey at his temples caught the light. The white strand above his ear. The shadow under his cheekbone where the stick had pressed.

"I will try," he said.

"I will take try."

She stepped into him. The kiss was quick and hard, her mouth on his, her hand flat on his chest as if she could feel the answer through his ribs. He held her face in both hands, his thumbs brushing her cheekbones, and said her name.

"Sol."

No number. No count. Only the name.

She pulled back. Her breath came uneven. "That is the best thing anyone has ever said to me."

She turned toward the stair. Over her shoulder she called to Mara, "I am going to be a long time and a short time."

Mara shifted on her stool. The wood creaked. "I have all day and all night. I am good at it."

Kael went with her as far as the cellar mouth and stopped at its edge, the stick planted on the stone. The map said the foot was hers. He gave her six steps of the stair, and then he followed, because that was the order.

Sol went down first. Her peg lay wrapped in cloth in the crook of her left arm, and her right hand trailed the wall. The stone was cold at the mouth, then cool, then warm as the stair bent. The hum gathered in her ribs. The air thickened and smelled of mineral and old iron.

Below her, Odo and Tessa had gone on with their cords coiled over their shoulders. Their boots rang on the stone, and their voices came back up the curve of the wall.

"The sill mark is good," Tessa said.

"The sill mark was always good," Odo said.

"It was good after I corrected it."

"It was good before. You corrected it into the place it already was."

"Then the chalk will say so again."

The argument faded down the stair, a familiar rhythm. Sol listened to it go. For forty days she had listened to the two of them dispute a hair's breadth of chalk, and she found that she wanted a great many more years of it. A long run of ordinary arguments, with nobody waiting at the bottom.

Behind her Kael came on, his stick finding each tread, keeping the gap the map allowed. She could hear it without turning: the ferrule, the step, the small pause where the left leg took the weight. She did not look round. She had asked him for that gap, and she would not spoil it by checking that he kept it.

The stair narrowed at the turn. The walls drew in and the warmth thickened. She passed the landing where, two days ago, she had stopped him and he had waited, and she did not stop there now. Kael did not stop either. Today the map brought him to the foot.

The last steps were steep, and the stone was hot through the soles of her boots. Kael held the lantern above her from the stair, and it threw her shadow down ahead of her onto the bare iron door. The plate sat in its face like a heart in a ribcage.

She stood before it. It had been warm like skin the first time she touched it, and warm again two days ago. Today it was warmer, as if it knew what the day was. The heat came up through her boots and into her palms as she held them out.

Odo and Tessa waited at the foot with their cords laid out along the bottom step. The two sockets showed in the lantern light, each ringed in chalk, one in the lintel above the plate and one in the sill below it. Odo ran his thumb round each ring in turn, then stood back and said nothing at all, which from Odo was a blessing.

Kael came down the last steps and stopped at the foot, an arm's reach behind her. He set the lantern on the lowest step. His peg was inside his coat, wrapped in its cloth, and he took it out and held it against his chest.

"Kael," Sol said. "Will you set your peg in the sill?"

She asked it aloud, as they had learned to do. He waited the count of three. Neither of them remarked on it any longer.

"Yes," he said.

He laid the stick against the wall and went down on his right knee, the left leg stretched out stiff beside him, and unwrapped the cloth. The sill socket was low, at the height of his hand. He set the peg home with both palms. It seated with a small exact sound, a latch closing. Then he stayed where he was, kneeling, and looked up at her. He had done what she asked, and now he waited to hear whether it was what she wanted.

"Is it right?" he asked.

"Yes," she said. "That is exactly right."

The breath he let go was loud in the narrow place. He took the stick and got to his feet, slow and careful, and stood back to his arm's reach.

Sol unwrapped her own peg. The lintel socket was above her head, and she had to rise on the step to reach it. She pushed it home with the heel of her hand. It seated with the same small sound, and the plate between the two pegs hummed a half note lower.

Odo tried each peg with his thumb, twice, and said, "Seated." Tessa tried them after him and said, "Seated." They looked at each other with the mild disappointment of two people who had found nothing to argue about.

Odo took the end of each cord and made it fast to its peg with the knot he trusted, and tugged each one twice. Then he and Tessa paid the cords out behind them and climbed, slack running through their hands, up the stair to the turn. From there, in the dimness, Tessa read the gauge. She read it again.

"Seventeen," she called down. "Fixed."

"Seventeen," Odo said after her. "Fixed."

Sol sat down on the single step at the plate. The iron was warm through her coat, through the wool of her trousers. She laid her palm flat on it, and it hummed under her hand, steady as a sleeper breathing. Behind her Kael stood at the foot, an arm's reach off, the stick planted on the stone. She did not turn. She could feel him there the way she could feel the warm iron, as a fact that did not need her to look at it.

She had told him once, in the days when the numbers were all she had, that she did not want to be paid in years. Today she would pay in them instead, a half year of her own, and take what the line gave back on its own terms. She turned the thought over and found no one else's hand in it. It was the first thing in the whole business that did not feel like another person's decision. She waited for the old fear to climb her throat and found it sitting quietly, a guest who had decided not to stay.

She had said those words to him once before, in a cellar, in a worse hour, and he had stood back unasked and been hurt for it. Today the sentence was not an order and not a plea. It was a plan, with his name in it and his answer already known.

She spoke aloud, because it was the rule and because she meant it.

"Kael, stand back."

He did not hesitate. He did not argue. He said, "Yes."

He took up the stick. He turned. He went up the stair, the ferrule clicking on each tread, past Odo and Tessa at the turn without a word, and on to the door of the passage at the head of the stair. He did not look back.

Tessa's voice came down from the turn, flat and exact, counting the drop in the trough. "Falling."

Sol breathed. The iron was warm under her palm. She asked it, in the plain way she had asked it two days ago, and this time it answered.

"One," she said.

The cold came up out of her bones. She had expected fire. She had burned before, and fire was the shape the cost took when it was Kael's years or her own. This was the thing underneath the fire, a long slow pull, as though a thread had been caught at the center of her and was being drawn out through the skin.

"Two."

"Falling," Tessa said again, quieter.

"Three." Nobody at her shoulder. Nobody within arm's reach in the whole narrow stair, nobody the cost could slide toward. Whatever it took would come out of her and out of no one else, and she held to that as she held to the iron.

"Four."

She felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the small bones of her hands.

"Five."

It was a cold she had no name for, and it was hers.

"Six."

Half a year. It went out of her through her palm, each beat a withdrawal, each beat a letting go, and the iron took it.

"Now," she said.

Odo and Tessa pulled in one breath.

The pegs came free, hers from the lintel and his from the sill, both in the same breath and the same motion. The cords ran back up the stair, and the pegs ran with them, and a clatter at the turn said that Odo and Tessa had them.

The plate gave one long sound, a note that lived in the iron and the stone and the air, and then went silent.

The line closed.

From the turn Odo said, low, "One breath. Both out." Tessa said, "The pull was clean," and then, to herself, "The trough is level," as if she did not quite believe it. "I will read it again," Odo said, and did.

The whole stored seventeen went to Sol as the nearest living body. She felt it arrive, all of it, like a tide rising in the dark. Years and years and years, flooding the space where the cold had been. They did not hurt. She had braced for pain, and what came instead was weight, enormous and even, settling into her the way water settles into a hollow.

Among the years there was one she knew. Her mother's. She knew it the way she would have known her mother's step on a stair, before the face, before the name. It sat in the flood, distinct and familiar, a single thread in a vast weave.

She said aloud, "I have them. I will carry them."

The iron was still warm beneath her palm. Then it began to cool.

From the cellar mouth, far above, Mara's voice came down the stair: "Now."

Soon had become now. Sol cried. The tears came hot and without asking, falling onto the cooling iron, onto her coat, onto the stone step beneath her. Nobody was there to see it, and she let it come.

Up at the head of the stair Kael stayed at the door of the passage. He did not come down. He did not call. She knew that he stood there with the stick planted and his whole body leaning toward her, and that he would stay exactly as he was until she asked, because they had made that the rule, and because he had learned it at a cost.

She had thought it would feel like triumph. It did not. It felt like carrying a loaded cart up a stair she had never climbed, in a body that had not yet learned the weight. She was not lighter. She was heavier than she had been in all her life, and it was the heaviness of a thing that was hers.

Then, slowly, the weight began to warm. It started under her ribs, where the cold had lived, a faint glow like the first coal of a banked fire, and it spread. It reached her shoulders. It reached her hands and her wrists and the cheeks where the tears were drying, and it felt, absurdly, like being forgiven for a thing she had never done.

The iron beneath her palm went on cooling. The heat that had met her skin steady and alive drew back by degrees, leaving the metal neutral and ordinary, a black plate in a bare door.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. The tears had not stopped, and she did not ask them to. Seventeen years sat in her, heavy as river stones in a current, and her mother's year among them, as distinct as a heartbeat in a held breath. She did not yet know what to do with them. She knew only that she would carry them, and that for the first time in her life she was not carrying anything alone.

"Kael," she said.

She did not shout. The stair carried it.

"Yes," he said, from the head of the stair.

She heard him start down. The ferrule met the first tread, and then the second. He came slowly, with the stick on every step, and she sat with her palm on the cooling iron and counted nothing at all. She had not known, until the stair gave back the sound of him, how much of her had been listening for it. The sound came closer. It passed the turn, where Odo and Tessa stood back against the wall to let him by, and neither spoke. The warmth in the stairwell had thinned, and the hum had dropped to a low note felt more in the ribs than heard.

Kael reached the foot. He stopped one step below her, the stick planted beside his boot. His breathing was even, the climb measured. He did not reach for her. He did not speak. He waited, his face turned up to hers in the lantern light, the grey at his temples caught in the glow, the white strand above his ear.

She looked down at him. The stick. The boots. The wool coat worn at the cuffs. The hand resting on the stick, fingers curved around the worn wood. The face she had asked to stand back, the face that had said yes and gone and waited and come when she called.

"Kael," she said.

The name left her a second time. Different from the call. Softer. Complete.
