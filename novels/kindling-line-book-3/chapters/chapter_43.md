Grey light filled the yard of the boiling house. The Thorne guard stood in a line at the far gate, copper clasps dull against Thorne grey. Mara sat on her stool at the cellar mouth, her stick across her knees, her face turned toward the stair. The air carried the smell of tar and mineral dust and the faint yeast-sour tang that rose from below.

Sol stood beside Kael at the bottom of the stair. Her undyed coat was folded over her arm. The lantern hung from a hook on the wall, its glass clean, its wick trimmed.

"Pass me my coat," she said.

Kael waited. A count of three passed, marked by the hum rising from the stairwell. Eleven breaths and a twelfth.

"Yes," he said. He took the coat from her arm and held it out, the wool heavy in his hands.

She slipped her arms into the sleeves. The fabric settled across her shoulders. She did not fasten the front.

"Hold the lantern," she said.

Another count of three. The wind off the cliff stirred the flame behind the glass.

"Yes." Kael lifted the lantern from the hook. The light shifted across the yard, across the guard, across Mara on her stool.

Mara's voice carried up the stair. "If you are going to do it in front of me you might do it faster. This stool is hard."

Sol smiled without turning. "We are almost done."

"Take my left hand on the stair," she said to Kael.

The third count. The hum rose and fell.

"Yes." His fingers closed around hers, warm and rough, the callus from the rope pressing into her palm.

Odo's voice floated down from the turn of the stair. "I take everything twice."

Sol looked up. Odo stood on the landing above, broad shoulders blocking the light, his chalk-line belt catching the grey. Tessa stood beside him, smaller, quicker, her own chalk at her belt.

"Then I love you twice," Sol called up.

Odo's deep voice rolled down the stone. "Once is plenty. I will take the second as read."

Tessa cupped a hand to her mouth. "I want it on record."

"The record is my coat," Sol said.

Kael's hand tightened on hers. His face went still in the way it did when a list formed behind his eyes. He opened his mouth. "I wish I could —"

He stopped. The words hung between them, unfinished.

"I wish I could be there," he said.

Sol's thumb traced the knuckle of his index finger. "That is the true sentence. Do not say the other one. Not even in your head. Not today."

He held her gaze. The grey at his temples caught the light. The white strand above his ear. The shadow under his cheekbone where the stick had pressed.

"I will try," he said.

"I will take try."

She stepped into him. The kiss was quick and hard, her mouth on his, her hand flat on his chest as if she could feel the answer through his ribs. He held her face in both hands, his thumbs brushing her cheekbones, and said her name.

"Sol."

No number. No count. Only the name.

She pulled back. Her breath came uneven. "That is the best thing anyone has ever said to me."

She turned toward the stair. Over her shoulder she called to Mara, "I am going to be a long time and a short time."

Mara shifted on her stool. The wood creaked. "I have all day and all night. I am good at it."

Kael followed Sol to the cellar mouth. He stopped at the edge, his stick planted on the stone. The map said the foot was hers. The rule said the nearest living body paid. He would not be nearer.

Sol went down first. The iron rungs were cold under her boots, then warm, then hot as she neared the turn. The hum rose, a vibration in her ribs, in the soles of her feet. The air thickened, smelling of mineral and old iron.

Odo and Tessa had gone ahead. Their cords lay coiled over their shoulders, thick hemp ropes that would carry the twin brass pegs. Their boots rang on the iron, slow and deliberate.

"Your reading of the sill was off by a hair," Tessa said, her voice bouncing off the curved walls. "The chalk will show it."

"My reading was exact," Odo replied, not looking back. "Yours was the one that wandered."

"Then the chalk will show that too."

The argument faded into the stairwell, a familiar rhythm. Sol watched them descend, the lantern light swallowing them whole. She had watched them do this for years. She would watch them do it again.

Kael followed her down. The stick clicked on every riser. Click. Step. Click. Step. The sound was steady, deliberate, the sound of a man being slow on purpose.

The stair narrowed at the turn. The wall closed in. The warmth thickened. The hum rose.

Sol stopped at the landing. The plate waited below, twenty-three steps down, the bare iron door set in the stone, the warm black plate in its face. She could see the edge of it from here, a darker rectangle in the lantern glow.

"Wait here," she said to Kael.

His hand tightened on the stick. "I can see the plate from the turn."

"You can watch from there. And you are allowed one day of watching. Tomorrow you will be at the door."

"That is a very generous allowance."

"I am a generous woman. You should not tell anyone."

He did not move from the landing. The stick stayed planted. His weight settled on the good leg, the left finding its careful purchase.

Sol went on alone. The last steps were steep, the iron cold under her boots, then warm, then hot as she neared the foot. The plate sat in the door like a heart in a ribcage. The air shimmered above it.

She stood before it. The enamelled knot on the Upper Iron Door upstairs had been warm like skin. This was warmer. The heat came through the soles of her boots, up her calves, into her palms as she held them out.

She laid her hand flat on the iron. The warmth met her palm, steady and alive.

Odo and Tessa reached the foot. They uncoiled their cords with practised efficiency. The twin brass pegs lay wrapped in cloth at their feet, the ledge peg and the table peg, same mold, the second placed.

"The sockets," Odo said. "Lintel and sill. One in the lintel above the plate, one in the sill below it."

Tessa knelt. Her chalk marked the sill socket. "Depth confirmed."

Odo checked the lintel. "Depth confirmed."

Kael descended the final steps. He stopped at the foot, an arm's reach behind Sol. The map said the foot was hers. The rule said the nearest living body paid. He would not be nearer.

Joren and Corren stood at the top of the cellar steps, grey cloaks motionless in the lantern light. The Thorne guard held the line at the east approach. Aldous and the works steward waited in the yard, the steward's hands folded in her sleeves, Aldous's hand light on her elbow. The pick stood beyond them, Council grey, unnamed, patient.

Mara's voice came down the stair, clear and calm. "The gauge reads seventeen."

Odo and Tessa climbed the cords to the turn of the stair. Their boots found the iron rungs. The ropes ran through their hands, smooth and sure.

Sol sat on the single step at the plate. The iron was warm through her coat, through the wool of her trousers. The heat rose into her, a slow tide.

Kael stood at the foot, his stick planted on the stone step. His face was turned toward her, the grey light catching the line of his jaw, the white strand above his ear.

She spoke aloud, because it was the rule and because she meant it.

"Kael, stand back."

He did not hesitate. He did not argue. He said, "Yes."

He turned. He went up the stair, stick clicking on each riser. Click. Step. Click. Step. He reached the turn. He reached the door of the passage. He did not look back.

Tessa's voice counted from the turn. "One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six."

Sol burned half a year. Six heartbeats. The cost came out of her and nobody near, and she felt it as a cold pulled out of the bones. Each beat a withdrawal. Each beat a letting go.

At the sixth heartbeat she said, "Now."

Odo and Tessa pulled in one breath.

---

The pegs came free. The ledge peg from the lintel socket. The table peg from the sill socket. Both in the same breath. Both in the same motion.

The plate gave one long sound, a note that lived in the iron and the stone and the air, and then went silent.

The line closed.

The whole stored seventeen went to Sol as the nearest living body. She felt it arrive, all of it, like a tide rising in the dark. Years and years and years, seventeen of them, flooding the space where the cold had been.

Among the years there was one she knew. Her mother's. The year carried off her mother's row on the leaf, the year she had decided to count as hers. It sat in the flood, distinct and familiar, a thread in the weave.

She said aloud, "I have them. I will carry them."

The iron was still warm beneath her palm. Then it began to cool.

From the cellar mouth Mara's voice came down the stair: "Now."

Sol cried. The tears tracked hot down her cheeks, falling onto the cooling iron, onto her coat, onto the stone step beneath her.

Kael stayed at the door of the passage. He did not move. He did not speak. He waited.

She called his name. "Kael."

He said, "Yes."

He came down the stair. Stick. Step. Stick. Step. The sound approached, slow and deliberate, the sound of a man being slow on purpose.

The sound drew closer. Click. Step. Click. Step. Each impact of the ferrule on iron marked the distance closing between them. The warmth in the stairwell had thinned, the hum dropping to a low vibration felt more in the ribs than heard.

Sol wiped her cheeks with the heel of her hand. The tears kept coming, hot and silent, and she let them. The seventeen years settled in her, heavy as river stones in a current. Not triumph. Not relief. Weight first, pressing against the cavity where the cold had lived. Her mother's year among them, distinct as a heartbeat in a held breath.

The iron beneath her palm continued its slow cooling. The heat that had met her skin steady and alive now retreated, leaving the metal neutral, ordinary, a black plate in a bare door.

Kael reached the foot. He stopped one step below her, the stick planted beside his boot. His breathing was even, the climb measured. He did not reach for her. He did not speak. He waited, his face turned up to hers in the lantern light, the grey at his temples caught in the glow, the white strand above his ear.

The weight in her chest shifted. The pressing stones began to warm.

She looked down at him. The stick. The boots. The wool coat worn at the cuffs. The hand resting on the stick, fingers curved around the worn wood. The face she had kissed in the yard, the face she had asked to stand back, the face that had said yes and gone and waited and come back.

"Kael," she said.

The name left her a second time. Different from the call. Softer. Complete.

He exhaled. The tension in his shoulders released, a long slow uncoiling. His hand left the stick and found her knee, heavy and warm through the wool.

"Sol," he said.

The hum in the stairwell rose and fell, eleven breaths and a twelfth. The gauge above read seventeen, fixed and unmoving. The twin brass pegs lay in the cloth at Odo's feet, their work done. The line was closed.

Mara's stool creaked at the cellar mouth. The Thorne guard held their line at the east approach. The steward stood in the yard with her hands folded in her sleeves. Aldous beside her. The pick in Council grey. All of them waiting above while the stair held its two figures in the grey light.

Sol covered his hand with her own. The weight in her chest had become warmth, spreading slow as dawn, reaching her fingertips, her toes, the crown of her head. Seventeen years. Her mother's year. The half year she had just burned. Sixteen and a half becoming thirty-three.

She did not say the number. She would not say it until he did.

"I am here," he said.

"I know."

The stick lay between them on the stone step. The plate cooled behind her. The air in the chamber stilled.

Above them the watch would change. Boots on cobbles. The lantern passed from hand to hand. The pick would rise from the stone at the Thorne line or would not. The steward would ask to be given to no one until the line was closed, and the line was closed, and she had not been given.

Sol leaned forward. Her forehead touched his. The contact was small. The contact was everything.

The warmth in her chest pulsed, steady as the hum in the stone. Thirty-three. Thirty-three each. They would grow old together.

Kael's thumb traced the knuckle of her index finger, slow, deliberate. The callus there. The line where the rope had bitten deep.

"Tomorrow," he said. "The world above."

"Tomorrow," she agreed.

He kissed her then, on the mouth, slow and careful, tasting salt and iron and the thing that had no name. She let him. She met him. The seventeen years turned in her, settling into the shape of a life.

When they parted the grey light had deepened. The stairwell held the last of the day's warmth. The plate was cool beneath her palm.

Sol stood. Her legs held. The weight was there, and the warmth, and the seventeen years breathing in her chest.

Kael rose beside her. The stick found his hand. He planted it. The ferrule bit the stone.

They began the climb. Click. Step. Click. Step. The sound echoed behind them, the sound of a man being slow on purpose, the sound of a woman walking beside him.

At the turn Odo and Tessa waited, cords coiled, pegs wrapped. Their faces were unreadable in the lantern glow.

"Gauge?" Odo asked.

"Seventeen," Sol said. "Fixed."

Tessa nodded once. "The pull was clean."

"One breath," Odo said. "Both pegs."

They fell in behind. Joren and Corren stood at the top of the cellar steps, grey cloaks motionless. The yard opened above, the far gate, the Thorne line, the three figures in Council grey.

Mara sat on her stool at the cellar mouth. She did not rise. Her eyes followed them up the final steps, river-water winter eyes.

"You are late," she said. "My stool is hard."

Sol laughed. The sound surprised her. It came from somewhere below the weight and the warmth, a place she had not known remained.

"We are here now," she said.

Mara's mouth curved. "So you are."

The cellar mouth yawned. The boiling-house cellar spread beyond, the Upper Iron Door in its wall, the enamelled knot warm like skin. The yard lay past that, the evening air cool on her face.

Kael stopped at the top of the stair. The stick planted. He turned to her.

The lantern light caught the grey at his temple, the white strand above his ear, the shadow under his cheekbone.

"Kael," she said.

The name a third time. A promise. A lifetime.

He smiled, the small thing, not a smile but something nearer to it.

"Yes," he said.

The night watch turned above them. Boots on cobbles. The hum continued its eleven-and-twelfth rhythm, indifferent and eternal.

They walked out of the stairwell together, into the yard, into the evening, into the after.
