The lamp in Yelva's back room burned low, its wick trimmed to a nub that threw more shadow than light. Mara lay on the narrow cot against the far wall, her breathing shallow, the grey wool blanket pulled to her chin. Her wrists were wrapped, the bandages clean, the cold settled now in her forearms. Yelva had said it would reach her elbows within the month.

A clay pot sat on the stool beside Kael, the tea inside gone cold hours ago. Yelva's blend, bitter root and pine needle and a bitterness that tasted of old bark. He had not drunk it. Sol had taken three sips and set it down, grimacing.

"She scolds the dead and the living in the same voice," Sol whispered, her head tilted back against the wall, eyes half closed. "And she prefers the dead. They do not argue back."

Kael's mouth twitched. "Mara has been listening for twenty years. She probably agrees."

"She would tell you to drink the tea."

"She would tell me to sit still."

Sol made a small sound, not quite a laugh. Her undyed coat was rumpled at the shoulder where she had leaned into him. The wool smelled of mineral water and the tar shop's pine resin, underneath it the faint salt of her skin. Her hand rested on her knee, fingers curled loose.

"Yelva said the soup Ansa sent was thin," Sol murmured. "She said Ansa measures onions by the shadow they cast."

"Ansa measures everything by the shadow it casts. She has forty years of tar to prove it."

"She also said the stranger's chain was good iron. Coiled like a dead snake, she said. Tam repeated it. He likes her turns of phrase."

"Tam repeats everything Ansa says. He has since he was tall enough to reach the workbench."

The passage beyond the door was dark. Joren sat on the bench out there, his scarred hand resting on his knee, the older warden's silhouette a cutout against the faint glow from the healer's front room. Corren had taken the terrace path, stone five, the message already sent. Joren and Corren. Always Joren and Corren.

Sol's chin dropped toward her chest. Her eyes fluttered.

"You are awake," Kael said, quiet.

"Am." The word slurred. "Watching. The tea. The pot. The."

Her head tipped sideways. The crown of it came to rest against his shoulder, the weight of it sudden and warm. Her breath slowed, evening out against the wool of his coat. Her fingers, loose on her knee, slid downward, found the opening of his sleeve, and slipped inside. The tips of them brushed the skin of his forearm, cold at first, then warming.

Kael did not move.

His left leg had gone numb to the hip. The familiar hollow spread from the knee upward, the absence of sensation creeping along bone and tendon. He had sat through worse. He had walked through worse. He did not shift. He did not adjust. He let the numbness settle and kept his shoulder still beneath her head.

He counted her breaths. In. Out. In. Out. Seven. Eight. Nine. The rhythm was slower than his own, deeper. The line's pulse, the east-break stones, the metering chamber, the blue point steady at the window, usually ran beneath his counting like a second heartbeat. Tonight it did not. He realized he had stopped counting the line around breath four. He was only counting her.

The thought arrived unbidden: thirty years. Thirty winters. Thirty springs. He saw them in fragments, a garden he did not have, a table with two chairs, the grey of her hair deepening, the lines at his own eyes multiplying. He saw himself old and she old beside him, the arithmetic balanced at last.

He stopped the thought. He did not know whether he had thirty years. He did not know whether he had thirty days. A stranger was measuring the east approach with a chain. The pegs waited in his coat pocket and in Sol's pack.

And then he heard her voice in the dark of the tar shop, stripped of humour: *I want you to have them.*

The wish. Not an offer. A wish.

His throat tightened. There was a question he wanted to ask her. He did not know the words. He did not know whether he had the right. He thought of the next thirty years and then of her saying those six words, and the two collided in his chest like flint and steel.

Joren shifted on the bench in the passage. Boots on stone, slow, deliberate. The door pushed open a hand's width. The older warden's face appeared in the gap, scarred hand faintly visible. He looked at them. He said nothing. He withdrew, and a moment later a folded blanket landed across their knees, wool rough and smelling of lanolin and old sweat.

Kael did not look up. He did not thank him. He only adjusted his grip on his stick, the wood solid beneath his right hand, and let the blanket's weight settle.

His eyes went to the cot. Mara had not stirred in all the hours they had sat. Under the grey wool her bandaged hands lay palm down, the way she stood a post, with nothing about her that asked for pity. Twenty years she had walked a pace behind him and to the left, on the side where a blade would come from, and he had never once asked her why she chose that side.

On the fourth of Cinderveil he had said her name into the cellar dark, and she had run.

It was the plainest sentence he knew, and he could not make it mean less. He had not meant it as an order. He had only been a man in black water calling for the one person who always came. She had come. The line had taken her for it, and the cold climbed by the finger-width, and tonight he sat beside the result with a numb leg and a sleeping woman against his shoulder and a blanket another man had thrown over his knees.

A week ago he would have found a reason to leave the room. A rope to check, a door, a roster. He stayed. The shame stayed with him, quiet, no larger than the lamp. He found it could be borne in a chair.

He let himself picture the long road with her, once. He did not pretend he had not.

---

Mara's eyes opened.

The sound she made was not a word at first, a breath drawn sharp, the blanket rasping against her chin. Then two syllables, clear as a bell struck once.

"Renn. Down."

Her voice was thin and dry, the first sound she had made since the fourth of Cinderveil. Her gaze fixed on the ceiling beams, the shadows moving across them. Her bandaged wrists twitched beneath the wool.

Kael was on his feet before the silence returned. The numb leg screamed, pins and needles flooding the dead space, but he moved. The blanket slid to the floor. Sol's head came off the wall where his shoulder had been, her hand pulling free of his sleeve, fingers catching on the wool before releasing.

"I was not asleep," she said, which was the first thing out of her mouth and the least useful.

"Mara." Kael's voice came out rough. He crouched beside the cot. "Mara, hear me. Renn is missing. We are looking for him."

Her eyes did not move. The pupils were dilated, the irises pale in the lamplight. Her lips moved again, slower this time.

"Down. The stair. He goes down."

"Renn's chalk is on the second stair. We have been down it."

"Down." The word was final. Her eyes closed. Her breathing returned to its shallow rhythm. The cold in her forearms did not show on her face.

Kael stayed crouched. The pins and needles faded into a dull ache. Sol turned back the blanket's edge and laid two fingers on the bandage above Mara's wrist, held them there, and drew them away.

"The cold has not climbed since the lamp was lit," she said. "Yelva says it comes up in the dead of the night, one hour, every night. Tonight's is still ahead of us."

Her hand found his wrist, her touch light, questioning.

"She has not spoken since the fourth," Sol said, very quiet. "Two words. Renn. Down."

"She was there," Kael said. "Fourth of Cinderveil. She ran in after me. The line took her. She saw where he went."

"Or she knows where he goes now."

Kael's jaw tightened. Renn's chalk on the stair riser: *THIS ONE HAS NO BOTTOM I HAVE FOUND*. The warm cellar marked on Renn's sheet. The stranger's chain measuring the east approach from the boiling house cellar mouth to the cliff edge, matching the survey. And the third gallery, sealed, its lamp dark since before the petition.

The second stair had no bottom. If Renn had written that and still not come back, perhaps he had gone another way.

"I need to see the third gallery," Kael said. "At first light."

Sol's grip on his wrist tightened. "The third gallery is sealed. The Council sealed it long ago."

"Then we find how it was sealed. Renn was a surveyor. He does not confuse a stair with a gallery. He wrote what he found, and then he went to a place the chalk does not say."

"Kael." Her voice sharpened. "The air in there is bad. The way is narrow."

"I know."

"Then say the rest."

"The rest?"

"You are deciding. Your jaw does it." She did not raise her voice; Mara had gone under again. "I will not have you carry it to the door and hand it to me there, finished."

He weighed the sentence he had, the whole of it: that he meant to keep her out of the narrow places, out of the third gallery, out of any room where the dark closed to the width of a shoulder. He weighed what she would say to it. He gave her half.

"I do not like the gallery. It is narrow. I do not like you in narrow places."

"I know you do not." Her eyes held his, steady, grey in the gold light. "That is the half you gave me. We will have the other half on the stair, or before. I am keeping the place for it."

"Keep it."

The door to the passage opened wider. Corren stepped in, his hand loose at his side. Joren followed, the blanket folded over his arm.

"The sealed third gallery," Corren said, his voice pitched low for the room. "The door at the end of the east approach. I walked to it tonight, after the message. It has two peg-holes. The size of Renn's tally pegs."

"Old work?" Kael asked.

"The frame has gone green. Nobody has opened it in years, and nobody has tried to."

Kael's hand went to his coat pocket. The table peg, cold brass against his fingertips through the wool.

Nobody spoke. The lamp flame flickered. Sol's eyes held his, grey in the gold light, and he knew she was seeing the same thing: two holes, and two pegs of the same mold.

"At first light," Kael said. "Joren and Corren. Aloud. We go together."

"Ulla knows Renn's habits," Sol said. "She goes with us. It is her husband."

"She goes."

Sol released his wrist. "Take the lamp. The spare. Yelva keeps one by the front door."

"I will bring it."

He crossed to the front room. Yelva slept in her chair by the cold hearth, a ledger open on her lap. She woke the way old healers do, all at once and already scolding, her eyes on his face before they were on anything else.

"She spoke," Yelva said. It was not a question.

"Two words."

"Then she has more in her. Do not ask for them tonight." She pulled the ledger higher on her knees.

"How long, Yelva?"

"I told you the elbows within the month, and asking again will not move them. She is stubborn. Stubborn bodies keep their own counsel longer than a healer expects." She said it without softness. It was not comfort, and she had not meant it as comfort. It was a measurement, and Kael had grown to prefer those. "The lamp is on the peg. Do not let it smoke."

The spare lamp hung by the door, its oil full, its wick fresh. Kael took it down. The brass was cool in his palm. He checked the flame, adjusted the wick, secured the catch.

At the threshold he looked back. Sol had already turned to Mara, her hand on the bandaged wrist, her lips moving as she counted.

He had resolved, in the quiet of the night watch, to keep her out of the narrow places. The third gallery. Whatever waited beyond the door. He had given her half of it aloud, and the other half he kept, and it sat in his chest alongside the wish and the arithmetic.

He did not say the rest now.

The night air hit him at the door, cold, smelling of iron and salt and the slow rot of hides. Joren fell in beside him on the step and said nothing for the length of three breaths.

"You did not wake her to say it," Joren said.

"No."

"She knew anyway. That one always does." He tipped his chin toward the dark of the lane. "Ulla has been at the tar-shop door since the message went. She has not sat down."

"Then she will want to go first."

"She will want to go alone."

"She will not," Kael said. "Tell her that, and tell her I said it aloud."

Behind them the lamp in the healer's back room burned low, and a woman counted another woman's pulse, and Kael went out to wait for the light.
