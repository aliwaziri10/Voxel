The lamp in Yelva's back room burned low, its wick trimmed to a nub that threw more shadow than light. Mara lay on the narrow cot against the far wall, her breathing shallow, the grey wool blanket pulled to her chin. Her wrists were wrapped, the bandages clean, the cold settled now in her forearms. Yelva had said it would reach her elbows by Day Twenty-Seven. Today was Day Eight.

A clay pot sat on the stool beside Kael, the tea inside gone cold hours ago. Yelva's blend — bitter root, pine needle, something that tasted of old bark. He had not drunk it. Sol had taken three sips and set it down, grimacing.

"She scolds the dead and the living in the same voice," Sol whispered, her head tilted back against the wall, eyes half closed. "And she prefers the dead. They do not argue back."

Kael's mouth twitched. "Mara has been listening for twenty years. She probably agrees."

"She would tell you to drink the tea."

"She would tell me to sit still."

Sol made a small sound, not quite a laugh. Her undyed coat was rumpled at the shoulder where she had leaned into him. The wool smelled of mineral water and the tar shop's pine resin, underneath it the faint salt of her skin. Her hand rested on her knee, fingers curled loose.

"Yelva said the soup Ansa sent was thin," Sol murmured. "She said Ansa measures onions by the shadow they cast."

"Ansa measures everything by the shadow it casts. She has forty years of tar to prove it."

"She also said the stranger's chain was good iron. Coiled like a dead snake, she said. Tam repeated it. He likes her turns of phrase."

"Tam repeats everything Ansa says. He has since he was tall enough to reach the workbench."

The passage beyond the door was dark. Joren sat on the bench out there, his scarred hand resting on his knee, the older warden's silhouette a cutout against the faint glow from the healer's front room. Corren had taken the terrace path, stone five, the message already sent. Joren and Corren. Always Joren and Corren.

Sol's chin dropped toward her chest. Her eyes fluttered.

"You are awake," Kael said, quiet.

"Am." The word slurred. "Watching. The tea. The pot. The —"

Her head tipped sideways. The crown of it came to rest against his shoulder, the weight of it sudden and warm. Her breath slowed, evening out against the wool of his coat. Her fingers, loose on her knee, slid downward, found the opening of his sleeve, and slipped inside. The tips of them brushed the skin of his forearm, cold at first, then warming.

Kael did not move.

His left leg had gone numb to the hip. The familiar hollow spread from the knee upward, the absence of sensation creeping along bone and tendon. He had sat through worse. He had walked through worse. He did not shift. He did not adjust. He let the numbness settle and kept his shoulder still beneath her head.

He counted her breaths. In. Out. In. Out. Seven. Eight. Nine. The rhythm was slower than his own, deeper. The line's pulse — the east-break stones, the metering chamber, the blue point steady at the window — usually ran beneath his counting like a second heartbeat. Tonight it did not. He realized he had stopped counting the line somewhere around breath four. He was only counting her.

The thought arrived unbidden: thirty years. Thirty winters. Thirty springs. He saw them in fragments — a garden he did not have, a table with two chairs, the grey of her hair deepening, the lines at his own eyes multiplying. He saw himself old and she old beside him, the arithmetic balanced at last.

He stopped the thought. He did not know whether he had thirty years. He did not know whether he had thirty days. The custodian's order had fourteen remaining. The pick was measuring the east approach. The pegs waited in his coat pocket and in Sol's pack.

And then he heard her voice in the dark of the tar shop, stripped of humour: *I want you to have them.*

The wish. Not an offer. A wish.

His throat tightened. He wanted to ask her something. He did not know the words. He did not know whether he had the right. He thought of the next thirty years and then of her saying those five words, and the two collided in his chest like flint and steel.

Joren shifted on the bench in the passage. Boots on stone, slow, deliberate. The door pushed open a hand's width. The older warden's face appeared in the gap, scarred hand faintly visible. He looked at them. He said nothing. He withdrew, and a moment later a folded blanket landed across their knees, wool rough and smelling of lanolin and old sweat.

Kael did not look up. He did not thank him. He only adjusted his grip on his stick, the wood solid beneath his right hand, and let the blanket's weight settle.

He let himself picture the long road with her, once. He did not pretend he had not.

Mara's eyes opened.

The sound she made was not a word at first — a breath drawn sharp, the blanket rasping against her chin. Then two syllables, clear as a bell struck once.

"Renn. Down."

Her voice was thin, stripped of the rasp that had clung to it since the fourth of Cinderveil. Her gaze fixed on the ceiling beams, the shadows moving across them. Her bandaged wrists twitched beneath the wool.

Kael was on his feet before the silence returned. The numb leg screamed, pins and needles flooding the dead space, but he moved. The blanket slid to the floor. Sol stirred, her hand pulling free of his sleeve, fingers catching on the wool before releasing.

"Mara." Kael's voice came out rough. He crouched beside the cot. "Mara, hear me. Renn is found. He is alive. He is with Ulla."

Her eyes did not move. The pupils were dilated, the irises pale in the lamplight. Her lips moved again, slower this time.

"Down. The stair. He goes down."

"Renn is not on the stair. He is at Ulla's. He has been there since we found him."

"Down." The word was final. Her eyes closed. Her breathing returned to its shallow rhythm. The cold in her forearms did not show on her face.

Kael stayed crouched. The pins and needles faded into a dull ache. Sol's hand found his wrist, her touch light, questioning.

"She has said it before," Sol said, very quiet. "Day Four. Day Six. Always the same two words. Renn. Down."

"She was there," Kael said. "Fourth of Cinderveil. She ran in after me. The line took her. She saw where he went."

"Or she knows where he goes now."

Kael's jaw tightened. The third gallery. The sealed door. Renn's chalk on the stair riser: *THIS ONE HAS NO BOTTOM I HAVE FOUND*. The warm cellar marked on Renn's sheet, the folded survey Ulla had given her. The stranger's chain measuring the east approach from the boiling house cellar mouth to the cliff edge, matching the survey.

The second stair descended behind the enamelled iron door in the boiling house cellar. That was the path they had planned. The path the pick would expect. But if Renn had gone down the third gallery — if the warm cellar connected to the third gallery — then the pick's measurements meant something else entirely.

"I need to see the third gallery," Kael said. "Tonight. Through the seal."

Sol's grip on his wrist tightened. "The third gallery is sealed. The Council sealed it forty years ago. The custodian's order —"

"Does not name the third gallery. It names the plate. The pegs. The rope rig. The pick measures the east approach. The chain matches Renn's sheet. The warm cellar. The second stair. But Renn's chalk said the second stair has no bottom. He wrote it on the riser. He knew. He went somewhere else."

"Kael." Her voice sharpened. "The third gallery is narrow. The air is bad. Renn's level hand — you saw his chalk. He was not well when he wrote it. He may have been confused."

"He was a surveyor. He does not confuse a stair with a gallery. He wrote what he found. The third gallery has a door. Corren said —"

The door to the passage opened wider. Corren stepped in, his scarred hand loose at his side. Joren followed, the blanket folded over his arm.

"The sealed third gallery," Corren said, his voice pitched low for the room. "The door at the end of the east approach. It has two peg-holes. The size of Renn's tally pegs."

Kael's hand went to his coat pocket. The ledge peg, cold brass against his fingertips through the wool. The table peg rode in Sol's pack, upstairs in the healer's front room.

"The twin pegs," Corren continued. "They fit the holes. Renn's tally pegs were the pattern. The same mold. The works steward placed the second."

Joren set the folded blanket on the bench by the door. "The pick knows the pegs. The pick knows the holes. The pick measured the east approach in paces that match the survey. The warm cellar. The second stair. The third gallery door. The peg-holes."

Kael rose, the numb leg taking his weight without buckling. The ache settled into something he could walk on. "The pick is scouting the close. Not the second stair. The third gallery. The pegs pull from outside the room. The rope rig runs from the peg-holes up the gallery, out the approach, to the pullers waiting beyond the room's reach."

"Odo and Tessa," Sol said. She had risen beside him, her undyed coat settling around her. "They are the pullers. They stand outside. They pull in one breath. The pegs come free. The line closes."

"Who sits at the plate?" Corren asked. "The pick stands at the plate. The pick is the nearest. The pick takes the years. Unless another body stands closer."

Kael looked at Sol. Her grey eyes held his. The lamp flame flickered, casting her face in gold and shadow. The wish sat between them, acknowledged, recorded, not undone.

"I will sit at the plate," she said. "On Day Twenty-Nine. I will be the nearest."

"The rule does not choose," Joren said. "The rule measures distance. The room is small. The plate at the foot of the stair. The pegs in the ledge and on the table. The rope rig from the pegs up the stair, through the iron door, out the cellar mouth."

"Or up the third gallery," Kael said. "If the plate is in the chamber behind the third gallery door. If the warm cellar connects there. The pick measured the east approach to the cliff edge. The chain marked the spots. The notebook recorded the paces. The geometry of a rope rig that needs two pullers beyond the room's reach."

Corren's scarred hand clenched. "The custodian's pick is a ward-taker of the Council's roll. One ward-taker. The order names only the role. The pick measures. The pick places. The pick pulls. The pick waits for Day Twenty-Nine."

"Fourteen days," Tessa's voice came from the front room, carrying through the passage. "Fourteen days to learn the room. Fourteen days to learn the pegs. Fourteen days to learn the rope rig."

Kael's thumb brushed the heel of Sol's palm where her hand still circled his wrist. The contact was small. It held everything.

"I am going to the third gallery," he said. "Tonight. Through the seal. I need to see the door. The peg-holes. The chamber beyond. The plate."

Sol did not argue. She did not tell him to wait, to plan, to bring Joren and Corren. She knew him better than that. She knew the narrow places called to him, the way the line called to her. She knew he would go whether she agreed or not.

"Take the lamp," she said. "The one from the front room. Yelva has a spare."

"I will bring it."

"And Kael."

He met her eyes. The grey flecks caught the lamplight.

"Stand back," she said. "Before you argue. Before you think. You go."

The words echoed. The sill above the dark Reach. The blue point burning. Her hand on his sleeve, checking for wear. *If I ask, I need you to go before you argue.*

He had said yes then. He had argued in his head. She had said that counted.

He did not argue now. He did not think. He nodded, once, and turned toward the passage.

Joren fell in beside him without a word. Corren took the rear. The front room of the healer's shop was quiet, Yelva asleep in her chair by the cold hearth, a ledger open on her lap. The spare lamp hung on a peg by the door, its oil full, its wick fresh.

Kael took it down. The brass was cool in his palm. He checked the flame, adjusted the wick, secured the catch. His movements were automatic, the ritual of forty years' service — no, twenty. Mara's twenty. His own shorter count.

The night air hit them at the door. Cold. Smelling of iron and salt and the slow rot of hides. The terrace path wound downward, ward-stones looming like dead sentinels. The boiling house vents exhaled grey plumes. The east-break stones waited ahead, eleven of them in a ragged line where the Kindling's pulse met the cliff.

Kael walked a half pace ahead of Joren. His stick tapped the stone, steady. His left leg held. The numbness had retreated to a dull throb below the knee. The lamp threw a circle of light ahead, the shadows of the ward-stones stretching long and thin.

The east approach. The boiling house cellar mouth. The sealed third gallery door at the end of it, two peg-holes waiting for brass that already rested in his pocket and Sol's pack.

Fourteen days.

He did not look back. He did not need to. He knew she was watching. He knew she would stay with Mara. He knew she would be there when he returned, or when he did not.

The path curved toward the boiling house. The cellar mouth yawned in the cliff face, the enamelled iron door a darker rectangle within. The knot above it caught the lamplight, twisted iron warm like skin.

He had been here five nights ago. The iron door warm under his palm. The second stair descending behind it. The plate at the foot of the stair, black and waiting.

He had not gone down the third gallery. He had not known it existed.

Now he did.

The lamp threw their shadows long against the ward-stones, the grey plumes from the vents swallowing the light at the edges.

The east approach was a tunnel carved through the cliff base, the floor worn smooth by forty years of boots and barrows and rope rigs. The walls wept mineral water. The air tasted of iron and old stone and something deeper, a slow rot that lived in the dark places where the Kindling's heat never reached.

Two hundred paces. Kael counted them. The survey table in his head, Renn's sheet, the warm cellar marked on the east. The stranger's chain had measured the same. Chalk marks on the bench at the tar shop, numbers and arrows, the geometry of a rope rig.

The third gallery door appeared ahead, a rectangle of blackness in the tunnel wall. No hinges visible. No handle. Two round holes bored through the iron at waist height, spaced a hand's width apart. Brass rings set into the holes, oxidized to dull green.

Kael stopped. The lamp light caught the rings. He set the lamp on a ledge in the tunnel wall, the flame steady behind glass.

"Renn's tally pegs," Corren said from behind him. "The holes match. Same diameter. Same spacing."

Kael reached into his coat pocket. His fingers closed on the ledge peg, cold brass, the edges worn smooth by the ledge below the Tar Lane forge. He withdrew it. Held it up to the left hole. The fit was exact. The peg slid in to the collar, seated with a click that echoed in the tunnel.

He pulled it free. Reached for the right hole. The peg seated the same way.

"Same mold," Joren said. His voice was flat. "The works steward placed the second. On the table in the chamber behind the iron door. B2 ch.32."

"B2 ch.36-38," Corren corrected. "The surveyor's tally pegs were the pattern. Renn's ten. The works steward cast two more from the same mold. One for the ledge. One for the table."

Kael returned the peg to his pocket. His palm tingled where the brass had rested. The lamp flame threw the door's surface into relief — pitted iron, rivet heads, the two holes clean as fresh drill work.

"The rope rig runs from these holes," Kael said. "Up the gallery. Out the approach. To the pullers waiting beyond the room's reach."

"Odo and Tessa," Joren said. "They have the rig coiled at the metering chamber. They have walked the distance. They know the breath count."

"One breath," Corren said. "Both pegs pulled in one breath. The line closes. The stored sum goes to the nearest living body."

Kael pressed his palm to the door. The iron was cold. Not warm like the enamelled knot above the cellar door. Not warm like the plate at the foot of the second stair. Cold, dead iron, sealed forty years by the Council's order.

"The seal," he said. "How does it open?"

"No lock," Joren said. "No keyhole. The Council fused the hinges. Poured lead into the bolt channels. The only way through is the peg-holes. The rope rig pulls the door free from its frame. The whole door. The pegs are the only purchase."

Kael's thumb traced the edge of the left hole. The brass ring was scored, the inner surface grooved by rope friction. Years of pulls. Or one pull, repeated in practice.

"The pick measured the east approach," he said. "Paces from the cellar mouth to the cliff edge. The chain marked the spots. The notebook recorded the geometry. The pick knows the distance from the peg-holes to the pullers. The pick knows the breath count."

"Fourteen days to learn it," Joren said. "Fourteen days to walk it. Fourteen days to time it."

"Fourteen days to decide who sits at the plate," Corren added.

Kael did not turn. The lamp light caught the grey at Joren's temples, the scar across Corren's hand, the tunnel stretching back toward the terrace path. He thought of Sol in the healer's back room, her hand on Mara's wrist, her grey eyes fixed on the bandaged forearms. He thought of the wish spoken on the sill above the dark Reach. *I want you to have them.*

He had resolved, in the quiet of the tar shop, to keep her out of the narrow places. The third gallery. The chamber beyond the door. The plate at the foot of whatever stair waited there. He would go. He would see. He would measure. He would rig. He would make sure the nearest living body was the one they chose — him, if it came to it. He would not let her be the nearest. He would not let the pick bring a stranger to die at the plate.

He had not acted on the resolution. He had not spoken it. He had only let it settle in his chest alongside the wish and the arithmetic and the fourteen days.

The door would not open for looking. The peg-holes were for pulling. The rope rig was for closing. The plate was for burning.

"Corren," Kael said. "The survey. Renn's sheet. The warm cellar marked on the east. Does it show the third gallery?"

Corren shifted. "Renn's sheet shows the east approach. The boiling house cellar. The second stair. The plate. A thin dotted line from the anchor's south edge runs east toward the cliff and the Lower Reach and off the paper. The third gallery is not on the sheet. The Council sealed it before Renn surveyed. The works steward knew. The works steward placed the second peg on the table in the chamber behind the iron door. The works steward taught the boy who forged the witness line. The works steward taught Renn."

"The works steward carried the lines for forty years," Joren said. "The carried hand on the leaf. The second hand under the word carried. The works steward's hand leans right. The forged witness hand is upright with a closed tail. Practised. Careful to look younger than it is."

"Ansa said the stranger's handwriting matched," Corren said. "Upright letters. Closed descenders. The hand that taught Renn. The works steward's hand."

Kael's jaw tightened. The works steward. Small, upright, past sixty, grey hair pinned flat, copper clasp of three knotted strands. The carrying hand. She had locked Renn in the third gallery to keep him alive and away from the pegs. She had not known the line would draw on him.

She had written Aldous's name on the witness line herself. She had wanted to be found. Not by the Council. Not by Thorne. By someone who would compare hands and ask who taught him.

The boy who taught him his letters. Farrow's postscript. *Ask the boy who taught him his letters.*

Aldous. The works steward had taught Aldous his letters. The works steward had written Aldous's name on the witness line. The works steward had locked Renn in the third gallery.

The third gallery door stood before them. Two peg-holes. A rope rig waiting. A plate somewhere beyond.

"Can we open it?" Kael asked. "Without the rope rig. Without the pull. Just to see."

Joren shook his head. "The hinges are fused. The bolts are leaded. The door is the seal. The only way through is the pull."

"Then we rig it," Kael said. "We rig it now. We walk the distance. We time the breath. We see the chamber. We see the plate."

"Tonight?" Corren asked. "The pick has fourteen days. We have tonight."

"We have tonight," Kael agreed. "Odo and Tessa are at the metering chamber. They have the rig. They have the rope. They know the distance. Send word. Bring them here."

Joren was already moving, turning back toward the tunnel mouth, his stick tapping faster now. "I will go. Corren stays with you."

"Corren goes," Kael said. "Joren stays. The pick may return. The pick may watch. Joren knows the pick's face. Corren knows the rig."

Joren paused. His scarred hand clenched on his stick. "The pick is a ward-taker of the Council's roll. One ward-taker. The order names only the role. Joren and Corren. We do not go alone."

"Corren goes," Kael repeated. "Joren stays. That is the order."

The older warden held his gaze. The lamp light caught the line of his jaw, the set of his mouth. He had served twenty years. He had watched Mara's cold climb from wrists to forearms. He had watched the custodian's order arrive sixteen days late. He had watched the stranger measure the east approach with a chain that matched Renn's sheet.

"Corren goes," Joren said. "I stay."

Corren moved past them, his boots silent on the worn stone. He disappeared into the tunnel darkness toward the terrace path.

Kael turned back to the door. The two peg-holes stared back, brass rings dull in the lamp light. He set the ledge peg into the left hole again. Seated it. Pulled it free. Set it into the right. Seated it. Pulled it free.

The table peg was in Sol's pack, upstairs in the healer's front room. He would need it. He would need both pegs in the holes, the rope rig attached, the pullers positioned, the breath counted.

He would need to see the chamber beyond. The plate. The stair. The distance from the plate to the door. The distance from the door to the pullers.

He pressed his forehead against the cold iron. The mineral water dripped somewhere in the dark. The Kindling's pulse thrummed in the rock, a slow heartbeat felt through bone.

Fourteen days.

He thought of Sol's fingers slipping into his sleeve in sleep. The cold tips warming against his forearm. The weight of her head on his shoulder. The blanket Joren had laid across their knees, wool rough and smelling of lanolin and old sweat.

He thought of the wish. *I want you to have them.*

He thought of the arithmetic. Thirty-three each. The stored seventeen. The sixteen and a half she had left. The thirty-three he had left. The close on Day Forty-Three. The years transferring to the nearest living body.

He would be the nearest. He would make himself the nearest. He would stand at the plate and let the years take him. He would grow old with her, thirty-three years each, the arithmetic balanced at last.

He would keep her out of the narrow places. He would not speak the resolution. He would only act on it when the moment came.

Footsteps on stone. Corren returning, Odo and Tessa behind him, the rope rig coiled over Odo's shoulder, Tessa's chalk line clicking at her belt.

The rope rig was simple. Two lengths of hemp, each knotted to a brass toggle that seated in the peg-holes. The toggles were new, turned to match the pegs, the knots dressed and seized. Odo had made them in the six days since the first stone. Tessa had checked every whipping, every splice, every measurement.

The pullers stood at the tunnel mouth, twenty paces back from the door. Beyond the room's reach. The rule measured distance. The room was the chamber beyond the door. The plate was at the foot of the stair in that chamber. The nearest living body to the plate would pay — or receive.

Kael stood at the door. Joren stood beside him. Corren held the lamp at Kael's shoulder.

The toggles seated. The rope ran back through the tunnel, straight and taut, no slack. Odo and Tessa braced themselves at the far end, feet planted, hands locked on the hemp.

"One breath," Odo called. His voice carried down the tunnel. "We pull on your signal. One breath. Both lines together."

Kael nodded. He did not look back. He did not need to. He knew the signal. He knew the breath. He knew the distance.

He pulled the ledge peg from his pocket. Seated it in the left toggle. The click was sharp. He reached for the table peg — but his pocket was empty.

"Sol has the table peg," he said. "In her pack. At the healer's."

"Then we wait," Tessa called from the tunnel mouth. "The rig needs both pegs. Both toggles. One breath. Both lines."

Kael's jaw tightened. Fourteen days. The pick had measured the east approach. The pick knew the peg-holes. The pick knew the rope rig. The pick waited for Day Twenty-Nine.

He could send Corren back. He could wait for Sol. He could plan. He could measure. He could time the breath.

"The gallery runs parallel to the approach," Joren said. "Renn's chalk on the stair riser. *THIS ONE HAS NO BOTTOM I HAVE FOUND*. The second stair has no bottom. The third gallery connects to the warm cellar. The warm cellar connects to the chamber. The plate is in the chamber."

"Renn did not say. He wrote what he found. The chalk on the riser. The level hand with closed descenders. He was not well. He may not have measured."

"Then I measure."

Kael turned from the door. The lamp light caught the tunnel walls, the ward-stones, the grey plumes from the vents. He walked back the way they had come, Joren at his side, the stick tapping steady.

The boiling house cellar mouth. The enamelled iron door. The knot above it, warm like skin. The second stair descending behind it, the plate at the foot, black and waiting.

Two years total. Twelve heartbeats per year. Six heartbeats per half year. Twenty-four heartbeats of her life, given to the Kindling, paid by her because nobody had been within the room's reach.

He would not let her pay the seventeenth. He would not let the pick bring a stranger to pay it. He would stand at the plate on Day Twenty-Nine. He would be the nearest. The stored seventeen would come to him. Thirty-three each. The arithmetic balanced.

Renn had written on the riser. The level hand. The closed descenders. He had gone down. He had found no bottom. He had come back up. He had written the warning. He had been locked in.

The works steward had locked him in. To keep him alive. To keep him away from the pegs. Not knowing the line would draw on him.

Mara's cold. The wrists on the fourth. The forearms by Day Eight. The elbows by Day Twenty-Seven. The line drawing on her as the first body it took. The draw ending when the line closed.

The line closed when the pegs pulled. The pegs pulled when the rope rig worked. The rope rig worked when the pullers pulled in one breath.

The pullers were Odo and Tessa. The pegs were the ledge peg and the table peg. The table peg was in Sol's pack at the healer's.

Kael turned back toward the gallery. The lamp light caught Joren's face, the older warden's eyes fixed on the plate, the warm stair, the empty niches.

"The pick knows," Kael said. "The pick measured. The pick placed. The pick waits."

"Fourteen days," Joren said. "Fourteen days to learn the room. Fourteen days to learn the pegs. Fourteen days to learn the rope rig."

Kael nodded. He did not speak the resolution. He did not speak the wish. He only turned and walked back up the gallery, the stick tapping steady, the numb leg holding, the lamp light throwing their shadows long against the narrowing walls.

Fourteen days.

He would learn the room. He would learn the pegs. He would learn the rope rig. He would make sure the nearest living body was the one they chose.

He would keep her out of the narrow places.

He would not pretend he had not.
