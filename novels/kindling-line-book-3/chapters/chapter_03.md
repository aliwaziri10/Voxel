The evening air over the tar-shop carried pine pitch and woodsmoke and the salt tang that rose from the Lower Reach at tide-turn. Ansa's room was warm, the hearth fed with slow-burning cordwood, the kettle singing behind the iron grill. Bowls steamed on the scarred table. Crust cracked. Dust from the morning's baking settled in the grooves of the wood.

Sol had come up the stair behind Kael with the long day still sitting in her shoulders. The talking at Thorne House, the careful faces, the questions that had to be asked in the right order: she had carried all of it through the lane like a stone in a coat pocket, and she was glad of any room that asked nothing of her but a spoon.

The room over the tar-shop was the same one the lane had given them, and it had begun to look as if it had always been theirs. His coat hung on the peg by the door. Her boots stood under the bench with the lane's mud drying pale on the toes. The stick leaned against the wall where his hand would find it without his looking, and the loaf Ansa had left under its cloth that morning had been cut and set out on a board, with a crock of rendered fat beside it and a dish of pickled roots from the cellar. Down in the lane the lamps were being lit one by one, and the sound of the tide turning came up through the floor of the Lower Reach like a slow breath let out.

Ansa ladled broth without looking up. "Two names on a petition and the Lord Ashworth still carries a stick like a scepter. Half the lane has asked me whether it is for show."

Tam sat across from Sol, his spoon moving steady. He did not look up. "The other half has asked whether it hurts."

"I told them it hurts less than the asking," Ansa said. She tore the heel off the loaf and set it in front of Kael without being asked. "Eat that. A man who carries a stick ought to carry bread behind it."

"The lane has opinions about the bread too," said Tam, to his bowl.

"The lane has opinions about everything. The lane had opinions about the tide last spring, and the tide did not listen either."

Sol watched Kael's jaw work. He swallowed the broth, set the spoon down, and did not answer. The stick leaned against the wall beside his chair, the ferrule angled forward, honest now. He had carried it openly since the Thorne vestibule. She had made him.

He had not argued then, either. That was what she was learning to watch for: how often Kael did not argue, and how much it cost him each time, and how much of the cost he thought she could not see.

Ansa snorted. "Ward-girl. You are letting the broth go cold."

Sol's fingers tightened on her spoon. "Eat."

Ansa met her eyes, grey and sharp as flint. "I am eating."

The word hung between them, a small victory. Sol looked at Kael. He was watching Ansa, his face calm, the needles in his hip buried behind the quiet mask he wore for strangers. He took the teasing without defending himself. She found it harder to watch than to bear the line's weight herself.

It was not that the teasing was cruel. It was the opposite. Ansa teased the way a person pats a horse's neck, to tell it that it is among friends, and Kael bore it like a man still learning the rules of a house that was not his own.

Two names on a petition. Isolde Vane and Kael Ashworth, his square hand and her initials under it, and now the whole lane could say them over its soup. She had not known it would feel like being seen through a window she had forgotten was glass.

Tam rose, his bowl empty. He reached for Kael's stack of dishes. "I will take those."

Kael half-rose with him, the stick clattering against the chair leg as he shifted. "I can carry them."

Sol's hand closed on his wrist. The wool of his sleeve was rough under her fingers. "Sit."

He stopped. The fight left his shoulders in a single breath. He settled back into the chair, the stick sliding from his lap to the flagstones with a wooden thunk.

Tam picked it up without a word. He balanced the ferrule in his palm, tested the weight, and set it back against the chair leg, handle forward, ferrule down. No remark. The kindness of it sat in the room heavier than any speech.

"Thank you," Kael said.

"It is a stick," said Tam, and went to rinse the bowls.

Under the table, Sol pressed her foot against Kael's good leg. He did not look down. She did not look at him. The contact was a rope between them, taut and silent.

It was a small thing. The side of her boot against the side of his, the long bone under the wool. He shifted his weight a little so that she would not have to reach, and went on eating, and neither of them said what the shift had been for.

Ansa spooned the last of the broth. "The awning came down the morning after the vote. Wages paid to the day. Wenna Varel sent bread. Varel is home."

The names fell into the steam. Sol filed them. Wenna Varel. Bread. Home.

Kael's shoulders came down a notch. "Varel is home," he said quietly, as if setting it on a shelf. "Good."

"Good," Ansa agreed, "and I am not telling you what Wenna Varel said about the Council's bread, because you are eating."

Sol would put it in the ledger tonight, beside the burns and the days, because it deserved a line.

The door latch lifted. Cold air cut the warmth.

Joren stood in the doorway, his scarred hand on the frame. Behind him, a woman Sol did not know, dark hair pinned back, healer's apron stained at the hem, and Corren, the younger warden, his eyes scanning the room.

"Joren and Corren," Kael said. His voice was flat. "Every time."

Joren, who had heard it more than once since morning, let the corner of his mouth move and nothing else. The scar across the back of his hand went white against the doorframe.

Corren nodded once. "Scouts returned from the coast. The skiff lead is cold. No sign of the surveyor's trace south of the cliff."

"We walked the shingle and the boat sheds both ways," he went on. "Nobody has sold a skiff, lent one, or lost one. Nobody has seen a lean man with chalk on his hands."

The woman stepped forward. Her hands were clean, the nails trimmed short. "Ulla. Renn's wife."

She stood very straight, the way a person does who has spent days teaching herself to stand that way. There was ink on her cuff that was not hers, a thumbprint of it, grey and old, as if she had been handling his papers and had not yet been able to wash her hands of them.

Sol set her bowl down. The broth cooled on the table. "You have a thing of his."

Ulla reached into the pouch at her belt and drew out a folded sheet, the paper worn soft at the creases. She held it out. "He left this with me before the fourth of Cinderveil. Said if he did not come back, a person who knows how to count should have it. A person who knows how to wait."

Sol had been the first all her life and was only now learning to be the second.

Sol took it. The paper was warm from her palm. She did not unfold it yet.

"Renn's last chalk," Ulla said, "was on the second stair. He wrote: *This one has no bottom I have found.* He went down, not south."

Nobody at the table argued with it. The words had the flat weight of a thing already known and not yet admitted.

Corren's jaw tightened. "The third gallery lamp was dark when we checked. Ansa reported it."

Ansa grunted. "Dark as a closed eye. The ropewalk-gallery lamp. I told the Council. They logged it and forgot it."

"They log everything," Kael said. "It is how they forget it."

Kael's hand found Sol's under the table. His fingers were cold. She turned her hand to take his, and the chill went into her palm like a question. "The iron door in the boiling-house cellar."

Ulla's eyes met his. "Marked on the survey. One cellar, marked warm. The east approach."

Sol's thumb traced the folded edge. The sheet was a survey of the east. She knew before she opened it. The iron door in the boiling-house cellar, the enameled knot above it, the warm black plate at the foot of the second stair. All of it drawn in Renn's neat hand, one cellar marked with a single word.

Warm.

One word, in a hand she would have known anywhere, small and level, every descender closed. She did not read it aloud. Nobody at the table needed her to.

Ulla did not stay. She laid her hand flat on the table once, near Sol's bowl, as if leaving a thing there that could not be carried back down the stair, and said that if the sheet raised a question she would be at her own door and awake. Kael stood when she stood, stick or no stick, and kept standing until she had gone. Joren took her down himself. Corren lingered at the threshold, looking at the stick against the wall and then at Kael, and said only, "I will bring word the hour there is any." Then his boots followed theirs down the stair, and the door closed on the cold, and the warmth of the room came back slowly, the way it does after a draught.

The fear that had lived in her chest since the petition, the fear of the line, of the count, of the years slipping away, had gone quiet. In its place sat a heavier thing. She was not afraid of the line any more. She was afraid for Renn. A lean man with ink in the creases of his knuckles, who had gone down a stair and not come back up.

She tried the old reflex to see whether it still answered: in four, hold six, out eight, and the count behind it, the burn waiting in her like a coal under ash. The Kindling stirred, as it did now, with the pulse from the east. She waited for the dread that used to come with it, and it did not come. What came instead was the picture of a lean man at the head of a stair with a stub of chalk, and her own helpless wish to have been standing beside him.

Kael's voice dropped, pitched for her alone. "I am afraid for him. And I do not know how to be afraid beside another person without taking the fear off her."

Sol turned her hand over, palm up. His fingers curled into hers, the square palm warm against her skin. "Then do not take it. Hold your end."

He exhaled, a long slow breath. "I will try."

"Trying is all I have asked since the first day."

He almost smiled. "You asked for more than that on the first day. You asked me to stop pretending the stick was a walking habit."

"That was the second day."

"Then I have been counting wrongly."

"You have been counting everything wrongly for days," she said, "and I have let you, because the arithmetic keeps you from the thing under it."

He was quiet. "And the thing under it?"

"Renn," she said, and the name sat on the table between the bowls like a small cold coin.

She leaned her head against his shoulder. The wool smelled of woodsmoke and cold stone and him. He did not shift his arm. His collarbone was hard under her cheek and his pulse moved in it, slow, then slower, as he made himself breathe the way she breathed. She stayed where she was and let him do it. It had been a long time since she had leaned on anything without counting what it would cost the thing she leaned on. Tonight the line was quiet, and the sum came out at nothing, and she let it. The room held its breath around them. Ansa's bowl scraped, Tam's boots crossed the flags, the lane's last cart rattled by below. But the space between her shoulder and his was a country with no borders.

Ansa banked the fire without being asked, the way she had banked it for forty years, with the poker laid flat and the ash drawn over. "Tam. The coal." Tam rose, and they went out together with the bucket between them, and Ansa's last word from the stair was the one she always had. "Eat. There is more. There is always more."

The door pulled to behind them. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Down in the lane a door shut, and a woman's voice called a child in from the dark, and the ordinary evening went on without them, which Sol found she was grateful for. She did not lift her head, and Kael did not lower his arm, and the room went on being warm around two people who were, for the length of one cooling kettle, not asked for anything.

She would read it in the morning, she told herself, in daylight, with a steady hand. She knew she would not wait that long.

The sheet burned in her other hand, folded small, the word *warm* waiting to be read.
