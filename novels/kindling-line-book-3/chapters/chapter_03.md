The evening air over the tar-shop carried pine pitch and woodsmoke and the salt tang that rose from the Lower Reach at tide-turn. Ansa's room was warm, the hearth fed with slow-burning cordwood, the kettle singing behind the iron grill. Bowls steamed on the scarred table. Crust cracked. Dust from the morning's baking settled in the grooves of the wood.

Ansa ladled broth without looking up. "Two names on a petition and the Lord Ashworth still carries a stick like a sceptre. The works steward would call it theatre."

Kael's spoon paused halfway to his mouth. "The works steward is not here."

"She sees everything." Ansa wiped the rim of her own bowl with a thumb. "Tam says the copper clasp catches the light when she turns her head. Like a signal."

Tam sat across from Sol, his spoon moving steady. He did not look up. "She turned her head today. The light caught it. That is all."

Sol watched Kael's jaw work. He swallowed the broth, set the spoon down, and did not answer. The stick leaned against the wall beside his chair, the ferrule angled forward, honest now. He had carried it openly since the Thorne vestibule. She had made him.

Ansa snorted. "Ward-girl. You are letting the broth go cold."

Sol's fingers tightened on her spoon. "Eat."

Ansa met her eyes, grey and sharp as flint. "I am eating."

The word hung between them, a small victory. Sol looked at Kael. He was watching Ansa, his face calm, the needles in his hip buried behind the quiet mask he wore for strangers. He took the teasing without defending himself. She found it harder to watch than to bear the line's weight herself.

Tam rose, his bowl empty. He reached for Kael's stack of dishes. "I will take those."

Kael half-rose with him, the stick clattering against the chair leg as he shifted. "I can carry them."

Sol's hand closed on his wrist. The wool of his sleeve was rough under her fingers. "Sit."

He stopped. The fight left his shoulders in a single breath. He settled back into the chair, the stick sliding from his lap to the flagstones with a wooden thunk.

Tam picked it up without a word. He balanced the ferrule in his palm, tested the weight, and set it back against the chair leg, handle forward, ferrule down. No remark. The kindness of it sat in the room heavier than any speech.

Under the table, Sol pressed her foot against Kael's good leg. He did not look down. She did not look at him. The contact was a rope between them, taut and silent.

Ansa spooned the last of the broth. "The awning came down the morning after the vote. Wages paid to the day. Wenna Varel sent bread. Varel is home."

The names fell into the steam. Sol filed them. Wenna Varel. Bread. Home.

The door latch lifted. Cold air cut the warmth.

Corren stood in the doorway, his scarred hand visible on the frame. Behind him, a woman Sol did not know — dark hair pinned back, healer's apron stained at the hem — and a second warden, younger, his eyes scanning the room.

"Joren and Corren," Kael said. His voice was flat. "Every time."

Corren nodded once. "Scouts returned from the coast. The skiff lead is cold. No sign of the surveyor's trace south of the cliff."

The woman stepped forward. Her hands were clean, the nails trimmed short. "Ulla. Renn's wife."

Sol set her bowl down. The broth cooled on the table. "You have something of his."

Ulla reached into the pouch at her belt and drew out a folded sheet, the paper worn soft at the creases. She held it out. "He left this with me before the fourth of Cinderveil. Said if he did not come back, someone who knows how to count should have it. Someone who knows how to wait."

Sol took it. The paper was warm from her palm. She did not unfold it yet.

"Renn's last chalk," Ulla said, "was on the second stair. He wrote: *This one has no bottom I have found.* He went down, not south."

Corren's jaw tightened. "The third gallery lamp was dark when we checked. Ansa reported it."

Ansa grunted. "Dark as a closed eye. The ropewalk-gallery lamp. I told the Council. They logged it and forgot it."

Kael's hand found Sol's under the table. His fingers were cold. "The iron door in the boiling-house cellar."

Ulla's eyes met his. "Marked on the survey. One cellar, marked warm. The east approach."

Sol's thumb traced the folded edge. The sheet was a survey of the east. She knew before she opened it. The iron door in the boiling-house cellar, the enamelled knot above it, the warm black plate at the foot of the second stair. All of it drawn in Renn's neat hand, one cellar marked with a single word.

Warm.

The fear that had lived in her chest since the petition — the fear of the line, of the count, of the years slipping away — had gone quiet. In its place sat something heavier. She was not afraid of the line any more. She was afraid for Renn. Locked in the dark. The nearest living body. Three years taken while the works steward thought she was keeping him safe.

Kael's voice dropped, pitched for her alone. "I am afraid for him. And I do not know how to be afraid beside someone without taking the fear off her."

Sol turned her hand over, palm up. His fingers curled into hers, the square palm warm against her skin. "Then do not take it. Hold your end."

He exhaled, a long slow breath. "I will try."

"Trying is all I have asked since the first day."

She leaned her head against his shoulder. The wool smelled of woodsmoke and cold stone and him. He did not shift his arm. The room held its breath around them — Ansa's bowl scraping, Tam's boots on the flags, Corren's boots on the stair — but the space between her shoulder and his was a country with no borders.

The sheet burned in her other hand, folded small, the word *warm* waiting to be read.---

Sol stood in the doorway of Ansa's room, the folded survey pressed against her coat. The night air bit at her face, clean after the hearth's heat. Corren's scouts had melted into the shadows, their lanterns swallowed by the lane. Ulla had gone with them, her healer's bag clicking against her hip.

Kael stood beside her on the landing. The stick rested against the wall, forgotten for a moment. His hand still held the warmth of hers.

"Fourteen days," he said. "The custodian's order. Sixteen gone before we saw it."

"Sixteen days the Council sat on it," she said. "Sixteen days the works steward carried the secret alone. She knew the gauge read fourteen when Renn went down. She knew it read seventeen when he was found. Three years taken from the nearest living body while he was locked in the dark."

"She locked him there to keep him alive."

"She locked him there and the line drank him anyway." Sol's voice stayed level. "That is the rule. Nearest living body pays. The line does not care about intent."

Kael's left hip throbbed a steady beat. He adjusted his grip on the stick, the ferrule striking stone. "The works steward. Forty years. She wrote every second hand. She taught Aldous his letters. She wrote his name on the witness line. She locked Renn in the third gallery. She asked to be given to no one until the line closes."

"She is the carrying hand," Sol said. "The leaf showed it. The carried hand leans right. Her hand leans right. Renn's hand leans right because she taught him as a boy. The forged witness hand is upright, closed tail, practised to look younger. Three hands from one teacher."

"Four, if you count Aldous."

"Four." She did not turn. "The tutor taught sums. The works steward taught letters. Malrik knew the tutor. He paid the tutor. He did not know the works steward."

"Forty years in his house. His father's order. He never knew."

"She did not want him to know." Sol's shoulder blades shifted under the coat, a small movement, almost a shrug. "She said the hand would be safer if no one knew where it came from. Safer for the line. For the years. For the boy who would grow up and speak against his house."

"For Aldous."

"For Aldous." The word hung between them. "He spoke against his house in the Council chamber. He asked leave to speak. He gave the witness line that broke the Deed. He did it with the hand she taught him."

The lane opened onto the wider cobbles of the Lower Reach. The tar-shop roof showed ahead, the ladder to Ansa's rooms, the hatch where they had stood the night before and felt the second line answer a flare. The ropewalk lines hummed low, a sound felt in the bones more than heard.

Kael looked at the stair that led up to Ansa's door. The lamp bracket on the second landing was still empty. The pale ring on the flagstone had not moved.

"Odo and Tessa," he said. "The techs. They pull the pegs on Day 29. Two pullers beyond the room's reach. One breath. The twin brass pegs."

"Renn's map will show where they sit," Sol said. "Ulla has the map. Renn's wife. She gave me the folded survey of the east, one cellar marked warm. She came to the research wing tonight. Before the Council. She knew where to find me."

"She said Renn would want you to have it."

"She said he would want someone to use it. Someone who knows how to count. Someone who knows how to wait."

Kael felt the corner of his mouth twitch. "You know how to wait. You made me wait an hour last night to hear your count."

"I made you keep a promise."

"You made me count the bell strikes. Three strikes. Three hours." He shook his head. "Eighteen years. You said eighteen. I said thirty-four. Sixteen between us."

"Sixteen and a half now," she corrected. "You paid half a year on the roof. The flare I tested. Six heartbeats. The cost jumped the gap. You took it."

"I stood at the parapet. Within arm's reach. The rule is the rule."

"The rule is the rule," she agreed. "But you stepped back when I told you to. Three steps. Outside the room's reach. You said yes before you argued. That was the echo. Ch.1 you asked. Ch.8 you do it unasked. Ch.31 you forget in panic and step in. Ch.43 I ask aloud and you say yes."

Kael stopped on the cobbles. The stick planted itself. "You are counting the echoes already."

"I am counting everything." She turned then, facing him. The morning light caught her face, the grey eyes, the mouth that had smiled at him in the lamplight and said *Ask me again in an hour.* "The flare test. The early beat. The gauge at seventeen. The works steward's forty years. Aldous's hand. Malrik's not-knowing. The custodian's order. Fourteen days. The pegs. The plate. The close. The stored years going to the nearest living body."

"Sol at the plate. Odo and Tessa pulling. Me standing back."

"You standing back." Her hand rose, fingers brushing the wool of his coat sleeve, the place where the ink had smudged. "That is the promise. No silent counts. Aloud and with her. Whoever the hand belongs to."

The words were the ones he had written on the Chancellor's slip. *I will do what I did in the petition. Aloud and with her, whoever the hand belongs to.*

He covered her hand with his, the square palm warm against her fingers. The needles in his hip faded behind the contact. "Aloud and with you."

"Every time."

"Every time."

She let her hand drop. The moment stretched, the ropewalk hum filling the space. Then she turned for the tar-shop stair.

"Ansa's loaf," she said over her shoulder. "The stair is harder on an empty stomach."

Kael followed. The stick moved cleanly now, the ferrule finding each riser without catching. He climbed on his right side, the left leg swinging from the hip, the needles a familiar companion. He did not hide the stick. He carried it openly, the wood warm against his palm, the ferrule biting stone.

Behind them the Thorne house stood grey and silent, its windows dark, its portraits watching. Ahead the tar-shop waited, the kettles cold, the loaf under its cloth. Fourteen days. The count spoken. The answer sent.

He set his foot on the first tread of Ansa's stair. The wood creaked under his weight. Sol was already two steps up, moving light and sure.

The day had begun. The line breathed twelve breaths to a cycle somewhere under the cliff. The gauge read seventeen. The pegs waited in their ledges, twin brass, same mold, one taken from the Tar Lane forge and one found on a table behind an iron door.

Kael climbed, and did not look back.
