The morning air over the Lower Reach tasted of salt and wet iron. Mist clung to the cliff face in strips, torn by the wind that came up from the water. The path to the east approach was a shelf of grey stone, slick with spray, narrowed in places to a single boot width. Sol walked it with her hand on the rope at her belt, the coils settling against her hip.

Kael fell into step beside her. The stick tapped a steady rhythm on the stone, ferrule finding purchase where the moss was thickest. His left leg swung straight from the hip, the motion practised, the needles in the joint buried behind a face that gave nothing away. He wore the Ashworth grey, the violet cuffs folded back, the square hand loose at his side.

Joren and Corren brought up the rear. Their boots made no sound on the wet rock. Joren's scarred hand rested on the hilt of his short blade. Corren carried the coil of spare line, the hemp dark with brine.

The path turned a corner and the boiling house loomed above them, the stone bones of it grown into the cliff like a second skeleton. Steam rose from the vents in the lower wall, curling into the mist. The smell of sulphur and hot mineral water cut the salt.

Sol stopped at the iron ring bolted to the rock face, the anchor point for the safety line. She uncoiled her rope, the fibres rough against her palms, and tied a bowline around her own waist. The knot seated clean. She held the working end out to Kael.

He took it. His fingers were cold. He fumbled the standing part, twisted the bight wrong, and pulled a knot that would have slipped under a child's weight.

Sol caught the rope before it could tighten. "Again."

Kael blinked. The corner of his mouth twitched. "It holds."

"It holds until it doesn't. A man who ties a knot like that should not be allowed near a cliff."

He huffed a breath that might have been a laugh. "I have been near several."

"I know. That is what worries me."

She undid the mess, laid the rope straight, and guided his hands through the turns. The rabbit comes out of the hole, around the tree, back down the hole. He pulled it tight. The bowline bit clean.

"Again," she said.

He untied it. His fingers moved slower this time, the square tips finding the rhythm. The second knot seated. The third. She watched his face, the concentration that drew the line between his brows, the way his thumb tested the standing part before he called it done.

"Better," she said. She clipped the carabiner through his waist loop, then her own. The line between them hung slack, a hand's breadth of hemp.

Kael tugged it. A test pull, sharp and sudden.

Sol slapped his hand off the rope. The sound cracked in the damp air.

He stared at his fingers, then at her. "It is a test."

"Test it on someone who is not wearing it."

His mouth flattened. The humour drained. "The line is the only thing between us and the drop."

"The line is the only thing between us and each other. You pull it, you pull me. You do not test the knot. You trust it."

He held her gaze. The wind caught a strand of hair across her forehead. He did not reach to move it.

"Trust it," he said.

They moved on. The shelf narrowed. A wave slapped the cliff base, spray flying up to spatter their coats. Kael's stick slipped on a patch of green slime. His weight went sideways, the left leg folding, the stick skittering on stone.

Sol's arm shot out. Her hand caught his forearm, the wool and the muscle beneath, and she took his weight before his brain registered the fall. The rope between them snapped tight. The carabiner bit her waist loop. He swung toward her, arrested, suspended by the hemp and her grip.

Close. Close enough to see the grey flecks in his eyes, the faint scar through the left eyebrow, the pulse beating in the hollow of his throat. Close enough to feel the heat of him through the wet wool, the rapid rise of his chest. The rope hummed between them, a live thing. She felt his pulse through the line, a second heartbeat echoing her own.

His stick clattered down the slope, lost to the mist.

He did not reach for the rock. He did not push away. His free hand found her shoulder, fingers closing on the coat, and he held there, breathing her air.

Sol looked at his mouth. The lower lip, chapped at the centre. The memory of that mouth on hers, in the lamplight, in the dark. Want rose hot and sharp, a flare in the chest. She could close the distance. She could kiss him here on the wet stone with the wardens watching and the Kindling breathing under the cliff.

She decided not here.

His eyes tracked the decision across her face. The pupils dilated. The hand on her shoulder tightened.

"Later," she said.

The word hung between them, heavier than the rope.

"Hold me to it," he said.

"Later," she repeated. A promise. A private word. Theirs alone from now on.

She released his forearm. He found his feet, the left leg taking the weight clean. The needles in his hip did not show. He retrieved his stick from the slope below, climbing down with the ease of a man who had made the journey a hundred times.

Sol watched him climb back. The rope between them went slack again. Her fingers ached where his pulse had beaten against her palm.

The boiling house door yawned ahead, the iron bands rusted, the hinges silent. Odo Marl stood in the frame, broad shoulders filling the width, a brass-bound notebook in one hand. Tessa Rook crouched beside the threshold, chalk dust on her fingers, a slate balanced on her knee. She looked up as they approached, quick eyes taking the rope, the wet coats, the space between Sol and Kael that had narrowed and widened and narrowed again.

"Readings," Odo said. His voice was gravel in a tin pail. "Eleven. Then twelve."

Sol's breath caught. "Twelve."

"Two readings. One breath between. The second held." He flipped the notebook shut. "The line is still adding."

Tessa rose, wiping chalk on her trousers. "The cycle was eleven natural. Ten with the chain on. Nine and then eight if nothing is done. Now twelve. It jumped a breath overnight."

Kael joined them at the door. His stick planted firm. "The east pulse adds a breath to every cycle. That was the reading the night of the petition."

"The east pulse added one," Odo said. "The natural cycle was eleven. The survey chain brought it to ten. The tally says ten. Sol counts eleven. Now the door reads twelve. Two readings. One breath apart. The third will tell."

Sol stepped past them into the cellar. The air thickened, warm and wet, smelling of mineral salt and old iron. The iron door stood at the far end, the enamelled knot above it dull in the lamp light. Renn's folded sheet in her pocket marked this spot *warm*. The metal radiated heat through the damp air, a fever skin.

She pressed her palm flat against the plate set in the door. The black iron met her hand, warm like living flesh, the pulse of the Kindling thrumming up her arm in a rhythm she knew by heart. Eleven breaths. Twelve. The metal answered her Kindling, a call and response older than the Vane name.

Odo cleared his throat. "Third reading."

He lifted his timing device, the brass wheel clicking. The cellar held its breath.

Sol kept her hand on the plate. The iron warmed her palm. The Kindling in her chest answered, beat for beat, breath for breath. Twelve. The needle settled.

Odo snapped the device shut. "Twelve again."

Tessa wrote it on her slate. The chalk screeched.

Joren and Corren stood in the doorway, silhouettes against the mist. The rope between Sol and Kael hung loose, a hand's breadth of hemp. The word *later* pulsed in her blood, in time with the line's new rhythm.

---

The iron door did not move. The enamelled knot watched them with eyes of fired glass. Sol kept her palm flat on the plate, the warmth spreading up her wrist, into the bone. Twelve breaths to a cycle. The second line adding its voice to the first, a harmony that had not existed when Renn walked the east stair.

Odo Marl turned the brass wheel again. The clicks marked the breaths in the cellar's damp silence. One. Two. Three. The needle swung. Four. Five. Six. The rhythm held, steady as a heart at rest. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. The click came. Twelve. The needle held.

He did not speak. He wrote in the notebook, the pencil scratching. Tessa copied the figure onto her slate, the chalk dust rising.

Kael stood at Sol's shoulder. His stick rested against the wall. His left hip showed no sign of the needles. The rope between them lay coiled at their feet, the slack taken up by the distance of a single step. He did not touch her. He did not need to. The line hummed with the beat she felt in her palm, the beat he felt through the hemp.

"Twelve," Odo said. "Three readings. Eleven. Twelve. Twelve. The line has settled on the new count."

"Or it is still climbing," Tessa said. She blew chalk dust from the slate. "The jump from ten to twelve in one night. That is not a pulse addition. That is a change in the source."

Sol lifted her hand from the plate. The warmth lingered in her palm, a ghost heat. "Renn's chalk said the stair has no bottom. The line draws on the living. Mara's cold climbs. Now the cycle adds a breath. The source is feeding."

Odo closed his notebook. "The readings are what they are. Twelve breaths. The east approach adds one. The second line adds one. The natural cycle is ten. The tally says ten. Sol counts eleven. The door reads twelve. The Kindling does not lie."

"Kindling does not speak," Sol said. "It answers. The question is what we are asking."

She turned from the door. The cellar narrowed behind her, the second stair descending into dark. Renn's last chalk waited somewhere below: *This one has no bottom I have found.* The twin brass pegs rode with them, the ledge peg in her pack, the table peg in Kael's pocket. Same mold. Placed. Waiting.

Joren shifted in the doorway. "The path back will be slick. The tide turns in two hours."

Corren nodded. "The wardens will hold the perimeter. The techs will take the readings to the surveyors' commission."

Sol looked at Kael. The grey eyes held hers. The word *later* sat between them, a knot tied clean, a promise made on wet stone. The rope at their waists connected them, slack but present, a line that would go tight the moment one of them fell.

"We go down," she said. "Not today. Not tomorrow. But we go down. Roped, and aloud."

Kael's hand rose. His thumb brushed her cheekbone, the touch light as chalk dust. "Aloud and with you. Every time."

"Every time."

She turned for the door. The mist hit her face, cold and clean. The path waited, the shelf of stone, the drop, the wardens' silent boots. The line breathed twelve beats under the cliff. The pegs rode with them.

She walked, and the rope followed, and Kael came after.

The path demanded attention. Each boot placement a calculation. Wet stone, loose grit, the wind finding gaps in their coats. Sol moved with the rope's slack feeding through her fingers, a habit from the tar-shop's upper reaches where a tangled line meant a damaged net and a lost day.

Kael matched her pace without effort. The stick tapped, paused, tapped. His breathing stayed even, the left leg taking each riser without hesitation. The mist clung to his hair, beaded on the scar through his eyebrow. He did not look at her. He did not need to. The rope between them carried the tension of the word *later*, a vibration she felt in the bones of her wrist.

Joren and Corren followed at the distance wardens kept. Silent. Their presence a boundary, not an escort. The tide breathed against the cliff below, a low boom that vibrated through the soles of her boots.

The shelf widened. Ahead, the tar-shop's awning showed weathered canvas against the grey, the chimney smoking thin. Ansa's window showed a rectangle of warmer grey. Smoke curled from the hearth flue.

Sol's stomach tightened. Not hunger. The iron door's warmth lingered in her palm, a ghost heat that pulsed with each step. Twelve breaths. The second line adding its voice. Renn's chalk waiting in the dark: *This one has no bottom I have found.*

She flexed her fingers. The rope slid.

"Your hand," Kael said. Low. For her alone.

She glanced down. Her knuckles showed white against the hemp.

"Cold," she said.

"It is not cold."

"No. It is not."

He did not reach for the rope. He did not reach for her. The promise held: *I will hold the rope from where I stand.* The space between them kept its charge, the word *later* a live coal in her chest.

The tar-shop door stood open. Ansa stood in the frame, arms crossed, her sleeves rolled to the elbow, flour dried grey on her forearms. Tam sat at the table inside, a bowl steaming before him. He looked up as they entered, spoon paused.

"Readings," Ansa said. Not a greeting. A demand.

Sol unclipped the carabiner. The rope fell slack between them, coiled at her feet. She wound it slow, deliberate, the fibres rough against her palm. "Eleven. Twelve. Twelve. The line has settled on twelve breaths. Or it is still climbing."

Ansa's gaze moved to Kael. "The tally says ten."

"The tally says what the chain allowed," Kael said. "The chain is gone. The east pulse adds one. The second line adds one. The door reads twelve."

Ansa's jaw worked. She uncrossed her arms, reached for the cloth hanging by the door, wiped her hands though they were clean. "And Renn's stair?"

"Still has no bottom anyone has found," Sol said. "Mara's cold climbs one hour each night. Wrists on the fourth. Forearms by the eighth. Elbows by the twenty-seventh. The line pulls on the living. It took the warden when she ran in. It will take the next body that stands close enough."

Tam set his spoon down. The bowl steamed, untouched. "Twenty-seven days left on the Council's order."

"And the Council means to try the line on the twenty-ninth," Kael said.

Ansa looked at Sol. "You go down there, you go roped. Both of you."

"Aloud and with you," Kael said. "Every time."

"Every time," Sol said.

Ansa nodded once. "Eat. The stair is harder on an empty stomach."

The word hung in the air, the same word Sol had thrown at Ansa across this table two nights past. *Eat.* A command. A kindness. The only handfast the Lower Reach knew.

Sol moved to the table. The bench was warm from Tam's body. She sat. Kael took the place beside her, the stick leaning against the wall within reach. His left hip showed no sign of the needles. The rope lay coiled at her feet, the connection slack but unbroken.

Ansa set a bowl before her. Broth, dark with herbs, a chunk of dense bread beside it. Steam rose, carrying salt and thyme and the mineral tang that lived in every breath of the Lower Reach.

Sol broke the bread. The crust cracked. She dipped it in the broth, raised it to her mouth. The heat spread through her fingers, into the ghost warmth of the iron plate.

Kael ate beside her, steady, unhurried. His square hand moved spoon to mouth, bread to broth. The rhythm of a man who had learned to measure his strength in mouthfuls.

Tam watched them. His eyes moved from Sol's face to Kael's, to the rope coiled at their feet, to the stick against the wall. He said nothing. He picked up his spoon and ate.

The wind shifted. The awning flapped. Somewhere below, the Kindling breathed twelve beats to a cycle, a heart grown larger in the dark. The word *later* pulsed in her blood, a promise made on wet stone, a knot tied clean.

She ate. She counted the breaths between mouthfuls. Twelve. The iron door answered her Kindling, beat for beat, breath for breath.
