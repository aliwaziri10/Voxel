Afternoon light fell across the table in the room over the tar shop. Dust motes drifted in the shaft, settling on the leaf and on Renn's folded sheet beside it. The shutters were propped open. Ansa was out, her tar buckets empty by the door. The house held the quiet of a held breath.

Sol sat with her hands folded in her lap. The leaf's rows marched down the page in Corrin's neat hand, totals inked in a column at the right. Every total stood a year or two above its lines. The difference sat underneath, in a second hand, darker, the word *carried* written small beneath each. That hand leaned to the right.

She could not make herself read her mother's row.

The date at the left. The name Vane. The lines of figures. The total. The carried year beneath it.

Her throat closed.

Kael sat opposite, his stick leaned against the table leg. His left leg was stretched out under the board, the numbness still waking in it. He wore the Ashworth grey, the violet cuffs turned back. His square hand rested on the table, palm down, the scar on the back of it pale against the skin.

He waited. He did not reach for the page.

"May I?" he asked.

Sol looked up. The question caught her, the way it always did. He asked. He did not assume. He did not take.

She nodded. "Yes."

He pulled the leaf closer. His fingers did not tremble. He read in the voice he used for ledgers, for manifests, for the tallies that came off the cliff count — flat, precise, each figure given its due weight.

"Row dated the twenty-ninth. Vane and Ashworth. Lines sum to forty-three. Total reads forty-four. Carried: one."

He looked up. "The next row is yours. Third gallery. Lines sum to eighteen. Total reads nineteen. Carried: one."

He turned the page. "Mother's row. Vane. Lines sum to thirty-seven. Total reads thirty-eight. Carried: one."

He stopped. His finger rested beside the carried line. The darker ink. The lean to the right.

Sol said, "Say it as though she were a person."

Kael blinked. The corner of his mouth twitched. He cleared his throat.

"Isolde Vane," he said, the ledger voice gone, replaced by something stiffer, more formal. "Born — the date is not here. Died at forty-two. Her carried year —" He faltered. "Her carried year is one. The hand that wrote it leans to the right. It is not Corrin's hand. It is not the clerk's hand. It is —"

He stopped. His ears pinked.

Sol laughed. The sound surprised her. It had been two days. The cellar. The stair. The plate. The dark. The laughter came out rough, a little broken, but it was laughter.

Kael looked at her, pleased and slightly hurt. "I tried."

"You sounded like a herald announcing a turnip harvest."

"The turnip harvest is vital to the —"

"Stop."

He stopped. His mouth pressed flat. The hurt stayed in his eyes, small and honest.

Sol's fingers found the edge of the leaf. She did not pull it back. "I want her year back."

The words sat in the air between them. Plain. Unadorned.

Kael's hand turned on the table. Palm up. An offering. An invitation.

"I would bring it back if I could."

Sol's hand moved. She laid it flat over the page, over his hand, covering the carried line. The paper was cool. His skin was warm. The lean of the carried hand pointed toward her wrist.

"That is not yours to pay," she said. "And I need you to stop saying that as if it were."

His fingers curled slightly, the tips brushing the heel of her palm. "I am saying it because it is true."

"Then say it and leave it. Do not reach for the bill."

The silence stretched. The tar shop below them creaked, a beam settling in the heat. Somewhere a gull cried, carried up from the water.

Sol leaned forward. Her forehead came to rest against his forearm, just above the wrist. The wool of his sleeve was rough against her skin. She breathed. In. Out. The rhythm of the plate, twelve beats, lived in her chest. She let it match her breath.

Kael did not move his arm. He did not pull away. He did not stroke her hair or cup her neck or make a gesture of comfort. He simply stayed. The muscle under her brow was tense, the tendons tight. He was holding himself still for her.

The leaf lay under her hand. Renn's sheet lay beside it, the small drawn line with its eleven ticks marked in chalk. The iron door marked *warm* in Renn's hand waited in the boiling-house cellar, a day's walk away. The carried differences had not been erased. They had been moved.

She lifted her head. Her hand stayed on the page, over his.

"Moved to where," she said.

Kael's eyes dropped to the leaf. To the carried line under the newest row. To the name written beside Vane.

Ashworth.

His breath caught. His hand turned under hers, palm up, fingers closing loose around her wrist. Not pulling. Holding.

"Ashworth," he said. The word came out thin. "That is — my family name. In the carried hand. In the newest row."

The lean of the hand. The rightward slant. The same lean as the carried line on her mother's row. The same lean as the carried line on her own row.

"Written after Corrin was frozen," Sol said. "The clerk who sent the leaf said the newest entry was added after."

Kael's thumb moved on her wrist. A small stroke. Once. "The works steward's hand leans right. The leaf says so. The carried hand leans right."

"The works steward taught Renn his letters as a boy. Renn's hand resembles the forged line because she taught him too."

"She carried for forty years. The gauge did not move while she carried. It moved when Renn was locked in the third gallery. Three years. The nearest living body paid."

"Renn was the nearest living body to the plate."

The words laid themselves out between them, each one a step on a stair they had not chosen. The eleven ticks on Renn's sheet. The twelve breaths of the charge cycle. The second line adding its breath.

Ansa's shadow fell across the doorway. The old splicer stood there, her tar-stained apron dark in the light, her nephew's face behind her shoulder. Tam. The boy who had signed the standard on the tenth of Cinderveil and withdrawn on the fifteenth and still worked the tar.

"Room's cooling," Ansa said. Her voice was dry, stripped of kindness. "Kettle's on if you want. Tea's bitter today."

Sol withdrew her hand from the leaf. The paper felt colder where her palm had been. Kael released her wrist. His hand stayed on the table, palm up, empty.

"Tea would be good," Sol said. "Thank you, Ansa."

Ansa jerked her chin. "Eat."

She turned. Tam followed, his boots silent on the boards. The door swung shut.

Sol looked at the leaf. At the carried line under the newest row. At the name Ashworth written in a hand that leaned right, the same lean as the year carried from her mother, the same lean as the year carried from her.

"Moved to where," she said again, quieter. She laid the leaf beside Renn's sheet, aligning the edges. The eleven ticks on the drawn line stared up at her. "That is the question."

The tea arrived in two chipped mugs, steam curling above the rim. Ansa set them down without a word, a slab of dark bread and a crock of salted butter beside them. The brew was bitter, tannic, the way she made it when the tar yield was low. Sol wrapped her fingers around the ceramic, letting the heat seep into her palms.

Kael blew across his mug. He took a sip, swallowed, and set it down. His face did not change.

"Renn's sheet," he said. "The eleven ticks. You counted them at the plate."

"I counted them at the plate," Sol said. "Eleven breaths between pulses. The second line adds the twelfth."

"And the gauge reads seventeen. Unchanged since Renn was found."

"Unchanged since Renn was found." She repeated the words, tasting them. "The nearest living body is no longer locked in the dark."

Kael's square hand closed around his mug. The scar on the back of it caught the light. "Mara's cold climbs. Wrists. Forearms. Elbows by the twenty-seventh."

"The custodian's order gives thirty days from Day Zero. Sixteen gone before it was served. Fourteen remain."

"Day Twenty-Nine. The first attempt to close."

"Day Thirty. The order runs out."

The count hung between them, spoken in the quiet of the tar-shop room. The shutters rattled in a gust. Dust motes rose and fell in the light shaft.

Sol tore a piece of bread. She buttered it slow, the knife scraping. "The works steward carried for forty years. The gauge did not move. She was not the nearest living body to the plate."

"Renn was."

"Three years on the gauge while he was locked in the third gallery. The nearest living body paid."

"The gauge does not rise when nobody stands near."

"No." Sol ate the bread. The crust was hard, the butter salty. "It rose when Renn stood at the plate. It stopped when he was found."

Kael's jaw tightened. "The works steward's hand leans right. The carried hand leans right. She taught Renn his letters. His hand resembles the forged line because she taught him too."

"She wrote the carried lines. Every second hand. Forty years of them."

"On the order of the old head of the house. Malrik's father. Alone."

Sol's knife paused. "Malrik did not know."

"No."

"She locked Renn in the third gallery to keep him alive and away from the pegs. Not knowing the line would draw on him."

"She did not know."

"She asks to be given to no one until the line is closed."

Kael nodded once. "She asked me. In the cellar. The night we found the glove."

Sol's hand stilled on the table. "You did not tell me."

"There was no time. The stair. The plate. The count." He met her eyes. "I am telling you now."

The admission sat between them, clean and unvarnished. No apology. No explanation beyond what the facts required. Sol felt something in her chest loosen, a knot she had not known she was holding.

"Thank you," she said.

He inclined his head. "The primer. The inked-out name. The date she began to carry."

"Forty years ago. The day she began." Sol's thumb traced the rim of her mug. "She wants to be found. But not by the Council. Not by Thorne."

"By us."

"By the ones who will close the line."

Kael was quiet for a long moment. The shutters clattered. A gull cried, distant. The tar shop below groaned, timbers shifting in the heat.

"The pegs," he said. "The ledge peg in your pack. The table peg in my pocket. Same mold. Placed."

"Pulled in one breath. Two pullers beyond the room's reach. Odo and Tessa. The rope rig from outside."

"You sit at the plate. I hold the rope from where I stand."

"Aloud and with you. Every time."

"Every time."

The ritual words. The promise. Sol drank the tea. It was bitter, yes, but hot. It warmed the cold place in her fingers.

"The anchor plate," she said. "Two hundred and eleven steps down. My mother's plate. Locked. Never touched."

"Not in this book."

"Not ever."

Kael set his mug down. The sound was final. "The third-gallery plate. The seized plate. You burned half a year there."

"I did."

"You will not burn there again."

The statement was not a question. It was not a command. It was a fact laid on the table between them, like the leaf and the sheet and the bread.

Sol looked at him. The grey light caught the line of his jaw, the faint scar through his left eyebrow, the square set of his shoulders. The stick leaned against the table leg. The numb leg stretched out under the board.

"No," she said. "I will not."

"Good."

The word was rough. He stood. The chair scraped back. He moved to the window, stood with his back to the light, looking out over the tar roofs toward the cliff. His shoulders were tight. The left hip did not show the limp, not standing still.

Sol watched him. The leaf lay under her hand. Renn's sheet beside it. The eleven ticks. The carried lines. The name Ashworth in the newest row, written in a hand that leaned right.

She rose. Her chair made no sound. She came up beside him at the window. The air was cooler here, smelling of pitch and salt and the green rot of the cliff face. Below, the Lower Reach curled away into mist. The boiling house loomed, stone bones grown into the rock. Steam rose from its vents, curling into the grey.

"Day Five," Kael said. His voice was low. "Five days since the petition. Twenty-nine since the Deed was set aside. Nine of twelve houses."

"Fourteen days until the order runs out."

"Fourteen."

He turned his head. His eyes found hers. The grey flecks in the dark iris. The pulse in his throat.

"Stay," he said. "Tonight. In the room. With me."

The request was naked. No ledger voice. No ritual. Just the words, offered in the cooling light.

Sol's hand found his sleeve. The wool was rough. She curled her fingers into the fabric. "I am not leaving."

His hand came up. Covered hers. The square palm warm, the fingers closing slow. "Good."

They stood at the window, the tar-shop room behind them, the leaf and the sheet and the cold tea and the hard bread. The rope between them was not hemp today. It was the carried lines on a ledger leaf. The twelve breaths of a charge cycle. The seventeen years on a gauge that had not moved in forty years until a surveyor was locked in the dark.

It was the name Ashworth written in a hand that leaned right, the same lean as the year carried from her mother, the same lean as the year carried from her.

It was the forehead against the forearm. The breath matched to twelve beats. The hand turned palm up on the table, waiting.

It was the promise spoken aloud and with you, every time.

The light faded behind the cliff. The shutters would need closing. The kettle would need refilling. The stair waited, and the plate, and the dark behind the bare iron door where the Kindling breathed twelve beats to a cycle and stored the years in iron and waited for the pegs to be pulled.

But not tonight.

Tonight, the room over the tar shop held two people and a table and a leaf that asked a question neither of them could answer alone.

Moved to where.

Sol leaned her shoulder against Kael's arm. He did not move away. He did not shift. The muscle was hard under her cheek, the tendons tight. He was holding himself still for her.

She smiled against his sleeve. He could not see it. He would feel it.

"Fourteen days," she said.

"Fourteen," he agreed.

The gull cried again. The shutters rattled. The tar shop settled, a deep groan in the beams.

Kael's thumb stroked the back of her hand. Once. Slow.

"Come to bed," he said.

Sol lifted her head. Her hand stayed in his. The leaf on the table caught the last of the light, the carried lines leaning right, the eleven ticks on the drawn line, the name Ashworth in the newest row.

"Yes," she said. "Come to bed."
