The back step caught the afternoon sun, a slab of warmth against the lane's grey stone. Sol sat beside him with her undyed coat folded under her and her hands empty on her knees. Kael planted his stick between his boots and let the quiet settle.

No lanterns. No cords. No watch to relieve. The lane behind the tar-shop held the two of them and the wall at their backs, and nothing else that needed anything.

"Today is the hour," Sol said. Her voice was low, shaped by the stillness. "The one I asked for. The one you said you would keep in your coat."

Kael did not answer at once. He watched a crack in the mortar widen toward the eaves. The sun warmed his face, his shoulders, the old ache in his left hip.

"I kept it," he said.

A long stretch passed. From below the yard the hum came and went, too faint to count. Far off a hammer struck metal, once, twice, then stopped.

"This is harder than the plate," Sol said.

Kael turned his head. Her profile caught the light, the bridge of her nose, the dark fall of her hair.

"It is the hardest thing I have ever done on purpose," he said.

She let out a breath that might have been a laugh. "We are terrible at this."

"At what?"

"At doing nothing. Properly."

He glanced at the wall. Counted the stones in the second course. Fourteen. Fifteen. The sixteenth was split down the middle.

"Fourteen, fifteen, sixteen," he murmured.

Sol's hand moved to her sleeve. Her fingers found the knot at the cuff, worked it loose, pulled the thread through. The fabric fell smooth.

"Zero," she said. "I lasted zero breaths."

"You counted the stones."

"You untied a knot."

"A draw, then."

"Draws do not count. We need a winner."

Ansa appeared at the lane's turn, a pot balanced on her hip, her brown hands knotted around the handle. She stopped. Looked at them. Her eyes moved from Kael to Sol and back.

"You look unwell," she said. "Both of you. Sitting in the sun like lizards on a stone."

"We are resting," Kael said.

"Resting," Ansa repeated. The word was flat. "It does not suit either of you."

She carried on up the lane, the pot swaying, her boots quiet on the cobbles.

Sol watched her go. A smile tugged at her mouth and faded. Her gaze came back to Kael and sharpened.

His face had gone still. The list was forming behind his eyes, item by item, the way it always did when the waiting grew long. Check the cord eyes once more. Ask Joren about the east approach. Read the gauge again. See that the steward's request is honored. Write the note for the clerk.

She reached out. Her palm covered his eyes, warm and light.

"You are doing it," she said.

"I am not."

"I can feel the list through my palm. It is pressing against my skin."

A pause. The corner of his mouth twitched.

"I am adding 'stop making lists' to the list," he said.

She laughed, a sudden bright sound, and pressed her face into his shoulder. Her fingers curled into the wool of his coat. The tension in his jaw let go a fraction at a time.

"Terrible at this," she murmured against the fabric.

"The worst."

She shifted. Lay back with her head in his lap and her feet braced on the step. Kael let his hand rest in her hair, the strands coarse against his palm, and flattened his other hand on the warm stone beside him.

The lane held its peace. The hammer did not strike again.

"What would you do," Sol asked, "with a whole season? No order. No plan. Nothing waiting."

Kael looked down at her. The sun caught the line of her jaw, the hollow of her throat, the pulse ticking steady there.

"I would walk the Reach," he said. "Every gallery. Every stair. And I would not look at one line. Not the gauge. Not the map. Not the chalk marks on the lintel. Just the stone and the air and the light on the water."

Sol's eyes closed. Her hand found his on the stone, fingers lacing through his.

"I would sit on the gallery wall with Ansa," she said. "And learn to splice. Properly. Not the emergency knots. The ones that hold for forty years. The ones she ties in her sleep."

"She would teach you."

"She would complain the whole time. And she would teach me."

The word hung between them. After. Small and plain. Not a prayer and not a bargain.

"Where would the bed go?" Kael asked. "In the room over the tar-shop."

Sol opened her eyes. They held the lane, the wall, the patch of sky above the rooftops.

"Under the window," she said. "Where the morning light hits the floorboards. A lamp on the shelf by the hearth. Ansa's chair by the door."

"Would she let the room be ours?"

"She said eat. That was her whole answer. I think she has already decided."

"Then it is decided."

Neither of them said a number. Neither of them said if.

"This is the first time," Sol said, her voice barely above the quiet, "I have said after without it sounding like a prayer."

Kael's thumb traced the knuckle of her index finger, slow, deliberate.

"This is the first time I have said it at all."

She turned her head and pressed her cheek against his thigh. The wool was rough and warm, familiar as his own stride.

The wall held its warmth. The sun tracked toward the western ridge.

Boots on the cobbles broke the stillness. Kael felt the step through the stone before he heard it, and Sol's hand tightened on his.

Joren came round the corner of the tar-shop at a walk, not a run, which Kael took as a courtesy. He stopped three paces short of the step.

"The pick and two men are on the east approach," he said. "They have stopped at the Thorne line."

Kael did not rise at once. The hour had a little light left in it, and he wanted to give it back properly. "Joren and Corren," he said, aloud, and was glad to find it no longer needed a push. "I will come to the line with you, and I will stop where the line stops."

Joren nodded and waited.

Sol sat up. The coat slid from her shoulder, and she caught it and settled it. "Then the hour ends," she said.

"It does." He turned to her. "Sol. I am going to the line. Will you stay on the step?"

He asked it aloud and held still. He could feel the old answer sitting ready at the back of his teeth, and he let it sit. One. Two. Three.

"Yes," she said. "Go. I will keep the sun for you."

He rose. The stick found the stone, and his left leg took the weight, the slow ache falling into the rhythm he had learned to carry. Joren fell in at his right, a half step behind. They went up the lane.

The yard had gone gold and long-shadowed. Odo and Tessa stood by the cellar mouth with the cord boards at their feet, doing a good impression of two people who were not watching anything. Mara sat on a stool at the head of the cellar steps, upright, her own stick across her knees, her face turned to the east approach. By the far wall Aldous stood with the works steward, whose hands were folded in her sleeves. Kael did not look at the steward, and the steward did not look at him. They had said what they had to say in the night.

Corren was already at the line, a half step in front of the Thorne guard, as though he had grown there. The guard stood in its long grey row and did not move a boot.

On the far side, where the east approach ran out of the guard's shadow, three figures in Council grey stood waiting. The one in front wore a hood. The other two stood a pace behind, and that was how Kael knew which was which.

He stopped at the line. Joren stopped with him. Nobody spoke.

His body knew exactly where it wanted him. Two steps forward, between the grey and the cellar mouth, the way it had known on the stair, the way it always knew. The wish was so plain it was almost restful. He put the ferrule of the stick down on the cobble and leaned his weight on it instead, and counted three, and then three more. The wish stayed where it was. He stayed where he was. The wind came up off the cliff with its smell of tar and cold stone, and the silence stretched until it had a texture, and he let it.

Then he said it, in a voice that carried across the cobbles without being raised.

"The room holds one seat at the plate, and it is taken. Nobody can be nearer than the one who sits."

He had expected the words to sound small. They did not. They stood in the air like a post set in the ground.

The hood turned. It took in the cellar mouth, and the stair going down into the dark, and the woman on the stool with a stick across her knees. It rested on the stool for a good while, as though the stool were the difficulty. Mara looked back at it and did not blink.

The hooded one did not come further. The two behind did not move either. The Thorne line held, and by the wall Aldous and the steward watched without a word, and the light went out of the cobbles one flagstone at a time.

Kael waited, and it was a different thing from what he had done all his life. He had held doors with his body, with his shoulders, with the places he could get to faster than anyone else. This was a line held with nothing but what he had said and what he would not do. It ought to have felt thinner than that. It frightened him less than he had expected.

At dusk the hooded one sat down on a stone at the roadside. The two in grey sat down on either side, as if it were an arrangement they had made long ago. Nobody told them to go. Nobody made them.

"Joren and Corren," Kael said. "I am going back to the step."

"We hold the line," Joren said.

Kael turned and walked back across the cobbles, stick and step, stick and step. As he passed the cellar mouth, Mara spoke without turning her head.

"He looked at my stool for a long time," she said. "I do not think he cared for it."

"It is a good stool."

"It is a hard stool, Kael. I am keeping it on principle."

He found that he was almost smiling when he reached the lane.

Sol had kept her word about the sun. One last strip of it lay along the step, thin and gold, and she had her boots in it. He lowered himself beside her, the stick across his knees, and the stone gave back the day's warmth through his coat.

"I held the door," he said, aloud. "I did not step past it."

She turned to look at him. "Did it cost you?"

"It did." He let her wait for the rest, because she had taught him that it was kind. "It was not a year."

Her hand found his on the stick and closed over it. The lane was quiet. The hammer did not strike. Out in the yard the last of the light went off the Council grey, and nobody stood up.

"Tomorrow, then," Sol said.

"Ask me first," he said.

"I will."
