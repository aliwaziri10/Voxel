The back step caught the afternoon sun, a slab of warmth against the lane's grey stone. Sol sat beside him, her undyed coat folded beneath her, her hands empty on her knees. Kael planted his stick between his boots and let the quiet settle.

No lanterns. No cords. No watch to relieve. The lane behind the tar-shop held only the two of them and the wall at their backs.

"Today is the hour," Sol said. Her voice was low, shaped by the stillness. "The one I asked for. The one you said you would keep in your coat."

Kael did not answer at once. He watched a crack in the mortar widen toward the eaves. The sun warmed his face, his shoulders, the old ache in his left hip.

"I kept it," he said.

A long stretch passed. The hum from the cellar mouth rose and fell in its eleven-and-twelfth rhythm, distant enough to ignore. Somewhere a hammer struck metal, once, twice, then stopped.

"This is harder than the plate," Sol said.

Kael turned his head. Her profile caught the light, the bridge of her nose, the dark fall of her hair, the grey at her temple that had not been there a season ago.

"It is the hardest thing I have ever done on purpose," he said.

She let out a breath that might have been a laugh. "We are terrible at this."

"At what?"

"At doing nothing. Properly."

He glanced at the wall. Counted the stones in the second course. Fourteen. Fifteen. The sixteenth was split down the middle.

"Fourteen, fifteen, sixteen," he murmured.

Sol's hand moved to her sleeve. Her fingers found the knot at the cuff, worked it loose, pulled the thread through. The fabric fell smooth.

"Zero," she said. "I lasted zero breaths."

"You counted the stones."

"You untied a knot."

"A draw, then."

"Draws do not count. We need a winner."

Ansa appeared at the lane's turn, a pot balanced on her hip, her brown hands knotted around the handle. She stopped. Looked at them. Her eyes, the colour of river water in winter, moved from Kael to Sol and back.

"You look unwell," she said. "Both of you. Sitting in the sun like lizards on a stone."

"We are resting," Kael said.

"Resting," Ansa repeated. The word was flat. "It does not suit either of you."

She carried on up the lane, the pot swaying, her boots quiet on the cobbles.

Sol watched her go. A smile tugged at her mouth, then faded. Her gaze returned to Kael, sharpening.

His face had gone still. The list was forming behind his eyes, item by item, the way it always did when the waiting grew long. Check the rigging. Confirm the watch rotation. Speak to Joren about the pick's escort. Verify the gauge reading one more time. Ensure the steward's request is honoured. Write the note for the clerk. Find the page in Renn's map that shows the sill socket depth.

She reached out. Her palm covered his eyes, warm and light.

"You are doing it," she said.

"I am not."

"I can feel the list through my palm. It is pressing against my skin."

A pause. The corner of his mouth twitched.

"I am adding 'stop making lists' to the list," he said.

She laughed, a sudden bright sound, and pressed her face into his shoulder. Her fingers curled into the wool of his coat. The tension in his jaw released, a fraction at a time.

"Terrible at this," she murmured against the fabric.

"The worst."

She shifted. Lay back with her head in his lap, her feet braced on the step. The coat pulled tight across her shoulders. Kael let his hand rest in her hair, the strands coarse against his palm, and flattened his other hand on the warm stone beside him.

The lane held its peace. The hammer did not strike again. The hum from below remained a vibration in the stone, not a sound.

"What would you do," Sol asked, "with a whole season? No order. No plan. Nothing waiting."

Kael looked down at her. The sun caught the line of her jaw, the hollow of her throat, the pulse ticking steady there.

"I would walk the Reach," he said. "Every gallery. Every stair. Every tunnel. And I would not look at one line. Not the gauge. Not the map. Not the chalk marks on the lintel. Just the stone and the air and the light on the water."

Sol's eyes closed. Her hand found his on the stone, fingers lacing through his.

"I would sit on the gallery wall with Ansa," she said. "And learn to splice. Properly. Not the emergency knots. The ones that hold for forty years. The ones she ties in her sleep."

"She would teach you."

"She would complain the whole time. And she would teach me."

The word hung between them. After. Small. Plain. Not a prayer. Not a bargain.

"Where would the bed go?" Kael asked. "In the room over the tar-shop."

Sol opened her eyes. The grey of them held the lane, the wall, the patch of sky above the rooftops.

"Under the window," she said. "Where the morning light hits the floorboards. The lamp on the shelf by the hearth. Ansa's chair by the door."

"Would she let the room be ours?"

"She said eat. That was her whole answer. I think she has already decided."

"Then it is decided."

Neither of them said a number. Neither of them said if.

"This is the first time," Sol said, her voice barely above the hum in the stone, "I have said after without it sounding like a prayer."

Kael's thumb traced the knuckle of her index finger, slow, deliberate.

"This is the first time I have said it at all."

She turned her head. Pressed her cheek against his thigh. The wool was rough, warm, familiar as his stride.

The lane stayed quiet. The wall held its warmth. The hum rose and fell, eleven breaths and a twelfth, and the sun tracked toward the western ridge.

Joren's boots on the cobbles broke the stillness. Kael felt the vibration before he heard the step. Sol's hand tightened on his.

"Joren and Corren," Kael said aloud. The words shaped themselves in the air. "The pick and two men are on the east approach."

He did not move. The list pressed against his ribs, quiet now, patient. The hour was not finished. The light still held the step.

Sol sat up. The coat slipped from her shoulder. She caught it, settled it, turned to face the lane's turn.

"Then the hour ends," she said.

"It does."

He rose. The stick found the stone. His left leg took the weight, the slow pulse of the ache matching the rhythm he had learned to carry. Sol stood beside him, her hand finding his arm, tucking beneath it as she had on the cliff road, as she had in the yard the night before.

The lane stretched ahead. The Thorne guard waited at the east approach, a line of copper clasps in the shadow of the wall. Aldous and the works steward stood in the yard beyond, small shapes against the grey stone. The pick stood with them, Council grey, unnamed, patient.

Kael walked. Stick, step. Stick, step. Sol matched him, stride for stride, her fingers warm through the wool.

The pick stopped at the Thorne line. The two men stopped with the pick. Nobody spoke. The silence stretched, measured in the hum from the cellar and the wind off the cliff.

Kael planted the stick. Let the quiet fill the space between the guard and the Council grey. The pick looked at the cellar mouth, at Mara on her stool at the stair's head, at the stair going down into the dark.

"The room holds one seat at the plate," Kael said. His voice carried across the yard, plain and aloud. "It is taken. Nobody can be nearer than the one who sits."

The words landed. The pick did not move. The two men did not move. The Thorne guard did not move. The steward and Aldous watched from the yard, motionless.

Kael felt the strange lightness in his chest, the thing he had found in the stairwell two days past. Holding a line with words, aloud and plainly, and not with his body. A different kind of strength. It frightened him less than he expected.

The sun dropped behind the ridge. Shadows lengthened across the yard. The pick sat down on a stone at the edge of the Thorne line. The two men sat beside the pick. Nobody made them leave.

Kael turned. Walked back across the cobbles. Sol fell into step beside him. The stick clicked. Her boots made no sound.

They reached the back step again. The wall. The patch of sun, now thin and golden.

"He held the door," Sol said. "And did not step past it."

Kael lowered himself onto the step. The stick rested across his knees. The ache in his left leg settled into its familiar shape.

"It cost me," he said.

"A year?"

"No. Not a year."

She sat beside him. Close. Her shoulder pressed against his. The lane held its peace. The hammer struck once, far off. The hum rose and fell.

Joren's voice carried from the yard gate. "The pick and two men are on the east approach. Moving toward the Thorne line."

Kael was already standing. The stick planted. Sol rose beside him, the motion fluid, no hesitation.

"Joren and Corren," Kael said. The names shaped the air. He walked toward the gate, Sol at his shoulder, Odo and Tessa falling in behind with their cords coiled. Mara sat on her stool at the cellar mouth, upright, her own stick across her knees, her face turned toward the east approach.

The Thorne guard held their position, a wall of grey cloaks and copper clasps. Aldous and the works steward stood in the yard, the steward's hands folded in her sleeves, Aldous's hand light on her elbow.

Three figures in Council grey approached the line. One tall. One broad. One slight, moving with the careful gait of someone who had walked far and would walk further.

The pick. And two others.

Kael stopped at the Thorne line. Joren and Corren flanked him, grey cloaks motionless. The pick halted three paces away. The broad figure and the slight figure stopped beside the pick.

Nobody spoke. The wind off the cliff carried the smell of tar and mineral dust. The hum from the stairwell rose and fell, eleven breaths and a twelfth.

Kael let the silence stretch. Counted the stones in the wall behind the guard. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. The list did not form. The quiet held.

"The room holds one seat at the plate," he said. "It is taken. Nobody can be nearer than the one who sits."

The pick's face was lined, patient, the eyes the colour of river water in winter. The gaze moved from Kael to the cellar mouth, to Mara on her stool, to the stair disappearing into the dark.

The broad figure shifted. The slight figure did not move.

The Thorne guard stood unchanged. The steward did not move. Aldous's hand stayed on her elbow.

Kael felt the lightness in his chest again. Words instead of the body. A line held aloud. It was not the strength he had known. It was not the strength he had trained for. It was something else, and it did not frighten him.

The sun dropped lower. The shadows stretched across the yard, long fingers reaching toward the cellar mouth.

The pick sat down on a stone at the edge of the Thorne line. The broad figure sat beside the pick. The slight figure sat on the pick's other side. Three figures in Council grey, waiting.

Nobody made them leave.

Kael turned. Walked back across the cobbles. Sol fell into step. Odo and Tessa followed. Joren and Corren took up the rear.

The back step caught the last of the light. Kael lowered himself onto the stone. The stick rested across his knees. Sol sat beside him, close, her shoulder against his.

"He held the door," she said. "And did not step past it."

"It cost me," Kael said.

"A year?"

"No. Not a year."

Her hand found his on the stick. Fingers closing slow. The palms rough. The grip steady.

The lane held its peace. The hammer did not strike again. The hum rose and fell, eleven breaths and a twelfth, and the first watch changed to the second in the yard beyond.

"Tomorrow," Sol said. "The attempt."

"Tomorrow," Kael agreed. "The pegs. The cords. The pullers."

She leaned her head on his shoulder. His cheek rested on her hair. The stick lay across his knees. The lantern in the yard threw their shadows long across the cobbles, leaning toward the cellar mouth, toward the stair, toward the plate.

The pick waited at the Thorne line. The steward watched from the yard. Aldous stood beside her. Mara sat at the cellar mouth. The gauge read seventeen. The pegs waited wrapped in cloth on the stair.

The night was long. The first watch settled. The second watch rose.

Kael closed his eyes. The darkness behind his lids was shot through with the afterimage of the three figures in Council grey, the line of the guard, the steward's pale mark at her throat.

His hand tightened on Sol's fingers. The warmth of her bled through the wool, into the chill of the evening.

The hum continued its eleven-and-twelfth rhythm. The gauge read seventeen. The pegs waited.

The watch changed again. Boots on cobbles, quiet voices, the lantern passed from hand to hand. The third watch took the stair. Joren and Corren walked the yard line, grey cloaks blending with the shadows.

Sol did not move from Kael's shoulder. His hand remained on hers, the grip slow, deliberate. The wool of his coat was rough against her cheek. The ache in his left leg pulsed, a slow rhythm she had learned to read in the tension of his fingers.

"Tomorrow, then," she said.

The words sat in the cooling air. The hum from the stairwell rose and fell, eleven breaths and a twelfth, indifferent to the figures waiting at the Thorne line, to the steward's pale mark at her throat, to the gauge reading seventeen fixed and unmoving.

Kael's thumb traced the knuckle of her index finger. The callus there, the line where the rope had bitten deep.

"Ask me first," he said.

She lifted her head. The lantern light caught the grey at his temple, the white strand threading above his ear, the shadow under his cheekbone where the stick had pressed.

"I will," she said.

His mouth curved, a small thing, not a smile but something nearer to it. The lightness in his chest, the thing he had found in the stairwell, the thing that was not the strength he had known — it settled quieter now, a weight he could carry.

The pick sat at the Thorne line, three figures in Council grey against the night. The steward watched from the yard. Aldous stood beside her. Mara did not move from her stool at the cellar mouth. The pegs waited wrapped in cloth on the stair. The gauge read seventeen.
