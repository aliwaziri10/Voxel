Grey light lay across the table in a long bar. The pot of Ansa's tea had gone cold. Steam rose thin and straight from the spout and did not curl. The table was too small for two, and their elbows brushed whenever either of them reached. Kael's stick leaned against the wall at his back. His left leg lay stiff beneath the boards, where there was no room for it.

Sol held her cup in both hands and did not drink. She watched him across the wood with her head tilted, a smile at the corner of her mouth.

"If I am going to ask you aloud and wait," she said, "I had better practice on small things. Pass the cup."

He picked it up at once. Nothing spilled. He set it in front of her.

"No." She shook her head. "You have to wait until I have finished asking. Then count three before you answer."

"I did wait."

"You handed it to me while I was still on the word cup."

His brow came down. "That was very fast asking."

"Try again. The salt."

He took the wooden salt cellar in his square fingers and did not move it. His throat worked. One heartbeat. Two. Three. He set it down beside her plate.

"Better." She turned the cellar over once. "The spoon."

The spoon lay between them. His hand hovered above it, the tendons standing faint along his forearm. One. Two. Three. His fingers closed on the handle and he passed it to her handle first.

"The stool."

He looked at the stool tucked under the table, and back at her. One. Two. Three. He pushed it out with his foot.

"The window."

He turned his head. The grey bar of light caught along his jaw. He counted, and found that he had stopped breathing to do it. "It is shut."

She laughed, small and surprised. "You are not meant to be turned to stone. Only to be a person who answers."

"That is a great deal to ask of a man in a coat."

"Take the coat off, then."

He looked down at the wool across his shoulders. "It is a great deal to ask of a man in a shirt as well."

She reached across the table and closed her fingers on his wrist. His pulse jumped under them and he let it. "The lamp. When we go down the second stair to see it. Will you let me carry it?"

His body leaned toward her before the last word was out. The lean stopped. His shoulders locked and his breath held. One. Two. Three. "Yes. And I would prefer to carry it myself. I am saying so aloud because you asked me to."

"That is the exact right answer." Her thumb moved along the bone of his wrist. "It is also a very small lie."

"It is a very small truth with a lie standing next to it."

She took both his hands in hers. The wood was cold under their forearms. She turned his palms up, and the grey light lay in the calluses that years of rope and stick and stone had put there.

"One. Two. Three." She said it with him, evenly, the way one might teach a child to breathe.

He turned her hands over in his. He leaned across the table, and the wood creaked under him, and he pressed his mouth to the inside of her wrist. Her skin was warm. He felt her breath stop and start.

"That is not part of the exercise."

He lifted his head. "I am adding a clause."

The smile went out of her face and a quieter look came in behind it. Her fingers tightened on his. "I would like to be asked a small thing by you. Aloud. With a wait after. And I will not hurry the answer."

He was quiet. The pot ticked as it cooled. Down in the lane a cart went by and was gone. One. Two. Three. "Will you pass the tea?"

She lifted the pot. The handle was cold and she poured anyway, and the tea ran the color of old amber into the cup. She set it in front of him. "Yes."

He had not moved. He found that his whole body had been waiting for that word, shoulders still, hands flat on the wood, and that he had not reached for the cup until she set it down. He drank. The swallow was long and slow.

"Good," he said.

Boots came up the tar-shop stair, slow, two pairs, one of them dragging a little. The door opened.

Ulla stood in the doorway with her hand beneath Renn's elbow, barely touching him. Renn's left wrist was bound in a strip of grey cloth, the knot neat, the fingers curled loose. He moved carefully and kept his weight on the right side. The ink in the creases of his knuckles showed dark against the pale skin.

Sol rose. Her chair scraped. She did not look at Kael. She looked at Renn.

"I am asking you aloud, and plainly," she said. "Draw the room. The stair, the turn, the plate, so that it is settled where each person may stand. And where the pegs sit."

She did not say anything after it. Kael watched her wait. Her hands hung still at her sides, and he saw her lips move once, on the count, and understood that she was giving Renn the same three heartbeats she had taught Kael over the salt. It cost her, and he could see the price in her shoulders. He knew the look of it, because he had worn it all week.

Kael looked at the bound wrist and thought of the gallery where Renn had been found, and kept the thought to himself. Renn's gaze went over the table, the window, the open door. He nodded once. "I can. I need the sealed page you have. The back of it is clean. I need two days. And I need a promise that nobody makes me go down."

Sol reached into her coat. The folded paper came out with its edges worn soft, and she held it across the table. He took it in his right hand, and the binding on his left wrist pulled tight.

"Nobody will," she said.

Ulla stepped in beside him. Her face was calm and her hands were folded at her waist. "We will leave after the close. I am saying it aloud so that no one is surprised."

"Thank you for saying it," Sol said.

Renn turned the page over. The back was blank, the fibers pale and clean. "Two days. You will have it the morning after next."

Joren came up the stair behind them and stopped at the door, the scar across the back of his hand pale against the wood of the frame. Corren stood a step below him. Joren held a folded square of paper with a crisp edge.

"From Malrik Thorne," Joren said. "A Thorne house guard brought it to the east approach at first light."

Sol took it. The paper was heavy, the fold sharp, the ink dark and even. She turned it over to look at the back, and the back was bare. She read it through once to herself, and then she read it aloud, and the words fell flat in the small room.

"From first light the Thorne house guard holds the east approach at Thorne's cost. The pick and anyone in Council grey are not to pass. Thorne keeps the door and nothing else."

Nobody spoke. Kael took his stick from the wall. He felt the wood settle into his palm, and the weight of the leg come onto it.

"It is an offer of a door," he said. "Not of a hand."

Sol folded the paper and tucked it into her coat. "Then we take it as a door."

"Joren and Corren," Kael said, aloud, to both of them, and Joren gave one short nod. "I am going to the yard gate to see it."

"May I come?" Sol said.

He wanted to say yes before she was done. He felt the word arrive and he let it stand in his mouth while the count ran. One. Two. Three. "Yes."

She did not smile. She looked at him, and her shoulders eased by a hair. Ulla and Renn stayed at the table with the sealed page between them, and the grey bar of light caught the ink on Renn's knuckles as he smoothed it flat.

In the lane Sol walked on his left, where the leg was. She had asked him that too, on the stair, in a low voice, and waited. The boiling-house vents breathed their mineral air across the stones. The lane was empty. A cold, sour breath of tar came and went on the wind.

Halfway along she stopped. "Will you tell me if the leg wants to stop? Aloud, before it does."

He gave it the whole count, though the answer was ready at one. "Yes. It wants to now."

They stood against the wall of the tar-shop while the ache passed. She did not offer her arm, and he was grateful for that, and said so, and she nodded as if he had passed her the salt.

---

The yard gate stood at the end of the lane with its crossbar down. Joren and Corren took their places on this side of it without being told. Beyond the bars the east approach ran away grey and open, and across the width of it, from the wall on one side to the drop on the other, stood a line of men in Thorne grey.

Kael stopped three steps short of the gate. The men did not turn. They stood at even intervals with their hands empty at their sides and their faces to the front, and not one of them looked at the gate, or at the wardens at the crossbar, or at the road behind them. They had been there since first light. Their cloaks hung straight in the still air.

The pick was gone. The two men in Council grey were gone with it. The Council road lay empty toward the cliff, the stones running off into mist.

He breathed out, and the breath fogged. His shoulders came down a little. They had been braced since the attempt, since the plate had hummed under Sol's hand and his body had moved before he could say a word, and he had not noticed the bracing until it let go. It did not let go all the way. His leg ached in the bone, a deep dull throb that had nothing to do with the cold.

Beside him Sol stood with her hands folded at her waist. She did not reach for his sleeve. The space between them was a hand's breadth, and he could feel it in his teeth.

He thought about the table. At the beginning he had been sure that the waiting would be the hard part, the three heartbeats with his body already leaning. It had been hard. But he had stood in it, and the want to answer first had stood beside him, and neither of them had done any harm. Sol asked and he answered. He had expected to chafe at the order of it. He found that he was easier in that order than in any other he had tried, because the asking was hers and the answer was all that was left to him, and he knew how to give an answer.

"An offer of a door," he said, low.

"You said." Her eyes were on the line. "I heard you."

"I wanted to say it where I could see the door."

"Is it a good one?"

He took the count before he answered. One. Two. Three. "It is a plain one. It will hold if the men holding it are paid, and they are paid by a man who has not asked us for anything. I do not trust that. I will use it."

"That is true," Sol said. "And it is a little cold."

"It is a cold door."

Sol turned her head toward him. "Tell me what you see at the gate. In order. I will wait for the whole of it."

He was not used to being asked for a list. He took the count, and then he gave it to her slowly, because she had asked for the order and he was learning that the order was the point. "The bar, and Joren and Corren on this side of it. The line of Thorne men from the wall to the drop. The road on the Council side, empty. Nothing moving on it at all."

"And where would you stand, if it were yours to say?"

That one he wanted to answer before she had finished, and he felt the want like a hand laid flat on his chest. One. Two. Three. "Here. Three steps from the bar, with Joren and Corren between me and it. Not nearer."

"Why not nearer?"

"Because I would like to be a man who stays where he says he will, and a gate is an easy place to begin. I am practicing on the gate."

Her mouth moved at the corner. "That is a very good small thing."

Corren stood at the bar with his jaw set and his eyes on the Thorne line, and he did not turn. Joren turned his head a quarter.

"How long have they stood there?" Kael asked him.

"Since first light. They did not speak when we came out. They have not moved."

Two days for Renn to draw the room. Two days in which Mara lay in Yelva's back room with the cold past her elbow, and the pick waited no farther off than the bend of the Council road. A door was a small thing to be handed with all of that in front of it. It was still more than they had held the day before, and Kael did not mean to despise it.

His thumb found the worn place on the stick and lay there. The wood was warm from his hand.

"Joren," he said. "Corren. Thank you."

Neither of them answered. Joren's scar showed white where his hand lay on the crossbar. Sol stayed at Kael's side and did not move closer or farther away, and the lane behind them emptied toward the stair mouth where the steps dropped into the dark.

At the gate the Thorne guard stood in a line across the east approach and did not look at them.
