Grey light lay across the table in a long bar. The pot of Ansa's tea had gone cold. Steam rose thin and straight from the spout and did not curl. The table was too small for two. Their elbows brushed whenever either of them reached.

Sol held her cup with both hands. The ceramic warmed her palms. She did not drink. She watched Kael across the wood, her head tilted, a smile touching the corner of her mouth.

"If I am going to ask you aloud and wait," she said, "I had better practise on small things. Pass the cup."

He picked it up at once. The liquid did not spill. He set it in front of her.

"No." She shook her head. "You have to wait until I have finished asking. Then count three before you answer."

"I did wait."

"You handed it to me while I was still on the word cup."

His brow furrowed. The corner of his mouth twitched. "That was very fast asking."

"Try again. The salt."

He picked up the wooden salt cellar. He held it in his square fingers. His throat moved. One heartbeat. Two. Three. He set it down beside her plate.

"Better." She turned the cellar in her fingers. "The spoon."

He waited. The spoon lay between them. His hand hovered. The tendons in his forearm stood faint against the skin. One. Two. Three. His fingers closed on the handle. He passed it handle first.

"The stool."

He looked at the stool tucked under the table. He looked back at her. One. Two. Three. He pushed it out with his foot.

"The window."

He turned his head. The grey bar of light caught his jaw. One. Two. Three. "It is shut."

She laughed. The sound was small and surprised. "You are not meant to be turned to stone. Only to be a person who answers."

"That is a great deal to ask of a man in a coat."

"Take the coat off, then."

He glanced at the wool across his shoulders. The left sleeve hung a little longer where the leg did not bend right. "A great deal to ask of a man in a shirt as well."

She reached across the table. Her fingers closed on his wrist. The pulse jumped under her touch. "The lamp. When we go down the second stair. Will you let me carry it?"

His body leaned toward her before the last word left her mouth. The lean stopped. His shoulders locked. His breath held. One. Two. Three. "Yes. And I would prefer to carry it myself. And I am saying so aloud because you asked me to."

"That is the exact right answer." Her thumb traced the bone of his wrist. "It is also a very small lie."

"It is a very small truth with a lie standing next to it."

She took both his hands in hers. The wood of the table was cold against her forearms. His palms were rough, the calluses mapped by years of rope and stick and stone. She turned them up. The lines on his palms caught the grey light.

"One. Two. Three." She said it with him, the count even, the way one might teach a child to breathe.

He turned her hands over. His thumbs pressed into her palms. He leaned across the table. The wood creaked. His mouth touched the inside of her wrist. The brush of his lips was hot. His breath fanned damp and warm against the skin.

"That is not part of the exercise."

He lifted his head. His eyes were dark in the grey light. "I am adding a clause."

The smile faded from her face. Something softer took its place. Her fingers tightened on his. "I would like to be asked a small thing by you. Aloud. With a wait after. And I will not hurry the answer."

He was quiet. The hum from the passage below rose through the floorboards, a low vibration in the bone. The tea pot ticked as it cooled. One. Two. Three. "Will you pass the tea?"

She picked up the pot. The handle was cold. She poured. The liquid was the colour of old amber. She set the cup in front of him. "Yes."

He had not moved. His whole body had been waiting. She saw it in the stillness of his shoulders, the way his fingers had not twitched toward the cup until the third heartbeat passed. He took it. The ceramic met his calluses. He drank. The swallow was long and slow.

"Good," he said.

The trapdoor creaked.

Sol turned. Ulla stood at the top of the ladder, Renn behind her. His left wrist was bound in a strip of grey cloth, the knot neat, the fingers curled loose. He moved slow, favouring the right side. Ulla's hand rested on his elbow, light as a feather.

The room shrank. The table seemed smaller. The grey bar of light caught the ink in the creases of Renn's knuckles, the pale line of his face.

Sol rose. Her chair scraped. She did not look at Kael. She looked at Renn.

"Draw the room," she said. "The stair. The plate. So that it is settled where each person may stand. Where the pegs sit."

Renn's gaze moved over the table, the window, the trapdoor. He nodded once. "I can. I need the sealed page you have. The back of it is clean. I need two days. And I need a promise that nobody makes me go down."

Sol reached into her coat. The folded paper came out, edges worn soft. She held it out. He took it with his right hand. The binding on his left wrist pulled tight.

"Nobody will," she said.

Ulla stepped forward. Her face was calm. Her hands were folded at her waist. "We will leave after the close. I am saying it aloud so that no one is surprised."

The words hung in the air. The hum from the passage did not waver. Renn turned the page over in his hand. The back was blank, the fibres pale and clean.

"Two days," he said. "I will have it by the morning of Day 35."

The trapdoor creaked again. Joren's head appeared, then his shoulders. The scar across the back of his hand was pale in the grey light. Corren followed, his face blank, his jaw set. Joren held a folded square of paper, the edges crisp.

"From Malrik Thorne," Joren said. "Brought to the east approach by a Thorne house guard at first light."

Sol took it. The paper was heavy, the fold sharp. She unfolded it. The script was neat, the ink dark. She read.

"From first light the Thorne house guard holds the east approach at Thorne's cost. The pick and anyone in Council grey are not to pass. Thorne keeps the door and nothing else."

She read it aloud. The words fell flat in the room.

Kael shifted his weight. The stick tapped the floor once. "It is an offer of a door. Not of a hand."

Sol folded the paper. She tucked it into her coat. "We accept it as a door."

"Joren and Corren."

The names anchored him. Joren glanced once. Corren did not look. Kael crossed to the trapdoor. The stick tapped a slow rhythm on the boards. Sol followed. Ulla and Renn stayed at the table, the sealed page between them, the grey light catching the ink in Renn's knuckles.

The lane was empty. The boiling house vents breathed mineral air. The yard gate stood at the end, the crossbar held by Joren and Corren on this side. Beyond it the east approach stretched grey and empty. A line of figures in Thorne grey stood across the width of the approach, spears rested on shoulders, faces blank. They did not look at the gate. They did not look at the pick.

The pick was gone. The two men in Council grey were gone. The Council road stretched empty toward the cliff.

Kael stopped three paces from the gate. "Joren and Corren."

Joren glanced. Corren did not. The Thorne guard did not move. The line held, solid as a wall, grey cloth and steel against the morning light.

The pick had withdrawn toward the Council road. The Thorne guard stood in a line across the east approach and did not look at them.

Kael felt the silence press against his ears. The hum from the passage below had faded to a vibration in the soles of his boots, a thing felt rather than heard. The morning air carried mineral dust and the faint sourness of tar. His left leg ached, a deep dull throb in the bone that had nothing to do with the cold.

The Thorne guard did not move. Twenty men, perhaps more, spaced at even intervals across the width of the approach. Their spears rested on shoulders, the shafts grey wood, the heads catching the pale light. Cloaks of Thorne grey hung straight, unmoved by any breeze. Faces were blank, eyes forward. Not one glance toward the gate. Not one glance toward the two wardens holding the crossbar.

Joren's scar showed white against his knuckles where he gripped the spear shaft. Corren's jaw was a hard line. Neither spoke. They did not need to. The line held.

Kael's fingers tightened on the stick. The ferrule pressed into his palm, the groove worn smooth by generations. He had carried this stick up two hundred and eleven steps yesterday. He had carried it down again. The wood knew his hand. His hand knew the wood.

Beside him, Sol stood very still. Her undyed coat hung straight. Her hands were folded at her waist. She did not reach for his sleeve. She did not need to. The space between them was a hand's breadth, no more, and it held a charge he could feel in his teeth.

"Thorne keeps the door," she had said. The words echoed in the lane, flat and final.

An offer of a door. Not of a hand. The distinction mattered. A door could be opened or barred. A hand implied something given freely, something that could be withdrawn. Malrik Thorne had offered the first and withheld the second. Kael understood the calculation. The old man protected his house. He gave them the east approach because the east approach was useless without the door, and he kept the door because the door was power.

The pick had withdrawn. The two men in Council grey had withdrawn with it. The Council road stretched empty toward the cliff, grey stones disappearing into mist. The custodian's order had lapsed on Day 30. The pick had no authority now, only presence. Presence had evaporated.

Kael exhaled. The breath fogged in the cold air. His shoulders settled. The tension that had lived in them since Day 29, since the plate had hummed and the line had woken and his body had moved before his mind could catch up, eased by a fraction. Not gone. Never gone. But eased.

He looked at the Thorne line again. Twenty men. Twenty spears. Twenty pairs of eyes that did not waver. They were not wardens. They were not Council. They were Thorne, bought and paid for, standing where Malrik had told them to stand. The cost came from Thorne's purse. The order came from Malrik's hand. The note had said it plain: at Thorne's cost.

Malrik had not come himself. The note had come. The guard had come. The door was held. Nothing else.

Kael thought of the works steward, small and upright in her copper clasp, forty years of carrying a secret no one had asked her to carry. He thought of Aldous, pale and twenty-five, his letters leaning right, his tail open, the name forged on a witness line by a hand that had taught him his letters. He thought of Renn, lean and cracked, ink in his knuckles, drawing a room so that it would be settled where each person might stand.

Two days. Renn had asked for two days. The morning of Day 35. Today was Day 33. Tomorrow would be Day 34. The day after, the map would exist.

The descent was Day 38. The pick would arrive Day 39. The close was Day 40.

Eight days. Eight days for a map, a descent, a close. Eight days for Mara's cold to climb past her elbow, for Renn's wrist to knit, for Ulla to pack whatever life they had built in the Reach. Eight days for Sol to ask aloud and wait, for Kael to answer aloud and mean it.

His thumb traced the groove on the stick. The wood was warm.

"Joren," he said. His voice carried in the still air. "Corren."

The wardens did not turn. Joren's scar caught the light. Corren's face remained blank.

"We hold," Joren said. One word. The fewest he could make it.

"We hold," Corren echoed.

Kael nodded. The stick tapped the cobblestones once. A slow rhythm. He did not look at Sol. He did not need to. He knew where she stood. He knew the rhythm of her breath. He knew the weight of her silence.

The Thorne guard did not look at them. The line held, solid as a wall, grey cloth and steel against the morning light. The east approach stretched empty behind them, the boiling house vents breathing mineral air, the lane emptying toward the stair mouth where two hundred and eleven steps dropped into dark.

The pick had withdrawn toward the Council road. The Thorne guard stood in a line across the east approach and did not look at them.
