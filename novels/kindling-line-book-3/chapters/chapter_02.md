The morning air tasted of wet stone and cold ash. Kael set his stick on the first tread of Ansa's stair and felt the familiar protest in his left hip, the needles climbing from ankle to thigh as the joint remembered the boiling house. The stair was steep, the risers worn hollow by generations of ropewalkers, and the stick did not like it any more than he did.

Sol walked on his left side. She said nothing about the leg. She said nothing about the stick. She matched her stride to his without shortening it, her undyed coat brushing the wall where the plaster had darkened from decades of damp.

The third time the stick caught on a riser, Kael exhaled through his teeth. "The left side."

Sol did not look at him. "Then keep up."

He almost laughed. The sound caught in his throat and came out as a breath instead. "I am keeping up."

"Good."

They reached the landing where the lamp bracket stood empty, the pale ring on the flagstone still visible from the night before. Ansa's loaf sat on the shelf under its cloth, crust cracked where they had torn it. The tar-shop below was quiet, the kettles cold.

At the table in the corner, Sol took the pen from the inkwell. She caught his wrist before he could pull away, turned his hand over, and pressed the nib to the skin at the base of his thumb.

"What are you writing?"

"The order of the questions." She wrote in quick strokes, the ink dark against the pale hairs on his forearm. "Malrik first. Then Aldous. Then the Chancellor's answer."

Kael watched the letters form. His cuff caught the wet ink and smudged it, a grey smear across the wool. "I wanted Aldous first."

"I know."

"He is the one who can tell us about the hand."

"Malrik is the one who will have to hear it."

The pen scratched. *Aldous. Malrik. Chancellor.* She capped the ink and set the pen down. "There. Now you cannot forget."

Ansa appeared in the doorway from the tar-shop, a cloth in her hand, her eyes sharp in the grey light. She looked at his cuff. "You have ink on your sleeve, Lord Ashworth. The works steward will have a fit if she sees it."

Kael glanced at the smudge. "The works steward is not here."

"She sees everything." Ansa wiped the table with a swipe of her cloth. "Even what happens in other houses. Tam says the copper clasp catches the light when she turns her head. Like a signal."

"Tam sees a great deal," Sol said. She pulled on her gloves, the leather creaking. "He also sees what he wants to see."

"Everyone does." Ansa tossed the cloth onto the shelf. "Eat the rest of the loaf before you go. The stair is harder on an empty stomach."

Kael looked at Sol. She was already tying the laces of her boots, her fingers quick and certain. The stick lay in his hand, the worn wood warm from his grip. He had carried it on the right side for weeks, tucked against his body, hidden from the eyes of wardens and clerks and the narrow man at the Lower Spine stair.

Sol stood. She took the stick from him, turned it in her fingers, and placed it back in his palm so the handle rested on the right side, the ferrule angled forward.

"Stop hiding it," she said.

"I am not hiding it."

"A man in a house like Thorne's wants to look sound." She fastened the top button of her coat. "Malrik will look at the stick and see what the house cost. Carry it openly."

The words settled in the space between them. Kael felt the weight of the stick shift, the balance different now, honest. He looked at Ansa, at the door, at Sol's face in the morning light.

"Fine," he said. "Openly."

They went down the last flight together. At the street door, Sol handed him his gloves. She kept hold of her end a second too long, her fingers brushing his wrist. The needles in his leg flared, sharp and sudden. He did not pull away. She did not let go. Neither of them mentioned it.

The door opened onto the cobbled lane that climbed toward House Thorne. The morning mist clung to the ropewalk lines, turning them to ghost ropes between the roofs. Kael pulled on his gloves, the leather settling over his square hand, and set the stick on the stones.

"Ready?" Sol asked.

He looked at the white front of the Thorne house, the plain silver clasp he knew waited inside, the pale heir who had spoken against his own blood. The Chancellor's question waited in his pocket, written on a slip of paper he had folded four times.

"Ready," he said.

The works steward met them in the vestibule. She was small, upright, past sixty with grey hair pinned flat and a copper clasp of three knotted strands at her throat. She wore Thorne grey, the fabric fine but unadorned. Her hands were folded at her waist.

"Lord Ashworth. Lady Vane." Her voice was quiet, pitched for the stone corridor. "Lord Thorne is in the private receiving room. The heir is with him."

She led them down a passage lined with portraits, the faces of Thorne ancestors watching from darkened frames. The air grew cooler. Kael felt the stick in his hand, the weight of it no longer hidden, and wondered if the portraits would have approved.

The receiving room door opened before they reached it. Malrik Thorne stood in the doorway, white hair caught back from his face, the plain silver clasp at his throat catching the light from the window behind him. He wore a dark coat, the cut severe, his spine straight as the stair they had climbed.

"Ashworth. Vane." He stepped back, gesturing them inside. "The works steward will leave us."

The woman behind Malrik's chair did not move at once. Her eyes found Aldous, seated in a chair by the fire, pale and still in the morning light. Her hand moved, a small gesture, fingers pressing against the arm of the chair as if to steady herself.

Aldous did not look at the door. His hands lay in his lap, the fingers long, the nails clean. The light caught the slight rightward lean of his writing hand, the tail of the thumb open.

"Go," Malrik said. "Close the door."

The works steward bowed her head. She passed Kael and Sol without looking at them, the copper clasp catching the light once more, and the door shut behind her with a soft finality.

The room was panelled in dark wood, the shelves heavy with ledgers bound in grey leather. A fire burned low in the grate. Aldous Thorne sat motionless, his eyes on the flames. Malrik took the chair behind the desk, his hands flat on the surface.

Kael planted the stick on the floorboards. He did not lean on it. He stood with his weight on both legs, the needles in his left hip a steady burn, and felt Sol settle beside him, her coat brushing his sleeve.

"Lord Thorne," Kael said. "We have a question from a letter written by a man named Farrow. The postscript reads: *Ask the boy who taught him his letters.*"

Malrik's face did not change. His eyes moved to Aldous, then back to Kael. "Farrow. The clerk from the research wing."

"The same."

"And the boy is my grandson."

"He is."

Malrik's jaw tightened. He looked at Aldous. "You were taught your letters in the still-room. By a tutor your father hired. I paid for the tutor. I knew the tutor's name."

"A tutor who was not kind," Aldous said. His voice was flat, devoid of inflection. "He taught me sums. He struck my knuckles when the numbers were wrong. I learned to hate the sight of a slate."

Kael felt Sol shift beside him. He did not look at her. He knew what she was seeing — the rightward lean of Aldous's hand, the open tail, the match to the carried hand on the leaf from Corrin's seized books.

"Who taught you the letters?" Kael asked.

Aldous turned his head. His face was pale, the bones fine, his grandfather's height in the set of his shoulders. "The works steward. In secret. In the still-room, after the tutor left. She taught me to write my name. She taught me to write the witness line."

The silence in the room thickened. Malrik's hands did not move on the desk. His eyes stayed on his grandson.

"I did not know," Malrik said. "Forty years of ledgers in this house. Forty years of watching the works steward carry the second hand. I did not know she taught the heir his letters in the still-room."

"She did not want you to know," Aldous said. "She said the hand would be safer if no one knew where it came from. She said a clerk would compare hands one day and ask who taught me. She wanted to be found. But not by the Council. Not by Thorne."

Kael pulled the folded slip from his pocket. He unfolded it, smoothed it on the desk beside Malrik's hand. The Chancellor's question, written in a thin hand: *If the hand you find belongs to a house we have both trusted, what will you do then?*

He took the pen from the desk, dipped it in the inkwell, and wrote two sentences on the back of the slip.

*I will do what I did in the petition. Aloud and with her, whoever the hand belongs to.*

He folded the slip again, placed it in the envelope addressed to the Chancellor, and sealed it with the wax stick from the desk. The impression was clean. The wax cooled grey.

"Done," Kael said. "The Chancellor will have her answer by midday."

Malrik picked up the envelope. He weighed it in his hand, the plain silver clasp catching the firelight. "Two sentences."

"Two sentences was the promise," Sol said. Her voice was calm. "We said the numbers aloud. We say the answers aloud. No silent counts."

Malrik looked at her. His eyes were the grey of the Thorne clasp, cold and clear. "The Deed was set aside by nine of twelve houses on the twenty-eighth of Cinderveil. The petition was entered on the thirtieth. The custodian's order is dated the night of the petition and gives thirty days from its date. Sixteen days have passed since the order was written. Fourteen remain."

"We know the count," Kael said.

"Do you." Malrik set the envelope down. "The custodian's pick is named on Day 26. The formal demand is read at the Lower Reach on Day 28. The first attempt to close the line is Day 29. The order runs out on Day 30. These are the dates the Council has fixed. They do not move for witness lines or secret tutors."

"The line does not move for dates either," Sol said. "It breathes twelve breaths to a cycle now. The second line adds a breath to every cycle. The gauge reads seventeen. It was fourteen when the surveyor went down. Three years rose while he was locked in the third gallery. He was the nearest living body."

Malrik's fingers tightened on the desk edge. "Renn. The surveyor. The works steward locked him in the third gallery."

"To keep him alive," Sol said. "And away from the pegs. She did not know the line would draw on him."

"She carried the years for forty years," Aldous said. He had not moved. His eyes stayed on the fire. "On the order of the old lord, your father. She wrote every second hand herself. She taught me my letters so someone would know the hand. She wrote my name on the witness line herself so a clerk would compare hands and ask who taught me. She wanted to be found. But not by the Council. Not by Thorne."

Malrik was silent for a long moment. The fire popped, a log shifting in the grate.

"She asks to be given to no one until the line is closed," Aldous added. "That was her condition."

"The works steward," Malrik repeated. "The carrying hand. Forty years." He looked at Kael, then at Sol. "You knew this before you came through my door."

"We had the leaf," Kael said. "We had the surveyor's map. We had the postscript. We put them together."

"And you brought the answer to the Chancellor before you brought it to me."

"The Chancellor asked a question," Sol said. "We answered it. The same way we answered the petition. Aloud. Together."

Malrik stood. The chair scraped back. He walked to the window, his hands clasped behind his back, the white hair bright against the dark coat. The morning light caught the plain silver clasp.

"Then we are done here," he said. "The Chancellor has her answer. The works steward has her condition. The heir has his witness line. The surveyor has his map." He turned. "The custodian's order does not care about any of it. Fourteen days. The pegs will be pulled on Day 29. The line will close. The stored years will go to the nearest living body."

"Sol will be at the plate," Kael said. "Odo and Tessa will pull the pegs. Renn's map will show where they sit."

"Renn's map," Malrik repeated. "The surveyor who was locked in the third gallery by the works steward. The surveyor whose hand resembles the forged line because the works steward taught him as a boy too." He paused. "A neat circle. Everyone taught by the same hand. Everyone carrying the same secret."

"Not everyone," Aldous said. "I was taught in secret. The tutor taught sums. The works steward taught letters. My grandfather knew neither."

Malrik's face hardened. "I knew the tutor. I paid the tutor. I did not know the works steward."

"She did not want you to know," Aldous said again. "She said the hand would be safer."

"Safer for whom?"

"For the line. For the years. For the boy who would grow up and speak against his house." Aldous met his grandfather's eyes. "For me."

The silence returned, heavier than before. Kael felt Sol's shoulder press against his, a point of warmth in the cold room. He did not look at her. He watched Malrik, the white hair, the straight spine, the plain silver clasp.

"Fourteen days," Malrik said. "The custodian's pick will be named on Day 26. The formal demand at the Lower Reach on Day 28. The first attempt on Day 29." He looked at Kael. "You will stand at the plate. The techs will pull the pegs. The line will close. And then we will see what the nearest living body receives."

"We will," Kael said.

Malrik nodded once, a sharp movement of the chin. "The door is behind you. The works steward will show you out."

They left the receiving room. The works steward waited in the corridor, her copper clasp dull in the passage light. She did not speak. She led them back through the portrait hallway, past the watching ancestors, to the front door.

The mist had lifted. The cobbles glistened. Kael set the stick on the stones, the ferrule biting into the gap between two flags, and felt the weight of it settle — open, honest, no longer hidden.

Sol stood beside him on the top step. The Thorne house loomed behind them, grey stone and dark windows. The ropewalk lines hummed in the wind, a sound like held breath.

She said one sentence.

"Aldous writes the way the leaf wrote."

Kael looked at her. Her face was calm, her eyes on the lane below. She did not name the hand. She did not name the carried years. She did not name the works steward who had taught a boy his letters in a still-room and written his name on a witness line and locked a surveyor in a gallery to keep him alive.

Neither did he.

The stick rested in his hand, the wood warm against his palm. The needles in his hip had faded to a dull throb. He took a breath, tasting wet stone and coal smoke and something sharper, mineral, rising from the cliff face where the boiling house lay buried.

"Fourteen days," he said.

"Fourteen," she agreed.

The lane stretched down toward the Lower Reach, the tar-shop, the stair where Ansa's loaf waited under its cloth. The day had begun. The count had been spoken. The answer had been sent.

Kael took the first step down. Sol fell into step beside him, on his left side, matching her stride to his without shortening it.

The stick did not catch on the first riser.

The stick did not catch on the first riser. Kael took the second step, then the third, the rhythm finding itself without his attention. The lane curved away from the Thorne front, narrowing between grey stone walls where moss grew in the mortar joints. Above them the ropewalk lines sagged in the damp air, silent now the morning wind had dropped.

Sol walked a half pace ahead, her undyed coat buttoned to the throat. He watched the back of her head, the dark hair pinned up, the line of her neck where the collar met skin. She had not put her gloves back on. Her hands hung loose at her sides, palms forward, the way they did when she was counting something she would not say aloud.

"Fourteen days," he said again, quieter this time. "The custodian's order. Sixteen gone before we saw it."

"Sixteen days the Council sat on it," she said. "Sixteen days the works steward carried the secret alone. She knew the gauge read fourteen when Renn went down. She knew it read seventeen when he was found. Three years taken from the nearest living body while he was locked in the dark."

"She locked him there to keep him alive."

"She locked him there and the line drank him anyway." Sol's voice stayed level. "That is the rule. Nearest living body pays. The line does not care about intent."

Kael's left hip throbhed a steady beat. He adjusted his grip on the stick, the ferrule striking stone. "The works steward. Forty years. She wrote every second hand. She taught Aldous his letters. She wrote his name on the witness line. She locked Renn in the third gallery. She asked to be given to no one until the line closes."

"She is the carrying hand," Sol said. "The leaf showed it. The carried hand leans right. Her hand leans right. Renn's hand leans right because she taught him as a boy. The forged witness hand is upright, closed tail, practised to look younger. Three hands from one teacher."

"Four, if you count Aldous."

"Four." She did not turn. "The tutor taught sums. The works steward taught letters. Malrik knew the tutor. He paid the tutor. He did not know the works steward."

"Forty years in his house. His father's order. He never knew."

"She did not want him to know." Sol's shoulder blades shifted under the coat, a small movement, almost a shrug. "She said the hand would be safer if no one knew where it came from. Safer for the line. For the years. For the boy who would grow up and speak against his house."

"For Aldous."

"For Aldous." The word hung between them. "He spoke against his house in the Council chamber. He asked leave to speak. He gave the witness line that broke the Deed. He did it with the hand she taught him."

The lane opened onto the wider cobbles of the Lower Reach. The tar-shop roof showed ahead, the ladder to Ansa's rooms, the hatch where they had stood the night before and felt the second line answer a flare. The ropewalk lines hummed low, a sound felt in the bones more than heard.

Kael looked at the stair that led up to Ansa's door. The lamp bracket on the second landing was still empty. The pale ring on the flagstone had not moved.

"Odo and Tessa," he said. "The techs. They pull the pegs on Day 29. Two pullers beyond the room's reach. One breath. The twin brass pegs."

"Renn's map will show where they sit," Sol said. "Ulla has the map. Renn's wife. She gave me the folded survey of the east, one cellar marked warm. She came to the research wing tonight. Before the Council. She knew where to find me."

"She said Renn would want you to have it."

"She said he would want someone to use it. Someone who knows how to count. Someone who knows how to wait."

Kael felt the corner of his mouth twitch. "You know how to wait. You made me wait an hour last night to hear your count."

"I made you keep a promise."

"You made me count the bell strikes. Three strikes. Three hours." He shook his head. "Eighteen years. You said eighteen. I said thirty-four. Sixteen between us."

"Sixteen and a half now," she corrected. "You paid half a year on the roof. The flare I tested. Six heartbeats. The cost jumped the gap. You took it."

"I stood at the parapet. Within arm's reach. The rule is the rule."

"The rule is the rule," she agreed. "But you stepped back when I told you to. Three steps. Outside the room's reach. You said yes before you argued. That was the echo. Ch.1 you asked. Ch.8 you do it unasked. Ch.31 you forget in panic and step in. Ch.43 I ask aloud and you say yes."

Kael stopped on the cobbles. The stick planted itself. "You are counting the echoes already."

"I am counting everything." She turned then, facing him. The morning light caught her face, the grey eyes, the mouth that had smiled at him in the lamplight and said *Ask me again in an hour.* "The flare test. The early beat. The gauge at seventeen. The works steward's forty years. Aldous's hand. Malrik's not-knowing. The custodian's order. Fourteen days. The pegs. The plate. The close. The stored years going to the nearest living body."

"Sol at the plate. Odo and Tessa pulling. Me standing back."

"You standing back." Her hand rose, fingers brushing the wool of his coat sleeve, the place where the ink had smudged. "That is the promise. No silent counts. Aloud and with her. Whoever the hand belongs to."

The words were the ones he had written on the Chancellor's slip. *I will do what I did in the petition. Aloud and with her, whoever the hand belongs to.*

He covered her hand with his, the square palm warm against her fingers. The needles in his hip faded behind the contact. "Aloud and with you."

"Every time."

"Every time."

She let her hand drop. The moment stretched, the ropewalk hum filling the space. Then she turned for the tar-shop stair.

"Ansa's loaf," she said over her shoulder. "The stair is harder on an empty stomach."

Kael followed. The stick moved cleanly now, the ferrule finding each riser without catching. He climbed on his right side, the left leg swinging from the hip, the needles a familiar companion. He did not hide the stick. He carried it openly, the wood warm against his palm, the ferrule biting stone.

Behind them the Thorne house stood grey and silent, its windows dark, its portraits watching. Ahead the tar-shop waited, the kettles cold, the loaf under its cloth. Fourteen days. The count spoken. The answer sent.

He set his foot on the first tread of Ansa's stair. The wood creaked under his weight. Sol was already two steps up, moving light and sure.

The day had begun. The line breathed twelve breaths to a cycle somewhere under the cliff. The gauge read seventeen. The pegs waited in their ledges, twin brass, same mold, one taken from the Tar Lane forge and one found on a table behind an iron door.

Kael climbed, and did not look back.
