The council meeting ran longer than usual. Yusuf sat at the head of the table in the room above the general store, his hands folded on the printed agenda, and let the discussion circle back to the same point for the third time. The valley's first harvest since the ring ritual had come in heavy and sweet. The apples had held. The water had run clear. No one had gotten sick in a way that couldn't be explained by ordinary means. But the paperwork had piled up faster than anyone expected.

"We have a record now," Walt Pruitt said, tapping his finger against the table. "A real one. Adelaide's letter. Whitlock's letter. The census pages Dev recovered. The transcripts from the ring. Wren's indexes. All of it sitting in boxes in the room behind the library because we don't have anyone whose job it is to keep it."

"We have Wren," someone said from the back.

"Wren has a warning project," Yusuf said mildly. "She has a map she's building. She has a list of every debt paid in this valley since the first settlement, and she's still finding names that weren't in the ledger. She doesn't have time to be an archivist too."

The room went quiet. Walt nodded once, satisfied.

"Marsh has been doing it," Odette Reyes said. She sat near the window, her hands folded over her purse. "Quietly. For months. He knows the system better than anyone. He built the cross-reference for the county index before he ever set foot here. He treated our mess like it was his own."

"He's not Kindred," a voice noted.

"He doesn't need to be," Mara Voss said. She leaned forward, her voice calm. "The archive isn't a gift. It's a practice. He's already proven he can do the work. The question is whether we want to keep him."

Yusuf looked around the table. Priya Nair sat with her notebook open, her pen still. Dev Nair leaned against the wall beside her, arms crossed, one knee bent. Caleb Reyes stood by the door, his expression unreadable. Denise, her hair white now and her face lined in ways that looked earned rather than stolen, sat with her hands in her lap.

"The offer stands," Yusuf said. "If he wants it. Permanent archivist for the Amity Falls Historical Archive. Salary from the town budget. Housing in the cottage behind the library. Full access to the restricted index. A seat at this table when the archive comes up."

He let the silence stretch.

"Does he know?" Priya asked.

"Not yet," Yusuf said. "I wanted the council's backing first. He's earned it. Whether he takes it is his own business."

Theo found the note on the kitchen table when he got back from the county seat. The drive had taken three hours each way. He had delivered the final copies of the Millbrook correspondence to the clerk's office, signed the receipt for the restricted file transfer, and stood in the parking lot for ten minutes with his hands on the steering wheel before he could make himself start the engine.

The note was written on a plain sheet of paper, folded in thirds. Yusuf's handwriting, blocky and precise.

*Theo. Council voted unanimous. Permanent archivist position. Cottage behind the library. Starts whenever you're ready. No pressure. Just the offer. - Y*

He read it twice. Three times. He set it down on the table and pressed his palms flat against the cool wood.

The cottage behind the library. He had walked past it a hundred times. Stone foundation, cedar shingles, a porch that caught the morning light. One room downstairs, a loft upstairs, a woodstove that threw heat like a heartbeat. He had imagined living there in the abstract, the way you imagine a life you don't expect to have.

His phone buzzed on the counter. A text from Wren: *Back from the ridge. The new markers are holding. Dinner at seven if you're here.*

He picked up the note again. Folded it carefully. Slipped it into his shirt pocket, over his heart.

*On my way,* he typed back.

The drive up the valley road felt different. The GPS on his dashboard still showed the turnoff as empty air, a glitch in the satellite data that no update had ever fixed. He knew the turn by heart now. The split-rail fence. The mailbox with the hand-painted numbers. The way the light fell through the trees at this hour, gold and slant.

He parked in the gravel beside the library. The building had been the town hall once, before the valley grew enough to need a separate room above the store. Now the ground floor held the public shelves, the reading table, the card catalog that Wren had reorganized three times in the past year. The back room held the archive. Acid-free boxes. Humidity monitors. A fire suppression system that Dev had wired himself. A desk with Theo's name on a piece of masking tape.

He climbed the porch steps. The front door was unlocked. Inside, the air smelled of paper and lemon oil and something floral from the garden Wren had planted along the south wall.

"Back room," she called from somewhere deep in the stacks.

He found her at the work table, a magnifying lamp clamped to the edge, a ledger open beneath it. Her hair was tied back with a clip. A smudge of graphite marked her cheekbone. She looked up when he appeared in the doorway, and something in her face shifted - softened, settled.

"Hey," she said. "How was the drive?"

"Long. The clerk in Millbrook wanted to verify the chain of custody on the Drake files three separate times."

"Did they?"

"Eventually." He set his bag down on the chair. "Yusuf left a note on the table."

Wren went still. The magnifying lamp hummed. "Oh."

"He offered me the archivist position. Permanent. Cottage behind the library. Starts whenever."

She didn't move. Her fingers rested on the edge of the ledger. "And?"

"And I told him yes in my head before I finished reading the first sentence."

A smile tugged at the corner of her mouth. "Good. That's good, Theo."

"Is it?" He crossed the room, stopped beside her chair. "Because it means staying. It means this is the place. The one place. No more driving back to Millbrook to check the county index. No more pretending I'm just consulting."

"You were never just consulting."

"I know." He exhaled. "I knew when I took the restricted file from the county clerk. I knew when I wrote the first cross-reference for your warning map. I knew when I sat in that council meeting and nobody asked me to leave."

Wren turned the ledger page. The motion was deliberate. "My grandmother used to say that belonging isn't a feeling. It's a decision you make every morning. You decide to stay. You decide to do the work. You decide that the people here are yours."

"Your grandmother sounds smart."

"She was terrifying. But yes. Smart." She looked up at him. "So. The cottage behind the library."

"The cottage behind the library."

"It has a leak in the roof over the loft. Dev says it's the flashing. He's been meaning to fix it for six months."

"I can fix flashing."

"Can you?"

"I can learn. I've learned worse things."

She laughed, quiet and real. "You have. You really have."

He reached out, meaning to touch her hand where it rested on the ledger. Stopped. Let his fingers hover.

"Wren," he said. "There's something else."

She went still again. Different this time. Alert.

"I didn't just say yes in my head because of the archive. I said yes because of you. Because this valley. Because the work we do together. Because I've spent nineteen years trying to find my grandmother's missing decade, and in one year here I've found more truth than I thought possible. Because you're the first person who's ever looked at my wounds and didn't flinch."

Her breath caught. Her fingers tightened on the ledger edge.

"Theo - "

"Let me finish. Please." He took a breath. "I love you. I have for a while. I think I knew it the night of the ring ritual, when you stood in that circle and chose to tell the truth anyway. I know it every time you hand me a file and trust me with it. I know it when you wake up at three in the morning and come down here to check the humidity readings because you can't sleep until you know the letters are safe."

"Theo, I - "

"I'm not asking you to fix anything. I'm not asking you to be anything other than what you are. I'm telling you that I want to build a life here. With you. In the cottage with the leaky flashing. In this room with the masking-tape desk. In whatever comes next, because whatever it is, I want to face it beside you."

He reached into his shirt pocket. Pulled out the folded note. Unfolded it. Turned it over.

On the back, in his own handwriting, written in the parking lot of the Millbrook clerk's office with a pen he'd borrowed from the receptionist:

*Wren Castellano. Finder. Keeper of the warning map. The only person who knows where every debt is buried. The only person I want to find me.*

He held it out to her.

She took it with trembling fingers. Read it once. Twice. Her eyes were wet when she looked up.

"You wrote this in a parking lot."

"I wrote it in a lot of places. In my head. In the margins of the census pages. In the space between heartbeats when you smile at me across the table."

She set the note down on the ledger. Slowly. Carefully. Like it was something fragile. Like it was something that mattered.

"I've been afraid," she said. "For a long time. That if I let myself want something, the bargain would take it. That if I loved someone, the debt would come for them. That's what happened to my father. That's what happened to Caleb. That's what almost happened to Dev."

"The bargain doesn't work that way anymore."

"I know that. Here." She tapped her chest. "But here..." She touched her throat. "Here it still whispers. Every time I use the gift. Every time I find something. Every time the gap opens up behind my eyes."

"I know." He stepped closer. "I know because you told me. You told me the truth about your fear. You trusted me with it. That's the only reason I'm standing here saying this."

"I don't want to be protected," she said fiercely. "Not from the truth. Not from the cost. Not from you."

"I would never protect you from the truth."

"I know. That's why I'm saying yes."

The word hung between them. Simple. Enormous.

"Yes?" he asked.

"Yes. To the cottage. To the life. To you. To all of it." She stood, the chair scraping back. She was shorter than him, but she didn't seem small. She seemed solid. Rooted. "Yes to building something that lasts. Yes to the archive and the warning map and the leaky flashing. Yes to mornings where we decide to stay."

He let out a breath he felt like he'd been holding for a year. Maybe for nineteen.

"Good," he said. "Good. That's - good."

She laughed, a wet sound. "Eloquent as always, Archivist Marsh."

"Archivist Marsh," he repeated. "Has a ring to it."

"Better than Consultant Marsh."

"Infinitely better."

She reached up, her fingers brushing his jaw. He leaned into the touch, his eyes closing. The contact was small. A few grams of pressure. Warmth. The ridge of her thumbnail against his stubble. But it carried the weight of every conversation in this room, every file passed across the table, every time she had handed him a truth he hadn't known he needed.

"I love you too," she said. "In case that wasn't clear. I love you, Theo Marsh. With the leaky flashing and the masking-tape desk and the three-hour drives to Millbrook. With the grandmother who can't remember a decade and the mother who stayed and the father who left. With all of it."

He opened his eyes. Her face was close. Her eyes were clear. No gaps. No shadows. Just her.

"All of it," he agreed.

She kissed him then. Not a question. Not a promise. A statement of fact. His hands found her waist, her hair, the back of her neck. The ledger pressed against his chest where she still held it. The magnifying lamp hummed. Somewhere outside, a car passed on the valley road, tires crunching gravel.

When they pulled apart, she rested her forehead against his.

"Dinner at seven," she murmured. "I made the thing with the tomatoes."

"The thing with the tomatoes is my favorite."

"I know. That's why I made it."

"You're dangerous, Wren Castellano."

"I'm a Finder. It's my job to be dangerous."

"Remind me to put that in the archive."

"Remind me to put *you* in the archive."

"I'm already there. Cross-referenced and everything."

She pulled back enough to look at him. Her smile was crooked. Real. The kind that reached her eyes.

"Good," she said. "Then you're stuck with me."

"I'm counting on it."

They stood there in the archive room, surrounded by the valley's truth in acid-free boxes, the hum of the lamp, the weight of the note on the ledger. The cottage behind the library waited with its leaky flashing. The warning map waited on the ridge. The council waited for the next meeting. The harvest waited in the orchard bins.

But in this moment, in this room, the work could wait. The debts could wait. The bargain, whatever remained of it, could wait.

They had chosen each other. They had chosen to stay.

That was the only record that mattered.
