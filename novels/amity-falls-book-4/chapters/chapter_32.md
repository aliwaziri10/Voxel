The kitchen table held the shape of their work. Index cards in neat rows. Photocopied pages from the county archive. Adelaide's letter in its protective sleeve. Whitlock's letter beside it, the ink faded to brown at the edges. Wren had stopped touching any of it three hours ago. She sat with her hands folded in her lap, watching the afternoon light move across the floorboards.

Theo stood at the counter, pouring coffee into a mug that had once belonged to Wren's grandmother. The ceramic was chipped at the handle. He had memorized the chip. He had memorized the way Wren tapped her index finger against the table when she was reading something she disagreed with. He had memorized the rhythm of her breathing when she fell asleep in the chair by the window.

He set the mug down on a coaster and turned toward the table.

"Wren."

She did not look up. Her eyes stayed fixed on a spot in the grain of the wood.

"We need to talk about the timeline."

"No."

"Wren, please. The draft you sent to Yusuf. The sections on the bargain's administrative terms. You left out the Millbrook connection entirely."

"I didn't leave it out. I placed it in the appendix. Where it belongs."

"It belongs in the narrative. Drake's first attempt was in Millbrook. Nineteen years ago. That is not an appendix detail. That is the through line."

"The through line is the valley. The bargain. The people who live here. Millbrook is a footnote in a story that isn't theirs."

"Theo's grandmother lost ten years of memory in that footnote."

Wren's head snapped up. Her face was pale, her jaw tight.

"Do not," she said, very quiet. "Do not bring Harriet into this to win an argument."

"I'm not trying to win. I'm trying to publish a record that will hold up when Drake releases his version. Which he will. Soon. And his version will lead with Millbrook because it proves the bargain's reach extends beyond the valley. It proves the pattern. If we bury it in an appendix, we look like we're hiding something. And we are."

"We are protecting something. There's a difference."

"The difference is whether the truth survives contact with the world outside this valley."

The silence stretched. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere outside, a screen door slammed.

Wren stood. Her chair scraped the floor. "I'm done. I'm not doing this anymore."

"The work isn't done."

"My part of it is. You have the files. You have the letters. You have the index. You know the county archive better than I do. Finish it."

"And put my name on the cover alone? After everything?"

"There is no cover. There's a digital archive and a printed binder for the council records. My name doesn't need to be on either one."

"You know that's not what I meant."

"I know exactly what you meant. You want the story told your way. The journalist's way. The investigator's way. You want every thread pulled until the whole fabric comes apart. And you call it truth."

"I call it accountability. You call it protection. We've been over this. Three times this week. Four times last week."

"Because you won't listen. You hear what you want to hear. You decide what the valley needs before you've asked a single person who lives here."

"I asked you. I asked Priya. I asked Yusuf. I asked Denise. Every single one of you told me something different. The only person who hasn't given me a straight answer is you."

Wren laughed. A short, sharp sound. "I've given you every answer I have. You just don't like them."

"Because they're not answers. They're deflections. You talk about the valley like it's a living thing that will break if you look at it wrong. It's not. It's a place. With people. And history. And a bargain that has been renewed in secret for fifty years by a woman who is still alive and sitting on the council. That is not a living thing. That is a story that has been kept from the people it affects. Including my grandmother."

"Harriet Marsh is not your excuse."

"She is my reason. Every day for nineteen years I have watched her stare at photographs she cannot place. She holds her own wedding album and does not recognize the man beside her. She cannot tell me where she was for a decade. And the man who took it from her is the same man who has spent the last year assembling a weapon out of this valley's secrets. You think I don't know what's at stake? You think I'm doing this for a byline?"

"I think you're doing it because you can't stop. Because the work is the only way you know how to love anyone. You dig until you find the bottom. And then you dig more. And you call it justice."

Theo went still. The mug forgotten. The counter forgotten. The afternoon light caught the side of his face, the line of his jaw, the crease between his eyebrows.

"That's not fair."

"Fair has nothing to do with it. You asked me to work with you. I did. I gave you everything I knew. I gave you access I wasn't sure I had the right to give. And every time we reached a choice point, you chose the version of the story that makes the valley look like a cautionary tale. Because that's the version that travels. That's the version that gets published in the county record. That's the version that makes sense to people who have never stood in this kitchen and watched Odette Reyes smile while she tells you she doesn't remember the words her husband used to propose but she remembers the joy."

"The joy is not the whole story, Wren. The missing words are part of it too. The cost is part of it. You want to publish the joy and call it the truth. I want to publish the cost and call it the truth. Neither of us is wrong. But only one of us is willing to let the other version exist alongside ours."

"Because your version destroys things. My version lets people keep living."

"My version lets people make informed choices. Your version makes the choices for them."

The front door opened. Heavy boots on the porch. The screen door slapped shut.

Priya Nair entered the kitchen carrying a cardboard box. She wore her deputy uniform. Her hair was pulled back. Her face was set in the expression she wore when she was about to deliver news no one wanted to hear.

She stopped in the doorway. Looked from Wren to Theo. Back to Wren.

"Am I interrupting?"

"No," Wren said. "We were just finishing."

"Good." Priya set the box on the counter beside Theo's mug. "Because I drove twenty minutes from the substation with the evidence logs Dev compiled, and I'm not leaving until both of you tell me what the hell happened to the publication schedule."

Theo exhaled. "There isn't a schedule. Not anymore."

"Yusuf called me. He said the draft was supposed to go to the printer Friday. Today is Wednesday. He said you two were 'finalizing details.'"

"We were," Theo said. "We finalized that we can't agree on the framing."

Priya's eyes moved between them. "Then un-finalize it. I drove twenty minutes for this."

"It's not that simple," Wren said. "Theo wants the Millbrook connection in the main narrative. I want it in the appendix. He says I'm hiding something. I say he's exposing something that isn't his to expose. We've had this conversation five times."

"Because neither of you is actually listening," Priya said. She pulled out a chair and sat down. The box sat between her hands. "Theo. Talk to me like I'm not your colleague. Talk to me like I'm your friend. Why does the Millbrook connection have to be in the main narrative?"

Theo was quiet. His hands flattened on the table.

"Because Drake's advantage comes from the pattern. Nineteen years ago he tried the same thing in Millbrook. Failed. Spent the next nineteen years building a case file on this valley. If we lead with that, we show the pattern before he can weaponize it. We control the frame. We say: here is a man who targets communities with bargains. Here is what he did in Millbrook. Here is what he tried to do here. Here is why he failed both times. That is a story about accountability. About a predator. Not about a valley that has something to hide."

"And if the Millbrook story brings people here?" Wren said. "Reporters. Curiosity seekers. People who want to see the town that trades memory for protection? What happens to Odette? To Denise? To Walt? To my mother? You've seen what happens when the outside world pays attention. You were the outside world paying attention."

"I was. And I stayed. And I'm still here."

"For how long? Until the story is published? Until the archive opens? Until you've satisfied whatever it is you're trying to satisfy in yourself?"

Theo's face went hard. "That's low."

"It's honest."

"Honest would be admitting you're terrified. Not of what the story says. Of what happens after. You're terrified that if the valley is known, it stops being yours. You're terrified that the protection the bargain buys is the only thing keeping you safe. And if people know the cost, they'll decide it's not worth it. And then what happens to you? What happens to your gift? What happens to the thing that makes you a Finder?"

Wren flinched. A small movement. Her shoulders drawing in.

Priya leaned forward. "Theo. Stop."

"No. She asked for honest. That's honest. She uses her gift. She pays small debts. Notes in drawers. Thank-yous that puzzle her. She protects the valley because the valley protects her from ever having to find out what a real cost feels like."

"I know what it costs," Wren said. Her voice shook. "I know because I watched Caleb lose two years. I watched Denise carry fifty years alone. I watched Dev serve a sentence for redirecting a toll he didn't choose. I know the cost. I just don't think the cost is a story you get to tell for other people."

"Then who tells it? The people who paid it? Half of them can't remember. The other half are too tired. You want to be the gatekeeper? Fine. But gatekeepers don't get to call it publishing. They call it curating. And curating is what got us into this mess in the first place. Ambrose Whitlock curated. He hid the terms. He hid his failure. He left a letter his family buried for generations. Adelaide curated. She renewed the bargain in secret for fifty years. She took Caleb's memories. She took Harriet Marsh's memories. Curation is the problem. Not the solution."

"Adelaide is not - "

"Adelaide is exactly the point. She made choices for everyone. She decided what the valley could bear. She decided what the bargain required. And she was wrong. Not evil. Wrong. And the only reason we know she was wrong is because someone finally told the truth. Not the curated version. The truth."

"The truth as you define it."

"The truth as documented. As evidenced. As witnessed. Dev's evidence work identified the exact gap in Drake's account. That evidence collapsed Drake's advantage. Not a curated narrative. Evidence. Facts. The same standard I'm asking you to meet."

Wren was shaking. Her hands clenched at her sides.

"You want facts," she said. "Here's a fact. My grandmother was a Finder. She paid debts. Large ones. She never told me what they were. She died before she could. My father left when I was six. He didn't leave because of the bargain. He left because he couldn't live in a place where the truth was negotiable. My mother stayed. She raised me here. She protected me from the things she thought would break me. And she was wrong too. But she did it out of love. Not control. Love. And every time you talk about the valley like it's a case file, like it's a puzzle to be solved, you sound like him. You sound like the man who left because he couldn't stand the not-knowing."

The words hung in the air. Theo's face went white. Then red. His jaw worked. He didn't answer right away.

Priya's voice cut into the silence before either of them could say something they couldn't take back.

"Both of you, stop." She stood, the box still in her hands. "You've been at this for three hours and you're circling the same six sentences. Theo, go stand on the porch for five minutes. Wren, drink some water. I'm not asking. I have evidence to show you both, and I'm not showing it to two people who are about to say something unforgivable over an argument you're going to have to settle anyway."

Theo looked at her, then at Wren. Something in his shoulders dropped, the fight going out of him the way it does when exhaustion finally outruns anger. He grabbed his jacket off the hook, not to leave the house but because the porch was cold, and pushed through the screen door without another word. It swung shut softly behind him, not slammed.

Wren sank into her chair. Her face went into her hands, just for a moment.

Priya waited. Counted breaths. Let the silence do its work.

When Wren looked up, her eyes were dry. "Open the box," she said.

Priya lifted the flaps. "Dev's evidence logs. The gap in Drake's account. The toll redirection records from the substation. Everything Dev compiled to collapse Drake's advantage. Yusuf asked me to bring them to you. He said the council needs to see the full picture before Friday."

Wren reached for the top file. Her fingers trembled. She steadied them. Opened the folder.

Pages of numbers. Dates. Names. Cross-referenced. Annotated in Dev's precise hand.

"The gap is precise," Priya said. "Drake's account claims the bargain was renewed continuously from 1972 to 2022. Dev found the substation records. Power draw spikes on the renewal dates. But there's a three-month window in 2022 where the draw flatlines. No spike. No renewal. Drake's account says the renewal happened March 14, 2022. The substation says it didn't."

"Denise," Wren whispered. "She was freed at the split-weight ritual. March 2022. The renewal didn't happen because she stopped doing it."

"Exactly. And the ring ritual distributed the weight. Ten volunteers. Dev was one. His Finder gift located the debt. The others carried it. The substation recorded the distribution as a sustained draw over seven days. Not a single spike. A sustained draw. That's the evidence. That's what breaks Drake's advantage. He claims the bargain is a continuous administrative record. The substation proves it's a living thing. It can stop. It can be shared. It can be ended."

Wren turned the page. More numbers. A diagram of the ring. Ten names. Dev's name highlighted.

"Dev did this," she said. "He built this case. He served his sentence. He came back and he built the case that saves us all."

"Dev did this," Priya agreed. "And he did it because you asked him to. Because you trusted him with the truth. Because you didn't curate. You gave him the files. All of them. And he found the one thing that mattered."

Wren's fingers traced Dev's name. "He's a Whitlock descendant. The bargain-ending practice runs in his blood. He didn't know. None of us knew. Until the letter."

"Ambrose Whitlock's letter. Not Ambrose Kell's. Two different men. Two different failures. Whitlock tried to end the bargain and failed. He hid the terms instead of destroying them. Kell exploited the hidden terms. Dev carries both bloodlines. He ended what Whitlock couldn't. He exposed what Kell buried."

"The ring ritual. Ten volunteers. Dev located the debt. The others carried it. Denise was freed. Caleb's memories returned. The bargain held."

"Because the bargain isn't a contract. It's a relationship. Between the valley and whatever lies beneath it. The terms are administered by people. People who choose. People who pay. People who can stop."

Wren closed the folder. Her hands rested on the cover.

"Priya. I don't know how to do this with him. Not like this."

"Then don't do it like this." Priya nodded toward the porch, where Theo's shape was visible through the window, hands braced on the railing, staring out at the darkening orchard. "He's not gone. He's twenty feet away, freezing, because he doesn't know how to walk away from you any more than you know how to walk away from him. That's not nothing."

"He said I sound like my father."

"You said it. Not him. He didn't get the chance to answer before I told you both to stop." Priya's voice was gentler now. "For what it's worth - I don't think either of you is wrong about the Millbrook question. I think you're both right, which is worse, because there's no clean answer to hand you. But I don't think tonight, exhausted, three hours into the same fight, is when you're going to find it either."

"Then when?"

"Tomorrow. When you've both slept. When you're not doing this on no food and cold coffee." Priya stood, tucking the folders back into the box. "I'll leave this with you. Read it tonight if you want, or don't. Either way, you have until Friday, not tonight, to decide how the Millbrook material goes in. That's more time than you're giving yourselves."

Wren looked toward the window. Theo hadn't moved. "And if we can't agree by Friday?"

"Then you'll have had a real conversation instead of the same five sentences a sixth time. I'd put money on that being enough." Priya picked up the box. Paused at the door. "Wren. He's not your father. He came here and he stayed. Sitting on a cold porch instead of driving to Millbrook is not nothing. Go talk to him. Not to win. Just to talk."

The screen door clicked shut, softer this time. Priya's boots receded down the steps. The car engine started. Tires on gravel, fading.

Wren sat alone at the table for a long moment, the folder of Dev's evidence closed beneath her hands. Through the window, Theo's silhouette hadn't moved from the railing.

She stood. She crossed the kitchen. She opened the screen door and stepped out into the cold.

He didn't turn around right away. When he did, his face held none of the hardness from an hour ago - just tired, and cold, and unsure of his welcome.

"I'm not leaving," he said quietly, before she could speak. "I know I said - I know it sounded like I was about to. I'm not. I don't know how to fix this tonight. But I'm not leaving."

"I know," Wren said. And found, standing there in the cold with him, that she did.

Neither of them solved anything that night. But they stood on the porch together until the cold drove them back inside, and the argument waited, unresolved, for morning.
