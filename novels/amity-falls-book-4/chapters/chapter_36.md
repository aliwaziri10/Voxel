The kitchen table held three coffee mugs and a stack of printed pages, corners softened from shuffling and re-shuffling. Morning light cut across the scratched surface at a slant that made dust motes visible, each one catching and releasing as Wren turned a page.

Theo sat across from her, his copy open to the same section. He hadn't touched his coffee in twenty minutes. The steam had stopped rising.

"You want me to answer it now," he said.

"I want you to answer it honestly." Wren kept her voice level. She had practiced the question in the shower, on the walk from the orchard, standing at the sink washing a single spoon. "If the county clerk's office asked for the full record tomorrow - not the redacted version, not the summary Yusuf approved for the public meeting - the whole thing, every name, every debt paid, every memory taken - would you hand it over?"

Theo didn't look up. His fingers rested on the table beside the manuscript, nails trimmed short, knuckles pale.

"Would you?" she asked.

He exhaled. The sound was quiet in the kitchen, swallowed by the refrigerator's hum. "You're asking if I'd violate the agreement we made with Yusuf. With the council. With every person in this valley who trusted us with their stories."

"I'm asking if you'd violate it," she said. "Not if you should. Not if it's legal. If the request came through proper channels, with a subpoena or a court order or whatever piece of paper makes it official - would you give them what they asked for?"

Theo looked up then. His eyes were the color of river water in late autumn, gray-green and depthless. Circles beneath them hadn't been there three weeks ago, before the first draft of Drake's manuscript arrived in a plain envelope with no return address.

"That's not a fair question," he said.

"Then don't answer it like a fair question. Answer it like the one I actually asked."

He was silent long enough that Wren heard the orchard sprinklers kick on outside, the hiss and thunk of water hitting aluminum pipe. Caleb's system, upgraded last spring. She had helped him test the pressure valves.

"My grandmother," Theo said finally. "Harriet. She lost ten years. The decade before 2007. She was thirty-seven when it happened. Forty-seven when she came back to herself. She doesn't know where she was. She doesn't know who she talked to. She doesn't know if she signed something, if she said something, if she *did* something. Nineteen years I've been looking. Nineteen years of FOIA requests and court petitions and quiet conversations with clerks who remembered her name. And every time - every single time - someone decided what she was allowed to know. Someone decided the truth was too dangerous. Too disruptive. Too *inconvenient*."

His voice dropped, roughened at the edges.

"So when you ask me if I'd hand over the full record to a government office - you're asking me if I'd do to this valley what was done to my family. You're asking me if I'd be the person who decides someone else doesn't get to know their own life."

Wren's throat tightened. She had known the shape of his wound. She hadn't known the weight of it pressing against this moment.

"I'm not asking you to be that person," she said. "I'm asking if you *are* that person. When the pressure comes. When the law says you have to choose."

Theo's jaw worked. "The law doesn't say I have to choose. The law says I have to comply. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes. There is." He stood abruptly, the chair legs scraping the floor. He moved to the window, shoulders rigid beneath his flannel shirt. "I've spent my adult life learning how systems protect themselves. How they bury things. How they rename suppression 'procedure' and call it justice. I know exactly what happens when a county clerk's office gets a complete record of something they don't understand. They don't study it. They don't preserve it. They classify it. They restrict it. They make it disappear into a drawer that nobody's allowed to open - not the people it belongs to, not the researchers, not even the judges half the time. So no. I would not hand it over. Not voluntarily. Not if I had any choice at all."

Wren nodded once. The motion felt small, insufficient. "And if you didn't have a choice?"

Theo turned. His face was drawn, the lines around his mouth deeper than she remembered from the cataloguing sessions in August, from the evenings on the porch with Caleb's cider and Mara's terrible cookies. "Then I'd make sure the version they got was the one *we* wrote. The one with context. The one that explains what a debt *is* before it lists who paid it. The one that says this valley isn't a case study or a cautionary tale or a supernatural anomaly - it's a community. People who love each other. People who make sacrifices for each other. People who *chose* this, even the ones who didn't know they were choosing."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have." He came back to the table but didn't sit. He braced his hands on the back of his chair. "Wren, what do you want me to say? That I'll burn the manuscript before I let anyone see it? That I'll lie under oath? That I'll - what, flee to another state with the only copy in my trunk? I'm an archivist. My whole profession is built on the premise that records survive. That truth outlasts the people who try to bury it. But I also know that *how* truth survives matters. *Who* tells it matters. *When* it's told matters."

"Drake is telling it," she said. "Right now. His version. Partial. Damning. Missing the parts that make it human."

"I know."

"He's going to publish in three weeks. His publisher has the galley proofs. The marketing team has the advance copy blurbs. 'The Secret History of Amity Falls: Memory, Power, and the Town That Forgot Itself.' That's the working title. His agent told a reporter at the *County Gazette* it's the most important regional exposé in twenty years."

Theo's expression didn't change. "I know."

"So we publish first. Our version. Complete. Honest. With every name and every debt and every memory - *except* the ones Yusuf and the council said stay redacted. The ones that belong to people who are still alive and didn't consent to public exposure. We give the world the truth *with* the context. We give the valley the protection *of* the context."

"That's the plan. That's been the plan since September."

"Then why does it feel like we're arguing about something else?"

Because they were. She knew it. He knew it. The question about consent had never been about a hypothetical subpoena. It had been about the night two weeks ago when she found him in the archive room at 2 a.m., the restricted files spread across the reading table, his phone recording page after page of the unredacted manuscript - the version only she and Yusuf and Priya were supposed to access. He had said he was checking cross-references. He had said he needed to verify the timeline on the 1987 renewal. He had said a lot of things.

She hadn't said anything. She had walked out. She hadn't come back to the archive for three days.

"You took pictures," she said now. "Of the unredacted pages. The ones with Odette's proposal memory. The ones with Denise's renewal records. The ones with my grandmother's name in the Finder ledger from 1972. You took pictures of things you promised you wouldn't photograph."

Theo went very still. "I took notes. For the footnotes. For the appendix on methodology. So the record shows *how* we reached our conclusions."

"Notes don't have metadata. Notes don't have timestamps and geotags. Notes don't get uploaded to a cloud backup you forgot to disable." Her voice cracked on the last word. She steadied it. "I checked your phone, Theo. When you left it on the charger in the kitchen. I didn't mean to. The screen lit up with a sync notification. Google Photos. Two hundred forty-seven images. Dated October 14th. 2:13 a.m. to 3:47 a.m."

The silence stretched. The sprinklers cycled off outside. Somewhere in the house, a floorboard settled.

"I didn't upload them," he said quietly. "The backup is automatic. I turned it off the next morning. I deleted the cloud copies. I deleted the local copies. The only version that exists now is the one on the secure drive in my bag - the one encrypted to the same standard the county uses for witness protection files. I *told* you I was verifying the timeline. I *did* verify the timeline. The 1987 renewal has a discrepancy in the witness signatures that changes the chain of custody for the memory ledger. It matters for the appendix. It matters for the historical record."

"It matters for *your* record," she said. "The one you're building in your head. The one where you're the person who finally gets it right. The one where you make sure no grandmother ever loses ten years without someone being held accountable."

"That's not fair."

"Neither was taking pictures of Odette's proposal memory. She lost the *words*, Theo. Forty-three years ago. She kept the joy. She told us that. She *trusted* us with that. And you photographed the page that says exactly which memory was taken and exactly when and exactly who authorized it - *me*. My signature. My handwriting. Because I was the Finder on duty that week. Because I was the one who located the memory in the archive. Because I was the one who handed it to the Warden."

Theo's face went pale. "Wren - "

"I was nineteen. I didn't know what I was signing. I thought it was a routine retrieval. I thought the memory was *lost* - a child's toy, a wedding ring, a set of keys. That's what the request form said. 'Locate missing item: proposal memory, Odette Reyes, 1979.' I found it. I brought it. I signed the release. I didn't ask why a proposal memory needed to be located. I didn't ask who was asking. I didn't ask *why*." She stopped, breathing hard. "That page - the one you photographed - it has my name on it. My signature. The date. The notation: *debt satisfied per standing arrangement.* That's *my* debt. A small one. A note in a drawer. A thank-you that puzzled me. I didn't even remember paying it until I saw it in the ledger last year. And you - you took a picture of it. For your appendix. For your methodology. For *your* truth."

"I didn't know," he said. "I didn't connect - I saw the discrepancy in the 1987 witness signatures. I was following the thread. I wasn't looking for your name. I wasn't looking for *anyone's* name. I was looking for the gap in Drake's account. The place where his partial record falls apart. Dev found it in the substation evidence. The exact gap. The missing renewal. The one that proves Drake doesn't have the complete terms. I needed to document *why* that gap exists. How the record-keeping failed. How the standing arrangement notation was used to hide - "

"To hide *me*," she said. "To hide what I did. What I didn't know I did. You photographed my ignorance, Theo. You photographed my complicity. And you didn't tell me. You didn't ask. You just - took it. Because the record matters more than the person."

"That's not true."

"Then why didn't you tell me? Why didn't you come to me *before* you opened the restricted files? Why didn't you say, 'Wren, I found something with your name on it, I need to understand it, can we look together?' Why did you wait until 2 a.m. when nobody was watching?"

His hands gripped the chair back until the knuckles whitened. "Because I was afraid. Because every time I get close to something that matters - something that explains what happened to my grandmother, something that might *help* - someone decides I don't need to know. Someone decides the truth is too dangerous. Someone decides *for* me. And I swore - I *swore* - that I would never be that person. That I would never withhold. That I would never protect someone by lying to them. So when I found the discrepancy, when I saw the thread pulling toward something real, I followed it. I followed it the way I've followed every thread for nineteen years. Alone. In the dark. Because that's the only way I know how to be sure nobody's hiding anything from me."

Wren stood. Her chair scraped back. She moved to the counter, poured the cold coffee down the sink, ran the water until it ran clear. Her hands shook. She gripped the counter edge.

"You hid it from me," she said to the running water. "You hid what you were doing. You hid what you found. You hid the pictures. That's not following a thread. That's *burying* it. That's exactly what you swore you wouldn't do."

The water shut off. Theo didn't answer.

She turned. He was still by the chair, shoulders hunched, the morning light catching the silver at his temples. He looked tired. He looked like he had looked the first time she met him, standing at the county index terminal with his careful notes and his careful questions, asking for a record that didn't exist.

"I'm not my grandmother," she said. "And you're not the clerk who denied her. And this valley isn't Millbrook. But you acted like it was. You acted like the only way to get the truth was to take it - to take *from* the people who trusted you with it. And that - that's the thing I can't get past. Not the pictures. Not the restricted files. The *taking*. The assumption that your need for the complete record outweighs my right to know you're looking at my name in a ledger I didn't know existed."

"I didn't - " He stopped. Swallowed. "I didn't assume that. I was *terrified* of that. I was terrified that if I asked, you'd say no. That Yusuf would say no. That the council would say no. That someone would decide the discrepancy wasn't relevant, or wasn't safe, or wasn't *necessary* - and I would lose the only chance to prove Drake wrong before he publishes. I was terrified of losing the truth *again*."

"And I was terrified of losing you," she said. "To the truth. To the version of you that decides the record matters more than the people in it. I've been terrified of that since the first week. Since you asked for the standing arrangement notation and I didn't know if I was allowed to give it to you. Since I realized you'd keep asking until someone stopped you - or until you stopped yourself. And you didn't stop. You *took*."

The word hung between them. Heavy. Final.

Theo's breath came shallow. "What do you want from me, Wren?"

The question landed in the space where her anger had been, and something shifted. Cracked open. The rage she had been carrying for two weeks - the betrayal, the violation, the cold knot in her chest every time she saw his phone on the charger - it didn't vanish. But it made room for something else.

She knew this feeling. She had felt it when her father left. When her grandmother died. When Caleb's memory returned and she realized how much she had been protected from. The feeling of naming a loss while it was still happening.

"I want you to ask," she said. "Next time. The time after that. Every time. I want you to come to me and say, 'Wren, I found something. It involves you. Can we look at it together?' I want you to trust that I can handle the truth about myself - even the ugly parts, even the parts I didn't choose. I want you to trust that *I get to decide* what happens to my name in your record. Not you. Not the county. Not Drake. *Me*."

Theo was silent. His throat moved.

"And I want you to tell me," she continued, her voice gaining strength, "that you understand why the redactions exist. Not why they're convenient. Not why they're strategic. Why they *exist*. Because every redacted name in that manuscript is a person who didn't consent to be a case study. Odette. Denise. My grandmother. Dev. Priya. My *mother*. They trusted us with their memories. They trusted *me* with their memories. And if you hand the unredacted version to a county clerk - or a publisher, or a journalist, or anyone with a subpoena and a budget - you're not just releasing information. You're releasing *them*. You're saying their consent doesn't matter because the record matters more."

"I know that," he said. "I *know* that. Wren - I didn't take the pictures to publish them. I didn't take them to give to Drake. I didn't take them to give to *anyone*. I took them because I needed to *see* the complete picture. To understand how the pieces fit. To make sure our version - the one we publish - is *stronger* than his. More complete. More honest. More *defensible*. I thought - I thought if I had the full context, I could build the argument that protects everyone. That the redactions aren't gaps in the truth, they're *part* of the truth. The part that says: this belongs to the people who lived it. Not to the archive. Not to the public. Not to history."

"Then say it," she said. "Say it out loud. Say: 'The redactions are part of the truth. The consent is part of the record. I will not violate them.' Say it like you mean it. Like it's not a negotiation."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. His eyes held hers, gray-green and raw.

"The redactions are part of the truth," he said. "The consent is part of the record. I will not violate them."

"Again."

"The redactions are part of the truth. The consent is part of the record. I will not violate them."

"One more time. Looking at me."

He didn't look away. "The redactions are part of the truth. The consent is part of the record. I will not violate them. I'm sorry, Wren. I'm sorry I took the pictures. I'm sorry I didn't tell you. I'm sorry I made you find out this way. I'm sorry I acted like my fear justified betraying your trust. It didn't. It doesn't. I was wrong."

The words landed in the kitchen, one after another, each one heavier than the last. Wren felt something in her chest loosen - not heal, not fix, but *loosen*, like a knot worked free by patient fingers.

"I believe you," she said. "I believe you're sorry. I believe you understand. But - " She stopped. Swallowed. "But I also need you to understand that sorry doesn't undo it. The pictures existed. The cloud backup happened. The metadata exists somewhere on Google's servers, probably forever. You can't un-take them. You can't un-know what you saw. And I can't un-know that you chose to take them instead of asking me."

"I know."

"And I need you to know - " Her voice cracked. She pressed on. "I need you to know that I *want* this. Us. The work. The publication. The life where you're in my kitchen arguing about archival methodology at 7 a.m. and I'm making terrible coffee and you're drinking it anyway. I want that. I've wanted it since September. Since the first cataloguing session when you asked me to explain the Finder cost structure and you *listened* - really listened, like it mattered, like *I* mattered. I want it so much it scares me. And that's why this hurts. Because I can lose it. I can lose *you*. To the truth. To the record. To the version of you that decides the archive is worth more than the person standing in front of it."

Theo moved then. Two steps. Three. He stopped close enough that she could see the individual lashes framing his eyes, the faint scar on his chin from a childhood bicycle accident he had mentioned once, the pulse jumping in his neck.

"I'm not going anywhere," he said. "Not to the county. Not to a publisher. Not to Drake. Not to any version of the truth that requires me to leave you behind. I - " He hesitated. "I've been building a case in my head for nineteen years. A case against the people who took my grandmother's memory. A case that says: *here is the evidence, here is the timeline, here is the name of the person responsible, here is the proof that they knew and they did it anyway.* And I thought - I *thought* - that if I just built it carefully enough, thoroughly enough, *honestly* enough, it would matter. It would fix something. It would give her back what she lost. But it doesn't work that way. The case doesn't give her back the decade. The record doesn't give Odette back the words. The publication doesn't give Denise back the fifty years. The truth *doesn't fix it*. It just - it just *is*. And the only thing that makes it bearable is the people who stay. The people who *choose* to stay. You. This valley. This kitchen. This terrible coffee."

He reached out, stopped, let his hand fall.

"Can I - " He started again. "Can I stay? Not in the valley. *Here*. With you. In whatever this is. The arguing and the cataloguing and the terrible coffee and the fear. Can I stay in it? Even if you're not sure you can trust me yet. Even if it takes a long time. Even if - "

"Yes," she said.

The word came out faster than she intended. Simpler. Truer.

"Yes," she repeated. "You can stay. I'm not - I'm not ready to say it's fine. It's not fine. It might not be fine for a long time. But I'm not letting go. Not of this. Not of you. Not unless you decide to go."

"I'm not deciding that."

"Good."

They stood in the kitchen, two people who had spent a year learning each other's wounds and a week tearing them open again. The morning light had shifted, warmer now, catching the dust motes in new angles.

"We have a manuscript to finish," Wren said. "The redacted version. The one Yusuf approved. The one we send to the publisher by Friday if we want to beat Drake's release date."

"Three days," Theo said. "We can do three days."

"We can do three days. But - " She met his eyes. "We do it *together*. Every decision. Every redaction. Every footnote. Every comma. You don't open the restricted files without me. You don't photograph anything without me. You don't *decide* what the record says without me. We write it together. Or we don't write it at all."

"Together," he agreed. "Every decision. Every comma."

"And the appendix on methodology - "

"Stays vague on the 1987 discrepancy. Notes that the witness signature chain has irregularities without specifying the nature. References the substation evidence Dev compiled without naming the gap. Protects the people. Protects *you*."

Wren nodded. "Good."

"Good."

He picked up his coffee mug. The coffee was cold. He drank it anyway, making a face.

"Terrible," he said.

"The worst," she agreed. "Mara's recipe. She swears it's an old family secret."

"Your family has terrible taste in coffee."

"My family has excellent taste in *everything else*."

He smiled then. Small. Real. The first real smile she had seen in two weeks.

"Yeah," he said. "They do."

She picked up her own mug, blew on the cold liquid, drank. It tasted like burnt beans and forgiveness and the terrifying, necessary possibility of tomorrow.

"Three days," she said. "Let's get to work."
