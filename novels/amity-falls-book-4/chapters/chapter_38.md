The certified letter arrived on a Tuesday, slipped under the Archive door while Wren cross-referenced Adelaide's correspondence against the town clerk's death registers. She knew the law firm's letterhead before she broke the seal. Millbrook County. The same jurisdiction where Theo had built his career chasing ghosts in the record room.

She read it twice. Then she walked to the front desk where Theo photographed a brittle ledger page, his camera mounted on a copy stand, and set the paper beside his elbow without a word.

Theo didn't look up right away. He adjusted the lighting, snapped the shutter, checked the display. Only then did he pick up the letter, unfold it, and read. His expression barely shifted - a tightening at the jaw, a slight narrowing of eyes that meant he was filing something away for later.

"Injunction," he said. "Filed in Millbrook Superior. Drake's counsel claims proprietary ownership of the compiled research materials - his phrase - and seeks to enjoin us from publishing any derivative work."

"Derivative work." Wren's voice came out flatter than she intended. "He's calling the valley's history his intellectual property."

"He's calling *his version* of it his intellectual property. The compilation, the analysis, the narrative structure. He's not wrong that he assembled it. He's wrong about what that gives him the right to do with it."

"And the judge?"

"Judge Harriet Ellery. Appointed twelve years ago. Reputation for being thorough, skeptical of sweeping claims, allergic to procedural gamesmanship." Theo refolded the letter, slid it back into the envelope. "She'll want to see the actual materials. Both sets. She'll want to understand what constitutes the valley's record versus what constitutes Drake's commentary on it."

"Which means we have to show her ours."

"Which means we have to show her ours." Theo met her eyes. "All of it. The unredacted bargain terms. Adelaide's letters. Whitlock's letter. The ring ritual documentation. The toll redistribution records. Everything we've spent a year deciding how much of the world gets to know."

Wren felt the familiar squeeze in her chest - the old reflex to protect, to withhold, to say *not yet* and *not like this* and *not to them*. A year ago she would have fought this disclosure with everything she had. Six months ago she would have negotiated conditions. Now she only nodded.

"When?"

"First hearing is three weeks out. Ellery set a briefing schedule that gives us ten days to respond. Discovery is expedited; she's not letting this drag." Theo's thumb traced the envelope's creased edge. "She also ordered both parties to produce witness lists by Friday."

"Witnesses."

"People who can speak to the provenance of the materials. People who were there." He didn't say *you*. He didn't need to.

Wren looked toward the reading room, where morning light fell across tables where they'd spent months building something that resembled truth. Mara's engineering reports on the flood mitigation structures. Caleb's orchard yield records, annotated with gaps where his memory had failed. Priya's deposition transcripts from the Kell investigation. Dev's evidence logs from the substation work that had broken Drake's hold the first time. Odette's careful notation of what she'd lost and what she'd kept. Denise's testimony, given freely after fifty years of silence. Yusuf's council minutes. Walt's photographed notebook pages, the ordinary man's extraordinary witness.

All of it real. All of it theirs. And all of it now subject to a judge's ruling in a courtroom thirty miles from the valley's edge.

"We'll need Dev," Wren said.

Theo's nod was slight. "I already called him. He's coming up from the substation tomorrow. Priya's driving him."

"Good." She exhaled. "Good. Because if Drake's lawyers try to paint his evidence as coerced or fabricated - "

"They won't. Not with the chain of custody Dev maintained. Not with the timestamps. Not with the substation's own automated logs corroborating every access point." Theo's voice took on the calm it held when he was certain of his ground. "Dev's work is the cleanest evidence I've seen in twelve years of records investigation. Drake's counsel knows it. They're not challenging the evidence. They're challenging the *standing*."

"Standing."

"Who owns the story. Who has the right to tell it. Whether a community's oral and documentary history constitutes a protectable work or a public domain of shared memory." Theo set the envelope down. "That's the battle they've chosen. And it's the one we have to win."

---

The drive to Millbrook took forty-seven minutes through country roads winding between ridges dusted with early frost. Wren drove. Theo rode passenger with a binder on his lap, turning pages with the deliberate care of someone who understood that paper remembers every hand that touches it.

"He's not a ritualist," Theo said without looking up. "That's what makes him dangerous in a different way. Kell believed he was serving the bargain by controlling it. Drake believes the bargain is a contract with terms that can be litigated, assigned, monetized. He doesn't want to end it. He wants to *administer* it."

"Can he?"

"Not legally. Not without the valley's consent. But if he establishes that his record is the *authoritative* record - if a court agrees his compilation constitutes the definitive history - then he controls the narrative. He controls what the world believes about Amity Falls. He controls what future researchers find. He controls whether the next generation of valley children grow up knowing the truth or knowing his version of it."

Wren's hands tightened on the wheel. "And if we lose?"

"Then we publish anyway. We just publish *second*. And his version gets the first impression, the citation priority, the media narrative. The valley becomes a footnote in someone else's book."

The phrase landed wrong. Wren glanced over. Theo caught her look and made a small sound, not quite a laugh.

"Poor choice of words. Not a book. A record. A filing. Whatever he calls it."

"What does he call it?"

"*The Amity Falls Archive: A Compiled History of the Valley's Protective Covenant and Its Administrative Terms, 1847–Present*. Subtitle: *With Commentary on the Legal Status of Communal Memory Obligations*." Theo's tone was precisely dry. "He registered the copyright eight months ago. Before we went public with our timeline."

"Eight months. He's been planning this since before the ring ritual."

"Since before we knew he was still watching." Theo closed the binder. "He filed the injunction three days after Yusuf announced the Archive's opening date. He waited until we had a deadline he could threaten."

The courthouse occupied a limestone building on Millbrook's square, its clock tower stopped at 4:17 for as long as anyone could remember. Inside, the courtroom smelled of lemon oil and old wool, benches worn smooth by generations of disputes over property lines, water rights, and the quiet violences that never made the newspapers.

Judge Ellery was a woman in her late sixties, hair pulled back in a severe knot, glasses perched low on her nose. She read the motions without expression, her pen moving occasionally to mark a margin. When she looked up, her gaze swept the room - Drake's two attorneys in charcoal suits, Theo in his navy blazer, Wren in the charcoal coat Mara had insisted she buy for "grown-up occasions," Dev in a button-down that had seen better days but was clean and pressed, Priya beside him in her deputy's uniform.

"Counsel," the judge said. "Mr. Drake's petition asserts a proprietary interest in historical research materials concerning a community that does not appear on standard jurisdictional maps. The respondent - the Amity Falls Archive, represented by Mr. Marsh - contends that the materials in question constitute a communal record that cannot be privately owned. Before we proceed to the merits, I need to understand the threshold issue: does this court have jurisdiction over a community that, for all official purposes, does not exist?"

Drake's lead attorney, a woman named Calder, rose smoothly. "Your Honor, the plaintiff is a resident of Millbrook County. The defendant operates an archive that receives mail at a Millbrook County post office box. The research materials were compiled using Millbrook County public records, among other sources. The injunction seeks to prevent publication within this jurisdiction. Jurisdiction is proper."

"And the community itself? Amity Falls?"

"The community's legal status is not at issue. The plaintiff's property interest in his own work product is."

Judge Ellery's pen stopped. "Mr. Marsh?"

Theo stood. "Your Honor, the plaintiff's work product consists substantially of materials that belong to the community he describes - private correspondence, familial records, ceremonial documentation, and the community's own historical accounting of a bargain that has governed its existence for nearly two centuries. The plaintiff obtained these materials through a combination of public records requests, interviews conducted under false pretenses, and access granted by individuals he misled about his intentions. He then arranged them into a narrative that omits, distorts, and in key instances falsifies the record. He now seeks to use copyright law to prevent the actual custodians of that history from correcting it."

Calder's smile didn't reach her eyes. "Your Honor, the plaintiff's methodology is not on trial. His compilation is. The law protects original selection and arrangement. It does not require the compiler to be virtuous."

"The law also does not protect compilations built on stolen materials," Theo said. "Or materials obtained by fraud. Or materials that constitute the cultural heritage of a living community. Your Honor, we have brought the actual records. We have brought the people who made them. We have brought the evidence that the plaintiff's 'compilation' is not a work of scholarship but a weapon - assembled over nineteen years for the purpose of leverage, now deployed for the purpose of control."

"Nineteen years," Judge Ellery repeated. "Mr. Marsh, your brief references a prior incident in this county. Nineteen years ago."

"Yes, Your Honor. Corwin Drake's first attempt. He posed as an academic researcher. He gained access to the Millbrook County records room. He attempted to reconstruct the valley's bargain terms from fragmented references in deed restrictions, tax anomalies, and vital statistics gaps. He failed. In the process, he caused the removal of approximately ten years of memory from a resident of this county - Harriet Marsh, my grandmother. She is alive. She is here. She cannot tell you where she was for that decade. She cannot tell you what was taken. But the medical records confirm the onset, the pattern, and the fact that no organic cause was ever found."

The courtroom went very still. Wren felt Dev shift beside her, the subtle tension of a man who had once been coerced into redirecting a toll and now sat watching the architect of that coercion face consequences.

"Your Honor," Calder said. "The plaintiff denies any involvement in - "

"Ms. Calder." Judge Ellery's voice was mild. "I have read the police report from nineteen years ago. The investigating officer noted 'unexplained cognitive deficits consistent with targeted neurological intervention.' The case was closed for lack of a suspect. The plaintiff's name appears in the visitor log for the records room on the three days preceding the onset. Is that correct?"

"He was conducting legitimate research."

"Under what affiliation?"

"Independent scholar."

"Funded by whom?"

A pause. "The plaintiff has never disclosed his funding sources."

"Thank you." The judge made a notation. "Mr. Marsh, you mentioned evidence. Mr. Nair - Dev Nair, is it? - your brief identifies you as the custodian of the substation evidence logs."

Dev stood. "Yes, Your Honor."

"Your lineage?"

"Finder, Your Honor."

"And your role in the prior proceedings?"

"I was the one who redirected the toll that took Caleb Reyes's memory. Under coercion from Ambrose Kell. I served my sentence. Since then I've worked the substation - the valley's infrastructure records. The automated systems log every access, every query, every data transfer. Including the plaintiff's."

Calder rose again. "Your Honor, the substation logs are not in evidence. They were not produced in discovery. They are hearsay - "

"They're business records," Dev said. "Automated. Timestamped. Tamper-evident by design. I have the certified printouts. I have the hash verification. I have the chain of custody from the substation's isolated server to this courtroom." He reached into his bag, withdrew a sheaf of papers in a clear sleeve. "May I approach?"

Judge Ellery nodded. Dev crossed the well, handed the sleeve to the bailiff, who delivered it to the bench. The judge put on reading glasses, turned pages. Her eyebrows rose once - a tiny movement, but in the silence it sounded like a gunshot.

"These logs," she said, "show the plaintiff accessing the substation's restricted archive on forty-seven occasions between 2018 and 2023. Using credentials issued to... a Mr. Christopher Vane."

"An alias," Dev said. "The plaintiff used it to gain access to the valley's infrastructure monitoring system. He claimed to be a state inspector conducting a grid resilience audit. The substation's automated verification flagged the credentials as valid - they were forged at a level that bypassed the first two authentication layers. The third layer logged the anomaly but did not block access, because the system was designed to fail open for safety reasons. The plaintiff knew this. He exploited it."

"And the logs show what he accessed?"

"Everything. The bargain renewal records. The toll payment ledgers. The lineage registers. The ring ritual documentation. The memory debt accounting. He downloaded it all. The logs show the exact file paths, the exact timestamps, the exact volume of data exfiltrated. He didn't just compile public records. He stole the valley's private archives."

Calder's face had gone pale. "Your Honor, these logs - their authenticity - "

"Is verified by the substation's hardware security module," Dev said. "Which I also have the certification for. The module signs every log entry with a key that never leaves the device. The plaintiff's access left a cryptographic trail that cannot be forged, altered, or denied. He has the valley's complete administrative record. He knows it. And he knows that if the actual record is published alongside his version, his version collapses."

Judge Ellery set the logs down. She looked at Drake's empty chair - he had not appeared, sending only counsel - then at Calder, then at Theo, then at Wren.

"Mr. Marsh," she said. "Your response brief argues that the plaintiff's compilation is a derivative work of the valley's own records, and that the valley's records constitute a communal work that cannot be copyrighted by any individual. Is that the core of your position?"

"Yes, Your Honor. With one addition. The plaintiff's compilation is not merely derivative. It is *fraudulent*. It presents itself as a complete history while systematically omitting the evidence that contradicts his narrative - specifically, the evidence that the bargain's terms are administrative, not mystical; that the debt mechanism is measurable, not arbitrary; and that the community has, on multiple occasions, successfully challenged and modified those terms. He omits the ring ritual. He omits Denise's release. He omits the fact that the 'Warden of the first debt' was a role, not a bloodline, and that Ambrose Whitlock held it and tried to end the bargain and failed and left a warning that his family buried. He omits the truth so that he can sell a lie."

"And the lie is?"

"That the valley is cursed. That its people are trapped. That the bargain is absolute and unchangeable and that the only way out is through him." Theo's voice carried to the back of the room. "He's not selling history. He's selling despair. And he's using this court to make sure despair is the only story the world ever hears."

Judge Ellery was quiet for a long moment. She removed her glasses, rubbed the bridge of her nose, replaced them.

"Ms. Calder," she said. "Your client seeks an injunction to prevent publication of a competing history. He claims copyright in his compilation. The defendant argues that the compilation is built on stolen materials, obtained by fraud, and that the underlying materials constitute a communal record that cannot be privately owned. The defendant has produced evidence - admittedly, evidence I have not yet ruled admissible - that the plaintiff accessed the valley's private archives through forged credentials and exfiltrated the complete record."

"Your Honor, the admissibility of those logs - "

"Is a question for trial. But for the purpose of a preliminary injunction, I must weigh the likelihood of success on the merits. And the logs raise... significant questions." She looked at Theo. "Mr. Marsh, you are asking this court to deny an injunction and effectively permit publication of materials that your client acknowledges contain sensitive personal information about living individuals. Memory debts. Family histories. The identities of lineage holders. How do you propose to protect those interests?"

Theo didn't hesitate. "We've already done it. The Archive's publication plan - filed as Exhibit C - redacts all living individuals' lineage status unless they have consented in writing. It redacts the actual memories taken as debt payments. It publishes the *terms* and the *history* and the *evidence of manipulation*, not the private ledger of who paid what. The plaintiff's version does none of this. His version names names. Lists debts. Identifies the vulnerable. He didn't redact. He *indexed*."

"Indexed."

"He built a searchable database of every toll payment, every memory taken, every lineage holder's identity and vulnerability. He calls it 'administrative transparency.' We call it a targeting list."

Judge Ellery's gaze moved to Wren. "Ms. Castellano. You are the Archive's director. A Finder, your brief states."

"Yes, Your Honor."

"And you stand by the publication plan?"

"I do."

"Even knowing it will reveal the valley's existence to a world that has spent two centuries not seeing it?"

"The world already sees us, Your Honor. The satellite anomaly has been documented by three universities. The hydrological data doesn't match the region. The crop yields don't match the climate. People notice. They just don't have a framework for what they're noticing. Drake's version gives them a framework of fear and control. Ours gives them the truth - that we are a community that has survived by caring for each other, and that the bargain is a system we can understand, discuss, and eventually change. But only if we're honest about it first."

"Honest." The judge's mouth twitched. "A dangerous thing, in a courtroom."

"Sometimes the only safe one, Your Honor."

The judge was quiet again. She shuffled papers, read a paragraph, made a note. The courtroom held its breath.

"Ms. Calder," she said finally. "Your client's injunction is denied. The balance of harms favors the defendant - the plaintiff seeks to suppress a corrective history using a compilation built on materials he obtained through fraud. The public interest favors disclosure of the complete record over the plaintiff's partial and misleading version. The plaintiff's copyright claim, to the extent it rests on selection and arrangement of stolen materials, is unlikely to succeed on the merits."

Calder's mouth opened. "Your Honor - "

"The denial is without prejudice to the plaintiff's copyright claim, which may proceed to discovery and trial. But no injunction will issue. The defendant may proceed with publication on the announced timeline. The plaintiff's materials may be referenced, critiqued, and corrected in the defendant's publication, subject to the redaction plan filed as Exhibit C. Any further motions to stay will be viewed with extreme skepticism."

She looked at Theo. "Mr. Marsh. Your client's redaction plan is... adequate. Barely. I expect full compliance. Any deviation will be treated as contempt."

"Yes, Your Honor."

"And Mr. Nair."

Dev straightened. "Your Honor."

"Your substation logs are admitted for the purpose of this hearing. For trial, you will need to lay a proper foundation. I suggest you retain a digital forensics expert. The plaintiff's counsel will tear them apart otherwise."

"Understood, Your Honor."

"Court is adjourned."

The gavel fell. The sound was surprisingly small.

---

Outside on the courthouse steps, November air bit at exposed skin. Wren stood with her hands in her coat pockets, watching her breath cloud. Theo joined her, the binder tucked under his arm again.

"We won," he said.

"We won the hearing. Not the case."

"The case was never the point. The point was the timeline. He wanted to stop us before the Archive opened. He failed." Theo's mouth quirked. "Also, Dev's evidence just became part of the public record. Which means Drake's access to the substation is now documented in a federal court filing. Which means the U.S. Attorney's office in the Northern District just got a very interesting referral package."

Wren turned. "You sent it?"

"I sent it the moment we left the courtroom. Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. Wire fraud. Identity theft - the Vane alias. Unauthorized access to a protected computer system. The substation qualifies. The logs are the smoking gun." Theo's voice was quiet. "Drake isn't just an information broker anymore. He's a federal defendant."

"And if the U.S. Attorney declines?"

"Then we still have the judgment. We still have the Archive. We still have the truth." Theo looked at her. "But they won't decline. Not with this paper trail. Not with Dev's testimony. Not with your grandmother's memory loss documented in medical records that match the plaintiff's visitor log."

Wren thought of Harriet Marsh, in her care facility in Millbrook, folding napkins into precise squares and smiling at visitors who asked questions she couldn't answer. Thought of the decade gone from her life, the wedding she couldn't remember, the grandchildren she'd met as strangers. Thought of Theo visiting every Sunday, bringing photographs, reading aloud from the newspaper, building a bridge across a gap no one had been able to explain.

"Your grandmother," Wren said. "She'll know. About the referral."

"She'll know something happened. She won't remember the details by tomorrow. But she'll know." Theo's jaw tightened. "That's enough for now."

Dev and Priya descended the steps behind them. Dev looked tired in the way of someone who had carried something heavy for a long time and finally set it down. Priya's hand rested on his elbow, the familiar anchor.

"Federal charges," Dev said. "That's... that's real."

"It's real," Theo said. "And it's not the valley's problem anymore. It's the government's problem. Ordinary law. Ordinary consequences. No rituals. No bargains. No memory debts."

"Just paperwork," Priya said. "And judges. And lawyers. And time."

"Just paperwork," Theo agreed. "Which, as it turns out, I'm rather good at."

Wren laughed, a short surprised sound. "You are. You really are."

They walked to the parking lot together, four people who had spent a year building something that would outlast them. The Archive's opening was in six days. The publication in six days. The truth would be on the shelves, in the cases, in the searchable database with its careful redactions and its honest gaps.

Drake would fight. He would appeal. He would drag it out. But he had lost the only weapon that mattered - the ability to tell the story first, to tell it wrong, to make the lie the default.

The valley would speak for itself now. On its own terms. In its own words.

Wren got in the driver's seat. Theo buckled beside her. Dev and Priya climbed into the back. The engine turned over. The heater blasted cold air that would warm eventually.

"Home," Wren said.

"Home," Theo echoed.

And they drove.
