The kitchen table held only the recorder and the stack of notebooks. Wren sat with her back to the window, morning light falling across the pages she had spent the last year filling. Theo sat opposite, his own notebook open, a fountain pen capped beside it. The house was quiet. Her mother had gone to the clinic early. The only sounds were the refrigerator's hum and the occasional creak of floorboards upstairs.

"Ready when you are," Theo said.

Wren picked up the first notebook. The cover was worn soft at the corners, the binding cracked from being pressed flat too many times. She turned to the first page. Her handwriting from a year ago looked cramped, uncertain. The dates at the top of each entry marched forward in a steady line.

"January third," she read. "First entry after the ring. Denise is sleeping in the spare room. She woke up twice in the night asking for her husband. He's been dead forty-three years."

Theo wrote as she spoke. His hand moved fast, the pen scratching against paper. He didn't look up. She had watched him work in the county archives — the same focus, the same economy of motion. He captured everything. The pauses. The corrections. The places where her voice cracked and she started over.

"Go on," he said.

She turned the page. "January fourth. Caleb came by the orchard. He remembered the proposal. He remembered the argument about the peach trees. He did not remember the drive to the hospital when Mara went into labor early. That memory is still gone. Mara says she doesn't know if she wants it back."

Theo wrote. The recorder's red light blinked steady.

"January fifth. Priya brought over the transcript from the council meeting. Yusuf wants the record formalized. He said the valley cannot afford another silence. He said we owe it to the ones who paid."

She kept reading. Theo kept writing. The stack of notebooks shrank. The stack of transcript pages grew.

By noon her throat felt raw. She stopped at the entry for February twelfth, the day Dev had shown them the gap in Drake's account. The day the leverage had collapsed.

"Lunch break," Theo said. He capped his pen. "I'll make sandwiches."

He moved through her kitchen like he knew where everything was. The bread in the second drawer. The peanut butter on the top shelf. The jam in the refrigerator door. He didn't ask. He simply did it. She watched him from the table, the notebooks spread around her, the work half done.

"You've been here a lot," she said.

"Six months."

"Feels longer."

"Feels shorter to me."

He set two plates on the table. They ate in silence for a few minutes. The peanut butter stuck to the roof of her mouth. She drank water from a glass he had poured without asking.

"Priya is coming this afternoon," he said. "She wants to go over the safeguards before we transcribe the Drake material."

Wren nodded. "The Caller safeguards."

"She calls them procedural controls. She gets annoyed if anyone uses the lineage term in official documentation."

"She's right to. The term implies something mystical. What she does is measurable. Five feet. Truth compelled. Calm compelled. Composure paid after."

"She'll appreciate the precision."

"She'll appreciate that you used the word composure instead of cost."

Theo smiled — a small expression, mostly in the eyes. "I've read her reports. She uses composure in every one."

"She's thorough."

"She's the only reason the substation evidence held up in court. Dev found the gap. Priya made sure the testimony stuck."

Wren swallowed. "How is Dev?"

"Busy. The substation work keeps expanding. The county hired him on contract after the trial. He says the procedural framework he built for the Drake case is being adapted for three other cold cases."

"Good."

"Priya worries about him. She won't say it directly. She'll say his workload is unsustainable or his documentation habits need refinement. But she checks his calendar every morning. She makes sure he eats."

Wren looked down at her plate. "My mother does that for me."

"Does it help?"

"Sometimes. Mostly it makes me feel like I'm twelve."

Theo didn't laugh. He took a bite of his sandwich, chewed, swallowed. "My grandmother asks me if I've eaten every time I call. She forgets she asked five minutes ago. She asks again. I answer every time. It's the only conversation we have that doesn't loop."

Wren set her sandwich down. "The decade before 2007."

"The decade she can't speak about. Nineteen years since Drake's first attempt. She was there. She knows something. She just can't reach it."

"Have you tried a Finder?"

"She refuses. She says some things are lost for a reason. She says she made peace with the gaps."

"Has she?"

"I don't know. She smiles a lot. She tends her roses. She listens to the same radio station she listened to when I was a child. She seems content. But sometimes I catch her staring at photographs she doesn't recognize. People she should know. Places she should remember. She traces their faces with her finger. She never says a word."

The kitchen felt smaller. The light had shifted. Dust motes drifted through the air above the table.

"I'm sorry," Wren said.

"Don't be. It's not your weight to carry."

"It's not yours either."

Theo looked at her. His expression was calm, but something moved behind his eyes. "It's the only weight I've ever had. Everything else I built around it. The degree. The archives job. The research. Every request I filed, every restricted file I chased, every FOIA appeal I wrote. All of it trying to reach her."

"And now you're here. Transcribing my notebooks."

"Now I'm here. Transcribing your notebooks."

He stood and took their plates to the sink. Water ran. Dishes clinked. He dried his hands on the towel and came back to the table.

"Priya will be here in twenty minutes," he said. "We should clear the Drake material to the front."

They worked in silence, sorting the notebooks. The Drake entries were fewer than she expected. She had not written about him often. She had written around him — the threat he represented, the letters he sent, the partial record he claimed to hold, the deadline he had given them.

September. October. November. The months when the valley held its breath.

She found the entry for November third. Her handwriting was jagged, pressed hard into the paper.

"Drake's letter arrived today. He has the names of everyone who renewed the bargain since 1972. He has the dates. He has the memories taken. He does not have the terms. He wants the terms. He says if we don't give them to him by the solstice, he publishes what he has. He says the valley will not survive the exposure."

Theo read over her shoulder. He didn't touch the page. He didn't breathe loud.

"Yusuf called an emergency council," she read. "Mara and Caleb came. Priya and Dev. Odette. Walt. Denise. We sat in the school gymnasium. Yusuf said we publish first. We publish everything. The complete true record. Before he can weaponize his version. He said the valley has survived worse than truth."

"He was right," Theo said quietly.

"He was. But I was terrified. I wrote that I was terrified. I wrote that I didn't know if I could be the one to tell it. I wrote that my grandmother never told me the full terms. She said I wasn't ready. She said I'd know when the time came. She died before the time came."

Theo's hand rested on the table near hers. Not touching. Close enough that she felt the warmth of it.

"The time came anyway," he said.

"Yes. It did."

The front door opened. Heavy boots on the porch. The screen door slammed.

"Knocking is a courtesy," Priya's voice called from the entryway. "It is also a procedural requirement for entering an active work site."

"Since when is my kitchen a work site?" Wren called back.

"Since you began transcribing classified valley records for public release. I've logged it. The site designation is Kitchen-Primary. The classification is Restricted-Until-Publication."

Theo stood. "Good afternoon, Deputy."

"Archivist Marsh." Priya entered the kitchen. She wore her uniform, the badge catching the light. Her hair was pulled back in a tight braid. She carried a briefcase that looked heavy. "Finder Castellano. The safeguards are prepared. I've brought printed copies for both of you. I've also brought the composure log. I'll need you to sign the acknowledgment forms before we begin the Drake segment."

She set the briefcase on the counter and opened it. Files emerged in perfect order. She distributed them with the efficiency of someone who had done this a thousand times.

"Safeguard one," Priya said, tapping the first page. "I will maintain a continuous Caller field throughout the transcription of all Drake-related entries. Radius: five feet. Effect: compelled truth and calm. This ensures no involuntary omissions, no emotional distortions, no protective editing. The record will be complete."

"Safeguard two," Theo read from his copy. "A composure recovery period of thirty minutes follows each hour of active field maintenance. During recovery, no transcription occurs. No questions are asked. No decisions are made."

"Correct. Safeguard three. A witness observes each session. The witness cannot be the transcriber or the subject. The witness documents any irregularities in the transcript compared to the source material. Archivist Marsh, you are designated witness for Finder Castellano's sessions. Finder Castellano, you are designated witness for Archivist Marsh's sessions when he transcribes his own research notes."

"What about you?" Wren asked. "Who witnesses you?"

"I do not transcribe. I maintain the field. My composure is logged by the automatic timer. The log is sealed and countersigned by the witness after each session. Any discrepancy invalidates the session transcript."

"Thorough," Theo said.

"Necessary. Safeguard four. The Drake material is transcribed last. All other material is complete and sealed before the field is activated for the final segment. This prevents contamination. If the field fails, the prior record remains clean."

"Contamination," Wren repeated.

"Emotional bleed," Priya said. "The Drake entries carry high distress markers. Under a truth field, that distress transfers. The transcriber may incorporate feelings that are not in the source text. The witness catches it. The composure break resets it."

She clicked her pen. "Signatures. Page three. Initial each safeguard. Date and time."

They signed. Wren's hand trembled slightly. She pressed harder. The ink bled into the paper fibers.

"Timer starts when I activate the field," Priya said. "I'll give a verbal warning at fifty minutes. Hard stop at sixty. No exceptions."

"Understood," Theo said.

"One more thing." Priya's voice softened. "The field compels truth. It also compels calm. You may feel emotions that don't belong to you. You may feel calm that isn't yours. Remember that the feeling is the field. The truth is yours. Separate them."

Wren nodded. "I've been in a Caller field before. During the ring. Dev maintained it."

"Dev's field was shared across ten people. Mine is single-source. The intensity is different. The composure cost is mine alone."

"You don't have to do this."

"I do. The valley needs this record clean. I am the only Caller available who understands the procedural requirements and the personal stakes. Dev is substation-bound. The other Callers are retired or untrained for sustained work."

"Your composure."

"Is mine to spend. I've budgeted for this. Six sessions. Six hours of field time. Three hours of recovery. I'll be functional by evening. I'll be tired for three days. I've cleared my shift schedule."

Wren looked at Theo. He was reading the form again, his expression unreadable. He signed. He handed it back.

"Ready," he said.

Priya activated the timer on her phone. The screen glowed. 00:00:00.

"Field active," she said.

The air changed. Wren felt it first in her shoulders, a sudden drop of tension she hadn't known she carried. Her jaw unclenched. Her breath slowed. The notebook in front of her looked ordinary. The handwriting looked like hers. The words looked true.

She turned to November third. She began to read.

"Drake's letter arrived today. He has the names of everyone who renewed the bargain since 1972. He has the dates. He has the memories taken. He does not have the terms. He wants the terms. He says if we don't give them to him by the solstice, he publishes what he has. He says the valley will not survive the exposure."

Theo wrote. His pen moved steady. The field held.

"November fourth. Yusuf met with the county attorney. The attorney said Drake's partial record is legally obtained. Public records requests. Interviews with former valley residents who left. Newspaper archives. Property transfers. Death certificates. He built it legally. He can publish it legally. The only defense is prior publication. We publish first. We publish everything."

The words flowed. She read. He wrote. The timer ticked.

"November fifth. Mara and Caleb argued. Mara said publishing destroys the valley's protection. Caleb said the protection failed nineteen years ago when Ambrose Kell covered it up. He said the protection failed again when Denise renewed in secret. He said the only protection left is truth. Mara said truth without consent is violence. Caleb said silence without consent is also violence. They both cried. I sat at their kitchen table and wrote it down."

Her voice did not waver. The field held her steady.

"November sixth. Priya came to the house. She said Dev found something in the substation logs. A pattern in the power fluctuations that matched the bargain renewal dates. She said it proves the bargain is measurable. Quantifiable. Not mystical. Administrative. She said Drake knows this. He wants the administrative terms because they're enforceable. He wants to control the enforcement."

The timer beeped. A soft chime.

"Fifty minutes," Priya said. Her voice was flat. Her face was pale. She sat perfectly still in the chair by the window, her hands folded in her lap. The composure cost showed in the tightness around her eyes, the stillness of her breathing.

"Ten minutes," Wren said. She closed the notebook. "We stop at sixty."

Theo capped his pen. "Understood."

They sat in silence. The field hummed invisible around them. Wren felt the calm like a weight on her chest. It was not her calm. It was borrowed. She thought of her grandmother's notebooks, the ones she had inherited. The gaps in those pages. The memories her grandmother had paid to find things. The memories her grandmother had chosen not to tell her.

She thought of Theo's grandmother. Harriet. Staring at photographs. Tracing faces she didn't recognize.

The timer beeped again. A different tone.

"Sixty minutes," Priya said. "Field inactive."

The calm vanished. Not gradually. It snapped away like a rubber band cut. Wren gasped. Her shoulders hunched. Her jaw clenched. Grief surged up, sudden and sharp, for her grandmother, for the secrets kept, for the valley she loved and the truth that might break it.

Theo put a hand on the table. He didn't reach for her. He just anchored himself there.

Priya exhaled. A long, shaky breath. Color returned to her face. She picked up her phone. "Composure log entry. Session one complete. Duration: sixty minutes. Recovery period initiated. Thirty minutes. No irregularities observed."

She set the phone down. She looked at Wren. "You held."

"I didn't hold. You held me."

"That's the job."

Priya stood. She moved stiffly to the counter, poured water from the tap, drank it in three swallows. She leaned against the sink, eyes closed.

Theo stood. He went to the refrigerator. He pulled out the lemonade pitcher. He poured a glass, set it on the counter near Priya's elbow. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

"Thank you," Priya said. She drank the lemonade. "That's... thoughtful."

"You're doing the work. The least I can do is bring the lemonade."

She smiled. It was small. Real. "You're learning the valley ways. Feed the people carrying the weight."

"It's a good way."

They sat in the recovery silence. Wren's heart rate slowed. The grief receded, not gone but manageable. Her own calm returned, thin and tired but hers.

"Twenty minutes," Priya said, checking her phone. "Then session two. The November seventh through twelfth entries. The council decision. The publication plan. The first draft of the public record."

Wren nodded. She looked at the notebook stack. Three more Drake entries. Then the transcription would be complete. Then the editing. Then the layout. Then the print run. Then the race.

"Did you hear about the Millbrook Gazette?" Theo asked suddenly.

Priya opened her eyes. "No. What about it?"

"An excerpt. Leaked. Published this morning. Page three. 'Valley's Secret Bargain: Memory for Safety.'"

Wren went cold. "This morning? The Gazette comes out Wednesdays. Today is Tuesday."

"Early edition. Subscriber only. Someone got it to the press before the print run."

"Drake."

"Has to be. The excerpt matches his partial record. Names. Dates. Memory types. Not the terms. Not the administrative structure. Just enough to damn us."

Priya stood straight. The fatigue fell away like a shed skin. Her posture shifted. Deputy. "Who has the copy?"

"My phone," Theo said. "I checked the digital edition before I came over. It's already spreading. The comments section has three hundred replies. Half of them are asking if this is the Amity Falls that doesn't appear on GPS. The other half are calling it a cult."

"Yusuf needs to know." Priya grabbed her briefcase. "The council needs to convene. Emergency session. Today."

"I'll drive," Theo said. "Wren, you stay. Finish the transcription. The record is the only answer."

"I'm not staying here while the valley burns."

"The valley isn't burning. The valley is being tested. The record is the shield. You write the shield. I'll handle the fire."

He was already moving. Keys. Jacket. Door.

Priya followed. "I'll call Yusuf. I'll get the council to the school gym. One hour."

The screen door slammed. The engine started. The truck pulled away.

Wren stood alone in the kitchen. The notebooks spread across the table. The recorder blinking red. The timer on Priya's phone counting down recovery minutes.

She picked up the November seventh entry. Her hands shook. She steadied them against the table edge.

"Field inactive," she whispered to the empty room. "Recovery period. Twenty minutes."

She opened the notebook. She read the first line.

"November seventh. Emergency council session. Yusuf presented the publication plan. He said we publish the complete record in three parts. Part one: the bargain history. Part two: the renewal record. Part three: the administrative terms. He said we release part one immediately. Digital. Free. Widely distributed. We beat Drake to the narrative."

She turned the page. Theo's handwriting waited on the transcript sheets. Blank lines ready to be filled.

She sat down. She picked up her pen. She began to write.

The phone on the counter buzzed. A text notification. She ignored it. It buzzed again. And again. She kept writing.

The valley had survived worse than truth. She had to believe that. She had to write it until it was true.

Outside, the morning light strengthened. The valley woke. Somewhere a rooster crowed. A tractor started. The ordinary sounds of a place that didn't exist on maps, a place that had traded memory for safety for generations, a place now racing to tell its own story before someone else told it for them.

Wren wrote. The pen scratched. The truth accumulated. Page by page. Line by line. The shield grew.
