The box smelled of cedar and old glue, the scent of things kept too long in the dark. Wren had expected leather bindings, maybe a journal. What she found was a stack of loose pages held together by a rubber band that had long since lost its elasticity, crumbling at the edges into brown dust.

"Christ," Theo said from beside her. He didn't touch anything. Just leaned in, penlight clicking on, the beam narrow and deliberate. "It's not a manuscript. It's a disaster."

The pages were numbered, at least. Someone had taken the trouble to pencil numerals in the upper right corners, though the sequence jumped — 1, 2, 3, then 7, 12, 19. Gaps like missing teeth.

Wren slipped on cotton gloves. Her fingers trembled, just slightly, the way they always did when she held something that had survived by accident. "The numbering's Whitlock's hand. I've seen his letters."

"Ambrose Whitlock," Theo corrected. Automatic now. He'd stopped letting her abbreviate three days ago. "Ambrose Whitlock. Not Kell. He never went by just Ambrose."

"Right. Ambrose Whitlock." She lifted the first page. The paper was thin, almost translucent, ink bleeding through from the other side in a spiderweb of brown. "He didn't bind it. He just... kept the pages. Like he expected to add more. Or take some away."

Theo's penlight moved to the second page. "The ink's different on this one. Darker. Iron gall, maybe, but fresher. The first page looks like it sat in sunlight for a decade."

"Different sittings, then. Different years." Wren turned the page carefully, aligning the torn edge with its neighbor. "He wrote this over time. Not in one sitting. He came back to it."

The third page was fragmented, the bottom third missing entirely. Wren set it down on the acid-free mat, then reached for the fourth — page 7, according to the numbering. The tear there was clean, almost surgical.

"Knife," Theo said. "Not tearing. Look at the fibers under the light."

She looked. He was right. The cut was straight, precise. "Drake?"

"Or Whitlock himself. Cutting out something he decided shouldn't stay." Theo's voice was flat. "People do that. Edit their own history before someone else reads it."

Wren felt her chest tighten. She thought of her mother, burning letters in the woodstove. Her father, crossing out paragraphs in the family Bible. The valley's entire history was a document with pages missing and lines blacked out, its margins crowded with corrections in hands that no longer existed.

"Page 12," she said, lifting it. "This one's intact."

The handwriting was cramped, Whitlock's later script — smaller, tighter, as if the words were being squeezed out of him. Wren read aloud, slow and careful:

*"The bargain is not a contract. It is a covenant. The distinction matters. A contract binds*
