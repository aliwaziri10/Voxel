The council chamber smelled of pine oil and old paper, the same scent that had clung to these walls since Wren was tall enough to see over the table's edge. Tonight the air carried something sharper beneath it - sweat, the metallic tang of fear held too long in too many throats.

Yusuf sat at the head of the table, his hands folded on the agenda packet. He looked older than the twelve months since the ring. The lines around his eyes had deepened into grooves. His hair, always silver at the temples, had surrendered the last of its darkness.

"Order," he said. His voice carried without rising. "We have a full docket. Item one: the Drake matter. Item two: the publication timeline. Item three - "

"Item one and two are the same item," Marta Cortes said. She sat three seats down on the left, her fingers tight around a water glass. "We cannot discuss a timeline for publishing our history while Drake holds his version over us like a blade. He wants the original terms. We give him those terms, we've handed him the keys to the bargain itself."

"We don't give him anything," Hal Brenner countered from the right side. His face was flushed, the color climbing his neck. "We stall. We negotiate. We buy time to - to what? To finish our own record? Wren's team has been at this for eight months. Eight months, and the manuscript still isn't ready for print."

"Because we're doing it right," Wren said. The words left her mouth before she could catch them.

The chamber went quiet. Eleven heads turned toward her.

She sat at the table's foot, not by right but by invitation - the Finder's seat, the documentarian's seat. Her notebook lay open before her, pages dense with her own handwriting. Theo sat beside her, his own notes organized in color-coded tabs. He didn't look at her. He didn't need to.

"Right," Hal repeated. "Right means perfect. Right means we miss the window. Drake has a publisher lined up. He has a distribution deal. He has - "

"He has a partial record," Yusuf said. "He has gaps. He has the parts that serve his narrative and nothing more. That is not a book. That is a weapon."

"And our record?" Marta asked. "Our record names names. Our record puts Denise's secret on the page for anyone to read. It puts Dev's lineage on the page. It puts the ring ritual on the page - ten volunteers who agreed to share a debt they never asked for. How many of them have told their families? How many of them want their grandchildren reading about it in a bound volume?"

"Every volunteer signed a release," Priya said. Her voice was calm, procedural. "Every release was witnessed. Every release is filed."

"Signed under pressure," Hal shot back. "Signed because the alternative was letting Dev carry it alone. You know that. I know that. Don't pretend procedure makes it clean."

Priya's jaw tightened. She didn't answer.

"This is not a procedural question," Yusuf said. "This is a question of trust. The valley trusted us with the truth. We owe them the truth in full, not the version that protects our comfort."

"Easy to say when it's not your comfort on the line," Marta muttered.

"It is my comfort," Yusuf said. "It is my record. I was chair when Denise renewed the bargain in secret. I was chair when the ring was called. I hesitated. I failed. I will not fail again by hiding what happened."

The words hung in the air. Yusuf had never spoken of his hesitation so plainly. Not in public. Not in the council minutes.

Hal's flush darkened. "You hesitated because you didn't know what you were walking into. None of us did. Drake knows. He's been building this for nineteen years. Nineteen years since Millbrook. He knows every crack in the foundation. He knows exactly where to press."

"Then we stop giving him cracks," Wren said.

She felt the words rise, not from panic or defensiveness, but from somewhere quieter. The place where she'd spent eight months sorting through Adelaide's letters, Whitlock's hidden archive, the county index Theo had pried open. The place where she'd learned to stop asking permission to know things.

"Drake's hold comes from what we haven't said," she continued. "Every gap he found, he found because we left it empty. The restricted notation in the county index: Theo found that. The standing arrangement: we knew it existed and we let it sit. Denise's fifty years: we let her carry it alone until she couldn't anymore. Whitlock's letter: we found it in a false bottom drawer because his family buried it instead of reading it."

She paused. The silence stretched.

"The answer isn't to negotiate with him," she said. "The answer is to publish first. Complete. Unedited. Every name. Every date. Every debt paid and every debt redirected. We put it all on the page before he can put his half-truths on his. That's how we win. Not by protecting the valley from the truth. By trusting the valley with it."

Her voice held steady. She didn't look at Theo. She didn't look at Yusuf. She looked at the table's surface, at the grain of the wood, at the ink stain near the left edge that had been there since before she was born.

When she finally lifted her gaze, Hal was staring at her. So was Marta. So was every councilor around the arc.

Yusuf's expression didn't change. But something in his shoulders released - a fraction, a breath's worth.

"Wren," he said. "You're leading this project. The timeline is yours. What do you need?"

She opened her mouth. The old words waited on her tongue - *I'm sorry, I didn't mean to speak out of turn, I'm sorry if that was presumptuous, I'm sorry for taking up space.*

They didn't come.

She caught herself not saying them. The absence felt like a muscle she'd finally learned to flex.

"One week," she said. "The same one week the council already voted to open the Archive. I'm not asking for more time. I'm asking you to hold the line on it. Theo and I have the last three chapters in draft. The appendices are indexed. The footnotes are verified against the original documents. We need the print run authorized, the distribution list confirmed, and the archive room ready for the public opening - on the timeline we already set."

"One week," Hal repeated. "And if Drake moves before then?"

"Then he publishes a lie," Wren said. "And we publish the truth the day after. The valley will know the difference. They've known the difference before."

Marta set down her water glass. "You sound certain."

"I'm not certain," Wren said. "I'm done pretending certainty is required."

A few nods around the table. Walt Pruitt, sitting near the door, caught her eye and raised his chin - a small gesture, barely visible, but it meant he'd heard her.

"One week," Yusuf said. "The deadline stands. Print authorization goes to the vote. All in favor?"

Hands rose. Not unanimous. Hal kept his flat on the table. Marta hesitated, then lifted hers.

"Motion carries," Yusuf said. "Item two: the archive opening. We'll schedule it for the day after publication. Wren, you'll coordinate with Odette on the store's display space - "

"Already done," Wren said. "Odette and I spoke last Tuesday. She's clearing the front window."

"Good. Priya, Dev - security for the opening?"

"Handled," Priya said. "Dev's coordinating with the county sheriff's office. They've agreed to a presence outside the perimeter."

"Mara, Caleb - the orchard's contribution?"

"Apple butter and cider," Mara said. Her voice carried the same steadiness it had held at the ring. "Same as always. Caleb's pressing the fall batch this week."

"Denise?"

The oldest woman at the table smiled, the corners of her eyes crinkling. "I'll bring the ginger cookies. The recipe my mother wrote down. The one I remembered wrong for fifty years; now I remember it right."

A ripple of something like laughter moved through the chamber. Tension breaking, not gone but breathed through.

"Then we're settled," Yusuf said. "One week. The record goes to print. The archive opens its doors. The valley learns its history from us, not from Corwin Drake." He gathered his papers. "Thank you. All of you. Dismissed."

Chairs scraped. Papers shuffled. The councilors filed out in ones and twos, their conversations low and urgent.

Wren stayed seated. Her notebook lay open. She hadn't written a single word during the meeting.

Theo closed his own folder, the tabs clicking against each other. He didn't stand.

"You didn't apologize," he said quietly.

Wren looked at him. The council chamber's single lamp threw light across his face: the bridge of his nose, the line of his jaw, the faint scar above his left eyebrow from a childhood bicycle accident he'd told her about during a cataloguing session three months ago.

"What?"

"You didn't apologize," he repeated. "For speaking. For leading. You just... spoke."

She considered this. The words she'd swallowed for twenty-one years - *sorry, sorry, sorry* - a litany meant to soften every edge she presented to the world. The habit her mother had taught her, the habit her grandmother had modeled, the habit that had kept her small and safe and unseen.

"I noticed," she said.

"And?"

"And it felt... ordinary. Not brave. Not dramatic. Just - what needed saying."

Theo nodded once. "That's the point, isn't it?"

She let out a breath she hadn't realized she'd been holding. "When did you get so insightful?"

"Eight months of cataloguing sessions. Also my grandmother calls me every Sunday and tells me exactly what she thinks of my life choices. It sharpens a person."

Wren laughed, quiet and sudden. "Does Harriet know about the publication?"

"She knows I'm working on a historical project. She knows it matters. She doesn't know the specifics, and I'm not going to tell her until it's printed. She deserves to read it like everyone else: on her own terms, in her own time."

The care in his voice made something in her chest expand.

"She'll be proud," Wren said.

"She'll be furious I didn't tell her first. Then she'll be proud. Then she'll call me and list every comma she'd have placed differently." He stood, stretching. "Come on. The manuscript won't finalize itself."

She gathered her notebook, her pen, the scattered sticky notes marking the chapters still needing work. The chamber emptied around them, the door clicking shut behind the last councilor.

Yusuf paused at the threshold. He looked at Wren, then at Theo. His gaze lingered on Wren a moment longer.

"Good work tonight," he said. "Both of you."

"Thank you, Yusuf," Theo said.

Wren opened her mouth. *Thank you. I'm sorry I - *

She closed it. She nodded.

"Good night," she said.

Yusuf's mouth quirked, not quite a smile but close. "Good night."

He left.

The lamp flickered. Outside, the valley settled into evening: the orchard's windfall apples thumping into grass, the general store's sign creaking in the breeze, the distant hum of the substation where Dev worked through the night on evidence logs.

Wren shouldered her bag. Theo held the door.

They walked down the hall together, toward the archive room where the manuscript waited: incomplete, imperfect, honest.

She didn't apologize for the space she took up on the path.

She didn't apologize for the truth she'd spoken.

She didn't apologize for the work still ahead.

The night air was cool. Stars pricked the sky above the valley's invisible boundary, the same stars that had watched every bargain renewal, every debt paid, every secret kept and every secret told.

One week.

She could work with one week.
