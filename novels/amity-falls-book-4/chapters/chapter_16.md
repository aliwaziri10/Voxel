The email arrived at 6:47 a.m. on a Tuesday, subject line blank, sender a string of characters that resolved to nothing when Theo traced it. He had been sitting at the kitchen table with a mug of coffee gone cold, the spreadsheet of county index cross-references open on his laptop, the morning light pale through the window above the sink. Wren was still asleep upstairs, the house quiet except for the refrigerator cycling on.

He almost deleted it. Spam, phishing, the usual noise. But the attachment name stopped him: *Amity_Falls_Protection_Myth_v1.pdf*.

He opened it.

The document was three pages, cleanly designed, professional typography. No ransom-note aesthetics. No threats. Just a calm, methodical dismantling. Page one: satellite imagery showing the valley's coordinate drift, the way the town sat a few hundred yards off where maps said it should be. Page two: a timeline of agricultural yields, flood records, illness statistics across three counties, each annotated with red arrows pointing to years when Amity Falls numbers diverged from every neighboring district. Page three: a single paragraph in black text on white background.

*For fifty years the residents of Amity Falls have been told their safety is a covenant. That their crops do not fail, their rivers do not rise, their children do not sicken because of a bargain struck with something older than the county. This is a story. The data suggests a simpler explanation: geography, aquifer stability, microclimate, and statistical variance. The protection is a myth. The bargain is a control mechanism. The memory loss is the price of belief.*

No signature. No contact information. Just a line at the bottom: *Full report forthcoming. The record speaks for itself.*

Theo stared at the screen. His fingers were cold around the mug. He heard footsteps on the stairs, light and quick, and Wren appeared in the doorway wearing the oversized cardigan she slept in, hair tangled, eyes still heavy. She stopped when she saw his face.

"Theo? What is it?"

He turned the laptop toward her. She read standing there, one hand braced on the doorframe. Her face went through three expressions in as many seconds: confusion, recognition, grim comprehension that settled into a quiet she did not often wear.

"Drake," she said.

"Yeah."

"He said forthcoming. Not tomorrow. Not next week. Forthcoming."

"The teaser is the point. He wants us to see it first. He wants the valley to see it."

Wren came around the table and sat in the chair beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his. She read it again, slower this time. Her thumb brushed the trackpad, scrolling up to the satellite imagery.

"He's not wrong about the coordinates," she said quietly. "Or the yields. Or the flood records. He's cherry-picked the explanation."

"He's framed the truth so it sounds like a lie."

"Because the truth *is* a lie, if you don't have the rest of it." She exhaled, long and slow. "The bargain isn't a story we tell ourselves. It's a mechanism. But he's presenting the mechanism's outputs as proof the mechanism doesn't exist."

Theo watched her process it. The Finder's mind, the archivist's mind, turning the same object over from different angles. This was what they did now. Catalog. Cross-reference. Build the record that would withstand what was coming.

"We need to call Yusuf," she said. "And Priya. Dev needs to see the data forensic side. If Drake's releasing this publicly, the county index restriction is going to become a problem faster than we planned."

Theo nodded. He reached for his phone, then paused. "Wren. The teaser calls the protection a myth. Not the bargain. The protection."

She met his eyes. "He's separating them. Making people ask: if the protection isn't real, what *is* the bargain buying?"

"Exactly."

They moved together then, the rhythm they had fallen into over months of shared work. Theo dialed Yusuf while Wren pulled up the secure channel to Priya. The kitchen filled with the low hum of voices, the familiar cadence of emergency coordination.

Yusuf answered on the second ring. "Tell me you've seen it."

"Just now. Teaser document. Three pages. He's calling the protection a myth."

Silence on the line. Then Yusuf's voice, flat: "He sent it to the council listserv. All nine members. Walt Pruitt forwarded it to me with seven question marks and the word *help*."

"Walt's on the council listserv?"

"Since the ring. He insisted. Said if the town was going to live with the truth, he wanted to see the mail as it arrived." A pause. "He's not wrong. The panic started at 6:52. Three council members have already replied asking if we need to issue a statement. Two more want an emergency session before noon."

"We don't have a statement," Theo said. "We have a half-finished archive and a publication timeline that just got accelerated by months."

"We have the truth," Yusuf said. "Or we will. The question is whether we can get it out before Drake's version hardens into consensus."

"Can we?" Wren asked, voice low beside Theo.

Another silence. Longer this time. "I don't know. But I know we have to try. Emergency session. Nine a.m. Council chambers. I'll send the notice. You two get here as fast as you can."

The line disconnected. Theo set his phone down hard enough to make the table jump.

Wren was already standing, moving toward the stairs. "I'll be down in five. Wake Mara and Caleb. Dev needs to be at the substation checking the evidence chain on the coordinate drift data."

"Theo."

He looked up. She was already halfway to the stairs, but she turned. Her face was pale, but her eyes were clear.

"We knew this was coming. We knew he had a partial record. We knew he'd weaponize it. The only thing that's changed is the timeline."

"Right. The timeline."

"And we're not starting from nothing. We have Adelaide's letter. We have Whitlock's letter. We have the ring testimony. We have Dev's forensic work on the toll redirection. We have the county index gap that brought you here in the first place."

Theo stood. "We have a teaser that makes the valley look like a cult."

"We have a teaser that makes the valley look like a cult *to people who don't know the rest*." She held his gaze. "The people who live here know better. The people who've lost memory know better. The people who got it back know better. Our job is to make the record complete enough that the rest doesn't matter."

She disappeared up the stairs. Theo stood alone in the kitchen, the cold coffee, the open laptop with its damning three pages. He thought of Harriet Marsh, sitting in her chair at the care facility in Millbrook, ten years of her life gone, unable to say where she had been or what had been taken. Nineteen years Drake had been building this. Nineteen years since the first attempt, the first failure, the first time he had reached for the valley's secret and come away with something he could use.

Theo's grandmother had been the cost of that learning curve.

He picked up his phone again. Dialed a number he knew by heart. The care facility's main line. The nurse who knew him. The wait while they checked if Harriet was awake, if she was having a good morning, if she could take a call.

"Hello, dear."

Her voice thin, distant, the same as always. But present. *Here*.

"Hi, Gram. I just wanted to hear your voice."

"Oh. Well. That's nice. Are you coming to visit soon? The roses are blooming early this year. Or late. I can never remember."

"Early, I think. I'll come soon. I promise."

"You always promise. You're a good boy, Theo. You always were."

The call ended. Theo set the phone down. His hands were shaking.

He went upstairs to change.

The council chambers were full by 8:55.

Not just the nine council members. Walt Pruitt sat in the front row of the gallery, notebook open, pen moving. Odette Reyes stood by the back wall, arms crossed, her store apron still dusted with flour. Mara Voss and Caleb Reyes arrived together, Mara's hand on Caleb's arm, both wearing the careful neutrality of people who had lived through too many emergencies to panic but too few to trust calm. Priya Nair flanked the dais, uniform crisp, radio silent at her hip. Dev Nair slipped in through the side door five minutes before the hour, substation badge clipped to his belt, a sheaf of printouts in his hand.

Denise sat in the second row. She looked older than she had a year ago, the freed bargain finally catching up to her in the ordinary way of time. But her posture was straight, her eyes clear. She had lived with the secret for fifty years. She could survive a teaser document.

Yusuf called the session to order at nine sharp. No gavel. Just his voice, steady in the quiet room.

"Everyone has seen the document. Corwin Drake has published a teaser challenging the fundamental premise of Amity Falls' safety. He calls the protection a myth. He calls the bargain a control mechanism. He promises a full report."

He let that sit. The room did not murmur. The room had learned to wait.

"Our publication timeline," Yusuf continued, "was six months. Phased release. Community sessions first. County coordination. State-level notification for the index restriction. We do not have six months. We have whatever time it takes for Drake's full report to drop."

"Can we stop it?" Council member Hernandez asked. "Injunction? Cease and desist?"

Theo stood from his seat beside Wren. The gallery turned. He felt the weight of their attention, the valley's attention, the way it had felt the first time he walked into the archives and asked for the restricted file.

"Drake isn't publishing classified material," Theo said. "He's publishing an analysis of public data. Satellite imagery. Agricultural records. Flood statistics. County health reports. All of it legally obtainable. His interpretation is wrong, but it's not actionable. You cannot enjoin a wrong interpretation."

"Can we counter-publish?" Council member Okafor asked. "Release our own data?"

"We're not ready," Wren said. She stood too, moving to stand beside Theo. "The archive isn't complete. The lineage records aren't verified. Adelaide's letter and Whitlock's letter need contextual annotation. The ring testimony needs redaction for the volunteers who asked for anonymity. If we release a partial record to counter a partial record, we become exactly what Drake says we are: people managing a narrative."

"So we wait?" Hernandez. "We let him define the story?"

"We finish," Yusuf said. "We finish faster. Dev, what's the status on the coordinate drift forensic?"

Dev stepped forward. "The satellite data Drake used is real. USGS, NOAA, county GIS. He didn't fabricate it. But he stopped at the divergence. He didn't run the control comparison against the three nearest towns with similar aquifer profiles and microclimates. I did. Amity Falls diverges. The other three don't. The divergence correlates exactly with the bargain renewal dates in the record Adelaide left and Whitlock confirmed."

"Can you prove that in a way a court would accept?"

"In a way a peer-reviewed journal would accept. Which is better. Courts follow journals. Journals follow data."

"Good. Priya?"

Priya stepped forward. "I've contacted the county sheriff. Drake's teaser went out via a mailing list registered to a shell LLC in Delaware. The IP trace routes through three VPNs. But the LLC's registered agent is a name we know from the Millbrook case. Drake's pattern is consistent. He builds infrastructure years in advance. The sheriff is opening a file. Not an investigation yet. A file."

"Can we get a restraining order on the full report?"

"On what grounds? He hasn't published it. He hasn't threatened violence. He hasn't defamed a specific person. He's published a data analysis with a provocative conclusion. That's not illegal. It's not even unusual."

The room was quiet. Walt Pruitt raised his hand.

"Yes, Walt."

"Question. Not a challenge. Just... if the protection is a myth, like he says, then what *are* we? What have we been doing? What have we been *paying*?"

The question hung. Denise stood slowly. The room turned to her.

"Walt," she said. "I renewed the bargain for fifty years. I took memories from people I loved. I took two years from my grandson's husband. I did it because the alternative was watching this valley drown, burn, sicken, and die. The protection is not a myth. The bargain is not a story. The cost is real. I am the receipt."

No one spoke. Walt nodded, wrote in his notebook.

"The cost is real," Theo said. "And the cost is the record. Every memory taken. Every debt paid. Every lineage gift used. That's what we're compiling. That's what Drake has a fragment of. That's what we will publish in full."

"When?" Okafor asked.

"Soon," Wren said. "We need two weeks. Maybe three. The coordinate drift analysis Dev did needs to be formatted. The lineage verification needs to be cross-checked against the ring testimony. The county index restriction needs a formal request to the state archivist, which Theo can file today. The annotation on Adelaide's and Whitlock's letters needs to be written so no one can say we're hiding context."

"Two weeks," Yusuf said. "Drake won't wait two weeks."

"Then we give him something to wait *for*," Theo said. "We announce the archive. We announce the publication date. We announce the public reckoning. We make the event the story, not his teaser."

"And if he publishes before our date?"

"Then we publish what we have," Wren said. "Incomplete. Unannotated. Raw. And we say: this is the record. Judge for yourselves. But we say it *first*."

A murmur moved through the gallery. Not panic. Calculation.

"Risky," Hernandez said.

"All of this is risky," Caleb said from the front row. "I lost two years of my life to a secret bargain. My wife spent months thinking I was a stranger. My friends ran a ritual that could have killed them to get my memory back. The risk isn't publishing. The risk is *not* publishing."

Mara squeezed his arm. "He's right. The valley has survived secrets. It won't survive being defined by someone else's half-truth."

Yusuf nodded. "Two weeks. Target publication date: the fifteenth. That gives us fourteen days. Dev, coordinate with Theo on the data package. Wren, coordinate with Priya on the lineage verification and redaction. Mara, Caleb, you're the community liaisons. Odette, Denise, Walt - you're the living record. Anyone who wants to tell their story on the record, we'll make space. Anyone who doesn't, we'll protect."

"What about Drake?" Okafor asked.

"We watch," Yusuf said. "We document. We prepare. And we finish the work."

The session broke at 9:47. People moved toward the doors with purpose, not panic. The valley had learned to move when the ground shifted.

Theo stayed at the dais, packing his laptop. Wren stayed beside him. Dev approached, printouts in hand.

"The coordinate drift analysis," Dev said. "I've got the raw files on the substation server. Encrypted. Theo, you'll need the decryption key. It's in the usual place."

"Usual place?"

"Under the third floorboard in the archive basement. Left side. You know the one."

Theo blinked. "You put the decryption key under a floorboard?"

"Where else would I put it? The cloud? Drake owns half the cloud. The USB drive in my pocket? I lose pockets. The floorboard has been there since 1923. Nobody looks under floorboards."

Wren smiled, a real one. "Dev, you're ridiculous."

"I'm thorough. There's a difference."

Priya appeared at Dev's shoulder. "The sheriff wants a meeting. Today. Two p.m. He wants to understand what Drake's teaser means for county jurisdiction."

"I'll go," Theo said. "Wren, you stay on the archive?"

"I'll stay. Mara and Caleb can handle community outreach. Odette can coordinate the store as information hub. Denise..." Wren glanced at the back row where Denise sat talking quietly with Walt. "Denise knows what she's doing."

"She always does," Priya said. "Two p.m. Sheriff's office. I'll drive."

Theo nodded. "Dev, get me that key. Wren, I'll be back by four. We'll regroup at the archive."

They moved out of the chambers together, the valley's working core dispersing to their tasks. Theo felt the familiar surge - not adrenaline, not fear. Clarity. The work had a shape now. A deadline. A counter-move.

Outside, the morning had brightened. The mountains stood sharp against blue. The air smelled of pine and apple blossom, the orchard starting its spring push. Ordinary. Beautiful. A morning that made people forget they lived on borrowed time.

Theo's phone vibrated. A text from an unknown number.

*Nice teaser response. The floorboard is clever. But floorboards burn. - C.D.*

He showed it to Wren. She read it, her face unchanging.

"He's watching the substation. Or Dev's phone. Or both."

"Or he's guessing. Floorboards are a classic."

"Drake doesn't guess. He knows."

They reached the archive building, the repurposed library on Main Street. The new sign hadn't gone up yet - *Amity Falls Historical Archive and Records Repository* - but the door was unlocked, the lights on, the worktables covered in documents, drives, cables, the chaotic abundance of truth being assembled.

Mara and Caleb were already at the center table, heads together over a spreadsheet. Odette stood at the counter, phone to her ear, nodding at something the caller said. Denise sat in the corner chair, a cup of tea beside her, reading a manuscript page with the intensity of someone proofreading her own eulogy.

Walt Pruitt came in behind Theo and Wren, notebook still in hand. He looked around the room, at the shelves being filled, the boxes being unpacked, the history being made visible.

"Huh," he said. "So this is it. The whole thing."

"This is it," Wren said. "Come on. I'll show you the redaction protocol for the ring volunteers."

They moved to a side table. Theo watched them go, then turned to his own workstation. The county index restriction request. The state archivist's contact. The formal language that would turn a standing arrangement into a documented exception, the legal key to the valley's visibility.

His fingers found the keys. The screen filled with the template he had drafted weeks ago, waiting for the moment. The moment was now.

He typed: *Request for Declassification of County Index Restriction, Amity Falls Entry. Basis: Public Interest Disclosure Under State Records Act Section 14.3. Supporting Documentation Attached.*

He attached Dev's coordinate drift analysis. The ring testimony summary. Adelaide's letter. Whitlock's letter. The toll redirection forensic. The lineage verification matrix.

He hit send.

The confirmation popped up: *Submitted. Tracking Number: CF-2024-047891. Estimated Response: 10 Business Days.*

Ten business days. They had fourteen calendar days.

Close. Too close.

But it was sent. The machinery of ordinary law was turning. Drake had spent nineteen years building his leverage in shadows. Theo had spent months building a paper trail in light.

He stood, stretched, rolled his shoulders. The sheriff's meeting at two. The archive work until then. The publication deadline looming.

He looked at Wren across the room. She was explaining something to Walt, her hands moving, her voice steady. Walt was writing, nodding, asking questions. Denise had set down her tea and was listening too, one ear turned toward them.

The valley was watching. The valley was waiting. The valley was *working*.

Theo went to his station and opened the coordinate drift files. The data spread across the screen, clean and cold and true. He began to write the annotation that would make it readable, make it undeniable, make it the record that would stand when Drake's teaser crumbled.

Outside, the mountains held their shape. The orchard bloomed. The valley breathed.

And the work continued.
