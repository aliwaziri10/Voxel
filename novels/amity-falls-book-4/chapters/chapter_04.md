The drive to Millbrook took forty minutes on a good day. Today the rain came down in sheets the wipers couldn't clear fast enough, and Theo held his speed five under the limit the whole way. Wren watched the treeline blur into a gray wall, the valley disappearing behind them mile by mile. She had never been this far out without Mara or Caleb. The ordinariness of it — highway, strip mall, a diner with a neon sign flickering PIE — felt like a different world entirely.

"You've been quiet," Theo said. His hands stayed loose on the wheel, one thumb rubbing the seam of the steering wheel cover.

"I'm thinking," Wren said. "About the folio. About what happens if we don't find it before he does."

Theo nodded once. "Fair."

They pulled into the Millbrook County Records Building just as the rain slowed to a drizzle. The building sat low and square, brick faced with limestone — municipal architecture meant to look permanent, and mostly succeeding. Inside, the air smelled of paper dust and floor wax. A security guard at the desk didn't look up from his crossword.

Theo led her past the public research room, through a door marked STAFF ONLY, down a corridor where fluorescent lights hummed. He stopped at a cage door with a heavy padlock, fished a key ring from his belt, and opened it.

"Restricted access," he said. "Sealed boxes, evidence from cold cases, things that got flagged during the digitization project and never unflagged."

The cage was maybe twelve by twelve. Metal shelving units ran floor to ceiling, each shelf packed with banker's boxes in varying states of decay. Labels faced outward: *Hawthorne Property Dispute 1987*, *Missing Persons — Unresolved 1992–1998*, *Tax Lien Forfeitures 1970s*.

Theo walked to the third shelf, fourth box from the left. The label read *Amity Falls Jurisdictional Anomaly — SEALED 2004*.

"County clerk's office flagged it when the GIS mapping project went live," Theo said, pulling the box down. "Satellite showed a populated area that didn't correspond to any incorporated municipality. Survey crew went out, found the valley, found the discrepancy. Somebody upstairs decided to seal the file rather than explain why a town of three hundred people doesn't exist on the map."

He set the box on a folding table bolted to the wall. The cardboard was brittle, the tape yellowed. Inside, manila folders sat in neat rows.

"The folio wasn't in here when I checked last year," Theo said. "But the inventory sheet lists it. Item twelve: *Bound manuscript, unknown origin, recovered from Amity Falls property sale 1923*."

Wren leaned over his shoulder. The inventory sheet was typewritten, the ink faded to sepia. Item twelve had a check mark in the column marked *RETURNED TO ORIGIN*.

"Returned to origin," she read. "What does that mean? Returned to the valley?"

"Could be," Theo said. "Could mean returned to the family that sold the property. The DeWitts. They held land on the east ridge before the county seized it for back taxes. The sale record is in folder three."

He pulled the folder. The deed was handwritten on vellum, the script spidery and faint. *Thomas DeWitt to Millbrook County, consideration $1.00 and other valuable considerations.* The date stamp was smudged but legible enough: *March 14, 1923*.

"Other valuable considerations," Wren said. "That's how they wrote it when the bargain was part of the transaction."

"Exactly," Theo said. "The DeWitts didn't just sell land. They sold the obligation that came with it. The county didn't know what it was buying. By the time anyone asked questions, the people who could answer them were dead."

He flipped to the next page. A handwritten note, different ink, different hand: *Folio recovered from DeWitt attic during clearance. Sealed per County Counsel directive 04/1923. Not for public access.*

"County Counsel directive," Wren said. "Someone knew what it was."

"Or someone knew it was dangerous," Theo said. "The folio went into the sealed archive in '23. The inventory sheet says it was returned to origin in '87. Sixty-four years later."

"Nineteen eighty-seven," Wren said. "That's the year my mother was born."

Theo looked at her. "Your mother was from the valley?"

"My grandmother was," Wren said. "My mother left. Married a man from outside. She didn't want me raised in the bargain. She thought distance would protect me." She pressed her fingers to the paper. "It didn't."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "My grandmother disappeared in 1987."

The words hung in the air between them, heavier than the rain on the roof.

"She didn't die," Theo continued. "She didn't leave a note. She walked out of her house in Millbrook one Tuesday morning and never came back. Her car was found at the train station. Ticket stub in the glove box for the 8:15 to Chicago. But she never boarded. The conductor remembered her on the platform. Said she was talking to someone. He couldn't describe the person. Just — a man in a gray coat."

Wren didn't speak. She let the silence hold.

"The police looked for six months," Theo said. "Cold case unit reopened it twice. I've read every file. There's nothing. No body, no witnesses, no financial trail. She vanished into air." He tapped the deed. "Nineteen eighty-seven. The year the folio was returned to origin. The year your mother was born. The year my grandmother disappeared."

"Coincidence," Wren said, but her voice lacked conviction.

"Maybe," Theo said. "But I've been chasing coincidences for eight years. This is the first one that ever led somewhere real."

He pulled another folder from the box. *Correspondence — County Counsel to State Archives, 1987.*

The top letter was on state letterhead. *Re: Amity Falls Manuscript. Per your request, the item has been transferred to the custody of the State Historical Society, restricted collection. Access requires authorization from the Office of the Attorney General.*

"State Historical Society," Wren said. "In the capital?"

"Three hours from here," Theo said. "I've been twice. They have a card for it in their catalog. *Manuscript, Amity Falls, circa 1820. Restricted — genealogical research only.* But when I asked to see it, they said it was deaccessioned in 2003. Sold to a private collector."

"Drake."

"Has to be," Theo said. "He's been buying up every scrap of valley history he can find for two decades. Estate sales, private dealers, the black market in occult Americana. He has money nobody asks about."

Wren's throat tightened. "So the complete history of the bargain — the true terms, the names of everyone who renewed it, the debt ledger — is sitting in Corwin Drake's private collection."

"Or it was," Theo said. "He sent the message to the council this morning. You haven't checked your phone."

Wren fished it from her pocket. The screen lit up with a notification from the valley's secure channel. A single message, timestamped 06:47.

*To the Council of Amity Falls:*

*I hold the complete administrative record of the bargain's renewals from 1823 to present. Three hundred twelve pages. Names, dates, debts paid, memories taken. The folio your ancestors hid. The ledger Denise Varela maintained for fifty years. The letters of Ambrose Whitlock and Adelaide Hart.*

*I intend to publish this record in thirty days. Unless, before that deadline, you provide me with the original bargain terms — the founding document signed by the first Warden of the First Debt. The document that defines the true scope of the exchange. The document your history claims was lost.*

*You have thirty days to decide what the world learns about Amity Falls. And what it learns about you.*

*C. Drake*

Wren read it twice. The rain hammered the cage's wire mesh roof.

"Thirty days," she said.

"Thirty days," Theo repeated. "He's not asking for money. He's not asking for power. He wants the one thing you don't have — the original founding document. The thing Whitlock hid, the thing Adelaide couldn't find, the thing nobody has seen in two hundred years."

"Because it doesn't exist," Wren said. "Or if it does, it's not in the valley. Whitlock's letter said he buried the complete terms rather than destroy them. He didn't say where."

"Then we have thirty days to find it," Theo said. "Or thirty days to write our own version and publish first."

"Publish first," Wren said. "That's the plan. Yusuf said —"

"Yusuf backs you," Theo said. "But the council isn't just Yusuf. And the valley isn't just the council. Three hundred days ago, nobody outside the valley knew the bargain existed. In thirty days, the world could know everything. Every name. Every memory taken. Every debt paid in someone else's mind."

He closed the folder. His face was calm, but his jaw was tight.

"My grandmother," he said. "The man in the gray coat. The conductor said he looked like he belonged there. Like he was waiting for her. Not like a stranger. Like someone she knew."

Wren felt the cold settle in her chest. "You think it was a Finder."

"I think it was someone who knew how to make a person disappear," Theo said. "Someone who knew the toll could be redirected. Someone who needed her gone for a reason that mattered enough to pay that price."

"Drake?"

"Drake was twenty-three in 1987," Theo said. "Living in Millbrook. His father was a county clerk. He had access to the sealed files. He had motive — his family lost land to the valley in the twenties, same as the DeWitts. And he had the one thing nobody else had: he knew the bargain was real."

Wren stared at him. "You've had this theory for years. Why didn't you say anything before?"

"Because it was a theory," Theo said. "Built on gaps and guesses. No evidence. No proof. Just a missing grandmother and a sealed file and a man who showed up twenty years later with a collection of valley secrets and a grin like he'd won a game nobody else knew they were playing." He met her eyes. "Now I have a witness. A Finder who can locate lost things. A woman who knows the valley's history better than anyone alive. And a deadline that makes it real."

The cage felt smaller suddenly. The air thicker.

"Finders locate lost things," Wren said. "The cost is a memory of the search. If I look for the founding document — if I look for your grandmother — I pay with a memory of looking."

"I know the cost," Theo said. "I've read every account of Finder debt in the valley records. I know what it takes. I know what it costs."

"Do you?" Wren's voice sharpened. "Do you know what it feels like to lose the memory of your own mother's face because you looked for a book she hid? Do you know what it's like to wake up and not remember why your hands are shaking, only that they are?"

Theo didn't flinch. "No. I don't. But I know what it's like to spend eight years staring at a cold case file, memorizing every page, every timestamp, every redacted name, and still not knowing the one thing that matters. Where she went. Why. Who took her." His voice dropped. "I know what it's like to build your whole life around a question you can't answer. To become the person who finds things for other people because nobody ever found the thing for you."

The rain stopped. The silence that followed was absolute.

Wren looked at the sealed box, the folders, the deed with its careful script. *Other valuable considerations.* The bargain written into property law, hidden in plain sight.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. We go to the State Historical Society. We find out who bought the folio in 2003. We trace it to Drake. And we find the founding document before he publishes."

"Thirty days," Theo said.

"Thirty days," Wren agreed.

She pulled out her phone again, typed a message to Yusuf: *Drake has the folio. Thirty-day deadline. Need council session ASAP.*

The response came back in under a minute: *Already called. 7 PM tonight. Schoolhouse. Bring Marsh.*

Wren pocketed the phone. "Council at seven. We should get back."

Theo replaced the folders, taped the box shut, returned it to the shelf. He locked the cage, checked the padlock twice. Procedural. Thorough. The way he did everything.

As they walked back down the corridor, Wren said, "The man in the gray coat. The conductor said your grandmother was talking to him. Did the report say what they talked about?"

Theo paused, his hand on the staff door. "The conductor said she looked scared. But she didn't run. She handed him something. A small envelope. He took it, put it in his pocket, and they walked toward the tracks together. She never looked back."

"An envelope."

"Could have been anything," Theo said. "Letters. Photos. Money. The police never found it. Never found him."

They exited into the drizzle. The parking lot was slick, the trees dripping. Theo unlocked his car, a sensible sedan with a dent in the rear quarter panel.

"Get in," he said. "I'll drive."

Wren got in. The heater blew warm air that smelled of old coffee and rain. She watched Theo start the engine, check his mirrors, pull onto the road.

"Your grandmother's name," she said.

"Eleanor," he said. "Eleanor Marsh. She was forty-three. She liked crossword puzzles and terrible romance novels. She made the worst coffee I've ever tasted, and she drank three cups every morning. She had a laugh like a startled bird. And she loved this county — its history, its secrets, its terrible roads — more than anything." He glanced at Wren, just for a second. "She was the one who taught me how to file things. How to find things in the noise. She said every story leaves a trace. You just have to be patient enough to follow it."

Wren thought of her own grandmother. Of the stories she hadn't told. The truths she'd kept buried for her own good.

"My grandmother," Wren said, "never taught me anything. She just — disappeared. Into the bargain. Into the debt. She paid for things she never told me about, and I spent my whole childhood thinking she was just quiet. Thinking the silence was normal."

"Silence is never normal," Theo said. "It's just a different kind of noise."

The highway stretched ahead, the valley waiting at the end of it. Thirty days. Three hundred twelve pages of names and debts and memories. A founding document that might not exist. An antagonist who had spent nineteen years building a weapon out of the truth.

And beside her, a man who had spent eight years chasing a ghost, who knew the cost of finding things and still wanted to help her pay it.

Wren leaned her head back against the headrest. The rain started again, harder this time, drumming on the roof.

"Tell me about the crossword puzzles," she said.

Theo snorted. "You don't want to hear about crossword puzzles."

"I do," she said. "Tell me."

So he did. And for forty minutes, the valley didn't exist. There was only the road, and the rain, and a story about a woman who liked terrible romance novels and made terrible coffee and disappeared into a gray coat on a Tuesday in 1987, leaving behind a grandson who never stopped looking.

Wren listened. And she didn't take a single memory of it.
