The council chamber smelled of lemon oil and old paper, the same scent that had greeted Wren at every meeting since she was tall enough to see over the table's edge. Today, though, the air carried something sharper — anticipation, maybe, or that distinct static that gathered when a valley secret was about to meet daylight.

She stood at the presentation end of the room, her notes arranged in the precise order Theo had helped her establish: chronological, sourced, annotated with every gap they'd found and every gap they hadn't. The complete record. Two hundred and forty-seven pages, bound in plain blue covers, sitting in a stack beside the podium.

Thirty-two chairs filled the room. Council members, department heads, the sheriff's deputy, Odette from the store, Walt Pruitt in his habitual second-row seat with a notebook open on his knees. Theo sat in the front row, not at the table — he'd insisted on that distinction, *observer, not participant* — but close enough that she could see the pen poised over his own pad, the way his thumb rested against the paper's edge.

Yusuf sat at the table's center, the gavel loose in his fingers. He looked older than he had a year ago, the lines around his eyes deeper, but his posture held the same deliberate calm she'd watched him settle into after the ring ritual. No hesitation. No processing time performed for the room's benefit.

"Wren," he said, and the single syllable carried the weight of every council opening she'd ever witnessed. "The floor is yours."

She exhaled. Spoke.

"What I'm asking for is permission to publish the complete historical record of Amity Falls' bargain — every renewal, every term, every debt paid and every debt redirected — before Corwin Drake publishes his version."

The room didn't gasp. Amity Falls didn't gasp. But the silence that followed had a different texture than the usual working quiet. Walt's pen stopped moving. Odette's hands, folded on the table, went still.

"Drake's version," Councilwoman Haines repeated, her voice pitched to carry. "You're treating his manuscript as a given. We don't know what he has."

"We know he has nineteen years of research," Wren said. "We know he was in Millbrook when the first attempt failed. We know he's been contacting publishers for the last three weeks — Theo confirmed that through his county contacts." She glanced sideways. Theo nodded once, minimal. "And we know his advantage depends on being first. If we publish the complete record ourselves, honestly and in full, his partial version becomes what it is: incomplete, contextualized, and dated."

"And if his version contains things ours doesn't?" Haines pressed. "Things we've missed?"

"Then we correct the record," Wren said. "That's how publishing works. That's how truth works. You don't suppress the incomplete version by hiding the complete one. You answer it."

A murmur moved through the room. Not dissent, exactly. The sound of minds turning over a proposition that, six months ago, would have been unthinkable.

Yusuf set the gavel down. The sound was soft, final.

"The council voted unanimously three months ago to authorize the Archive's creation," he said. "The motion included language about 'public accessibility of the valley's complete historical record.' I read that as a mandate to publish. If the council wishes to amend that mandate, the floor is open for a new motion."

He looked around the table. One beat. Two. Three.

No one moved. No one spoke.

"Then the mandate stands," Yusuf said. "Wren, you have the council's backing. Theo, your office will coordinate with the county on ISBN registration and distribution channels?"

"Already in motion," Theo said. "I've spoken with the Millbrook library system. They're prepared to carry it as a special collection. The university press in Castleton has expressed interest in a scholarly edition — they'd handle academic distribution while we manage local sales through the Archive."

"Timeline," Yusuf said.

"Eight weeks to print," Theo said. "Four to distribution. Drake's people have been quoting twelve to fourteen weeks to their publishers. We beat them by a month at minimum."

"Good." Yusuf's gaze returned to Wren. "You'll want to speak to the 'why' — publicly. The valley deserves to hear it from you."

The words landed differently than she expected. Not a question. Not a suggestion. A statement of fact, delivered with the same certainty he'd brought to the ring ritual's closing moments: *We do this now. We do it together. We do it right.*

The memory surfaced without invitation — her mother's voice, kitchen-table serious, the summer Wren turned sixteen and asked why the valley didn't just *tell people*.

*"Because some truths don't protect you when you know them, Wren. They protect you by staying unknown. The bargain isn't a secret because it's shameful. It's a secret because it's fragile. And you — you're a Finder. Every time you look for something, you pay with a piece of what you already know. Do you understand what that means for someone who goes looking for the valley's deepest debts?"*

She'd been furious. Sixteen and furious, convinced her mother was hiding things *for her own good*, the phrase that had shaped her childhood into a series of locked doors. Now, standing in the council chamber with the complete record bound and ready, the memory carried a different weight. Her mother hadn't been wrong. The gift *did* take. Every search for a lost thing — a missing wedding ring, a child wandered into the woods, the true terms of a century-old bargain — exacted its toll in memories Wren could feel slipping: the exact shade of her father's tie at her graduation, the smell of rain on the orchard road the night Caleb's memory returned, the way Theo's laugh sounded the first time she'd made him genuinely smile.

She'd paid for this publication with pieces of herself she couldn't name. Would pay more before the printed copies reached the shelves.

"Wren?"

Yusuf's voice pulled her back. The room had gone very still. Everyone watching her.

She gripped the podium's edges. Felt the grain of the wood, the faint ridge where someone had once carved initials. *Present. Here. Now.*

"My mother used to say the bargain was fragile," she said. "That knowing it didn't make you safer — it made you responsible. She was right about the responsibility part. But she was wrong about the safety." She looked across the faces: Haines, Odette, Walt scribbling again, the deputy sheriff with his arms crossed, Theo watching her with an expression she couldn't quite read. "The valley hasn't been safe for a long time. Not from the bargain, and not from the secrets we kept to protect it. Drake has a partial record. He's going to publish it. The only question is whether the valley's first full accounting comes from us — honest, complete, and *ours* — or from someone who wants to weaponize what we hid."

She paused. Breath.

"I'm a Finder. My job is to locate what's lost. The true terms of this bargain have been lost for longer than any of us have been alive. I found them. So did Theo. So did Mara, and Caleb, and Dev, and Priya, and Denise, and everyone who stood in that ring a year ago. We found them together. And now we tell the truth together. That's the plan. That's the whole plan."

Silence. Then Walt Pruitt's hand rose, slow and deliberate.

"Yes, Walt."

"If we publish everything," Walt said, his voice carrying the rough patience of a man who'd spent forty years fixing other people's plumbing, "what stops someone from reading it and deciding the bargain's a bad deal? What stops the whole thing from unraveling?"

The question everyone had been holding. The question *she* had been holding.

Wren glanced at Yusuf. He didn't look at her. Didn't nod. Just waited.

"The bargain unravels when people don't trust it," she said. "Trust requires knowing what you're trusting. We've been asking the valley to trust a deal they couldn't read, administered by people they couldn't question, paid for with memories they couldn't remember losing. That's not trust. That's coercion with better manners." She straightened. "If the bargain can't survive being known, it doesn't deserve to survive. But I think it can. I think the valley is stronger than the secrets we kept for it."

Walt held her gaze a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, and picked up his pen.

"Motion to proceed stands," Yusuf said. "Wren, Theo — coordinate the public announcement. I want it in the *Millbrook Gazette* by Friday, the *Castleton Chronicle* by Monday. Walt, you'll handle the community meeting schedule?"

"Already drafted," Walt said. "Three sessions. Fire hall, school gym, church basement. Give everyone a seat."

"Good." Yusuf rose, the chair scraping loud in the quiet. "Then we're done here. Thank you, all of you."

The room exhaled. Chairs scraped. Papers gathered. Conversations started in low tones — the sound of a valley beginning to process a truth it had never been allowed to hold.

Wren stayed at the podium until the last of them filed out. Odette paused beside her, smelling of peppermint and card stock.

"You did good, honey," Odette said. "Your mama would've hated it. But she'd have been proud too."

The observation landed sideways. Wren's throat tightened. "You knew her better than I did, at the end."

"I knew her different," Odette corrected. "She came in every Tuesday for the special blend. Always asked for extra cinnamon. Said it helped her think." Odette's eyes crinkled. "She talked about you. Not the details — never the details. But the shape of you. How you asked questions she couldn't answer. How she wanted to give you the world and knew she could only give you the valley." A pause. "She wasn't trying to keep you small, Wren. She was trying to keep you *here*. There's a difference."

Wren couldn't speak. The memory-cost of this conversation was already settling — she could feel the space where her mother's cinnamon-scented Tuesday visits used to live, fading into generic outline.

Odette squeezed her shoulder. "Go on. Your young man's waiting."

Theo was indeed waiting — leaning against the hallway's stone wall, one ankle crossed over the other, the picture of archivist patience. He pushed off when she emerged.

"Eight weeks," he said. "You heard him."

"I heard him." She fell into step beside him, the corridor's cool air raising gooseflesh on her arms. "You heard me. The 'telling the truth together' part."

"I heard you." His tone was dry, but his eyes held something warmer. "Also the part where you quoted your mother to a room full of people who've known you since you were in pigtails. Bold move."

"Was it?"

"For you? Yeah. You usually let the research speak." He held the door for her, the afternoon light sudden and sharp after the chamber's dimness. "Shift happening?"

She considered the question as they walked the gravel path toward the Archive building — the converted barn where the complete record would live, where the public sessions would begin, where she and Theo had spent the last three months turning chaos into catalog.

"Maybe," she said. "Or maybe the research finally spoke loud enough that I had to get out of its way."

Theo's laugh was quiet. "That's the version you'll put in the acknowledgments?"

"That's the version that's true." She stopped at the Archive's threshold, the new sign still smelling of fresh paint: *AMITY FALLS HISTORICAL ARCHIVE — EST. 2024*. "The other version — the one where I was brave all along — that's the one Drake would write. The one where I'm the hero of my own story, and the valley's just backdrop."

"And the version where you're terrified but do it anyway?"

"That one doesn't make a good story either. Too messy."

"Messy's what makes it real." He stepped inside, turning to hold the door wider. "Come on. We've got a publication timeline to build. And I believe you promised me the story of the cinnamon tea."

She followed him in, the door settling shut behind them with a solid thunk. The Archive's main room stretched ahead — shelves already filling, the big table cleared for layout work, the window framing the valley's ridgeline against a sky going pale with evening.

Three months ago, this room had held nothing but dust and the ghost of a bargain no one dared name. Now it held the truth, bound and waiting. And she held the memory of her mother's warning, thinned by the finding but not erased — *some truths don't protect you when you know them* — alongside the newer, harder certainty that had carried her through the council chamber: *but the lies protect no one at all.*

"Cinnamon tea," she said, pulling out a chair. "Right. It started with a kettle my grandmother brought from the old country..."

She talked, and Theo listened, and the valley's first honest evening began.
