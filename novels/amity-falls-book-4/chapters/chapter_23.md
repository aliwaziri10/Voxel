The drive to the state historical society took three hours each way if traffic held. Wren had mapped it the night before, printed the directions, and set the stack of folders on the passenger seat of Theo's car. He had offered to drive. She had accepted without the hesitation that would have stopped her six months ago.

The valley fell away behind them, the ridge line smoothing into the wider flattening of the foothills. By the time they reached the interstate the trees had thinned to scattered stands of oak and the sky opened into the pale washed blue of late autumn. Wren watched the mile markers tick past. Her fingers rested on the folder containing the photocopied pages from Adelaide's ledger, the one that showed the gap where the 2003 folio should have been.

"How's your grandmother?" she asked, not looking over.

Theo's hands didn't tighten on the wheel. "Same. The home says she had a good week. She remembered my aunt's name three days in a row. She asked about the garden at the old house." He paused. "She still doesn't know where she was for those ten years. She never will."

Wren turned her head. The profile of his face was familiar now, the line of his jaw, the faint crease between his eyebrows when he drove into the sun. "Does that bother you? That the answer might just be gone."

"It used to. I thought if I found the right record, the right witness, the right piece of paper, it would click into place. She'd get it back." He glanced at her, quick and sideways. "Now I think some things don't come back. You learn to live in the shape of the absence."

The words settled in the car like something heavy placed carefully on a shelf. Wren understood that shape. She had grown up inside it, the space where her father should have been, the space where her grandmother's stories stopped before they reached the hard parts. Her mother had called it protection. Wren had come to call it something else.

"What changed your mind?" she asked.

Theo was quiet long enough that she thought he might not answer. Then: "You did. Watching you work. You don't chase the return. You document the loss. You make the absence legible. That's a different kind of honor."

The praise landed strangely, warm and unearned. Wren looked down at her hands. "I'm just writing it down. So the next person doesn't have to guess."

"That's not nothing."

The interstate stretched ahead, a gray ribbon stitched between fields turning brown for winter. A flock of starlings rose from a corn stubble field, wheeled once, and settled again. The car's heater blew warm air that smelled of dust and the pine air freshener Theo had clipped to the vent.

"Twelve days," Wren said.

"Twelve," Theo agreed.

"Drake's deadline. He said he'd publish on the fifteenth. Today's the third."

"Eleven days if you count today. Twelve if you don't." Theo's voice was dry. "I've always counted the day of. Gives you an extra twenty-four hours of panic."

"Which count are we using?"

"The generous one. We'll need it."

They fell into silence again, but it was not the silence of strangers. It was the silence of people who had spent weeks sitting across a table from each other, transcribing cramped handwriting, cross-referencing land deeds with census records, matching the dates of bargain renewals to the names of the Wardens who had witnessed them. Wren knew the rhythm of his breathing when he concentrated. She knew the way he tapped his pen twice against the page before he wrote a note. He knew she pressed her thumb to the corner of a page when she was about to turn it, a small ritual of respect for the paper.

The historical society occupied a limestone building on the capitol's east side, its columns weathered to the color of old bone. Inside, the reading room smelled of paper dust and lemon oil. A woman at the reference desk, her hair pinned in a silver coil, looked up over bifocals when they approached.

"State Historical Society. How can I help you?"

Theo stepped forward with the ease of someone who had done this in a dozen counties. "We're researching a manuscript acquisition from 2003. A folio of personal correspondence, possibly related to the Amity Falls area. We believe it was purchased at auction or through a private dealer."

The woman's expression didn't change. "Name of the collection?"

"Adelaide Voss. She was a Warden. The folio would have been dated roughly 1950s through 1970s."

The woman typed, her nails clicking on the keys. The monitor's glow reflected in her glasses. "Adelaide Voss. We have a Voss family collection. Diaries, land records, some correspondence. Acquired 1998. Donated by a descendant." She scrolled. "No folio acquired in 2003 under that name. There's a gap in the accession log for March through June of that year. The archivist on leave. When she returned, the folio was catalogued but the acquisition file was empty. No donor name. No purchase record. Just the item description."

"Can we see the catalogue entry?"

"Of course. Let me pull the finding aid."

She left them at a table near the windows. The winter light fell thin across the surface. Wren opened her notebook. Theo sat beside her, close enough that his sleeve brushed hers when he reached for his own pen.

The finding aid arrived in a manila folder. The catalogue entry was sparse: *Folio, Adelaide Voss correspondence, 1952-1978. Acquired June 2003. Condition fair. Restricted access.* The restriction note had a date stamp: *July 2003. Per donor request.*

"Restricted access," Wren said. "Donor request. But there's no donor name."

"Convenient," Theo said. "Who processed the acquisition?"

The finding aid listed a name: *M. Harrow, Processing Archivist.*

"M. Harrow," Theo repeated. "Margaret Harrow. She retired in 2011. Lives in the suburbs, last I heard. I can look her up."

"Would she remember?"

"She might. Processing archivists see everything. They're the ones who notice when a file is empty." Theo wrote the name down. "But the restriction itself is interesting. July 2003. That's a month after the acquisition. Someone came forward fast to clamp it down."

"Or the restriction was there from the start and the catalogue just recorded it late."

"Possible. But the accession log gap suggests the paperwork wasn't done properly. Which means someone wanted it that way."

Wren traced the entry with her fingertip. The paper was cool. "Drake has this folio. Or he had it. He quoted from it in his letters to Yusuf. He knew things about the bargain renewals that only Adelaide would have written down."

"Then he got it from here, or from the dealer who sold it here. Or from the donor who restricted it." Theo folded his arms. "Three threads. The dealer. The donor. The archivist who processed it."

"Four," Wren said. "The person who stole it from the valley in the first place."

Theo looked at her. "You think it was stolen."

"I think Adelaide didn't give it away. She hid things. She hid the letter from Ambrose Whitlock. She hid the record of Denise's renewals. She wouldn't have let a folio of her own correspondence walk out the door unless she had no choice."

"Or unless she trusted the person she gave it to."

Wren shook her head. "She didn't trust easily. Not after what happened to Caleb. Not after what she found out about Denise."

The reference librarian returned with a second folder. "I pulled the correspondence file for the Voss collection. There's a letter from the donor's attorney. Dated June 2003. It accompanied the folio."

She laid it on the table. Wren picked it up. The letterhead was embossed: *Law Offices of Harlan & Associates, Capital City.* The body was typed, formal, signed with a flourish that obscured the name.

*Dear Ms. Harrow,*

*Enclosed please find the folio of correspondence belonging to the late Adelaide Voss, as instructed by my client. My client wishes to remain anonymous. The folio is donated to the Society with the following restriction: access limited to direct descendants of Adelaide Voss, or their authorized legal representatives, in perpetuity. No exceptions. My client has authorized me to state that the folio contains material of a sensitive personal nature and that unrestricted access would violate the privacy of living individuals.*

*Please confirm receipt and the implementation of the restriction.*

*Yours truly,*

*[Signature]*

*Partner, Harlan & Associates*

"No donor name," Theo said. "Just the law firm."

"Harlan & Associates," Wren repeated. "That's a big firm. Corporate, mostly. Real estate, trusts, estates."

"Expensive," Theo said. "Someone with money. Someone who wanted this buried but not destroyed. If they wanted it destroyed, they'd have burned it. Donating it with a restriction means they want it to exist. Just not where anyone can read it."

"Or they want the restriction to expire when the descendants die out."

"Adelaide's line runs through Mara. Through Caleb. Through their kids." Theo's pen scratched in his notebook. "The restriction holds as long as there's a living descendant. Which there will be for a long time."

"Unless the restriction is challenged. A court could override it. Public interest. Historical significance."

"Drake would know that. He'd know how to challenge it. He'd know the firm." Theo looked up. "Harlan & Associates. I can pull their client list from 2003. See who they represented in estate matters around Amity Falls."

"Can you do that from here?"

"I can request the records. They're not public, but a court order or a subpoena would get them. Or..." He hesitated. "I know a reporter at the *Capital Gazette*. She owes me a favor. She might be able to run a quiet inquiry."

Wren felt a small spike of alarm. "A reporter. You want to bring the press into this before we publish?"

"I want to know who paid a high-powered law firm to bury Adelaide's folio twelve days before Drake drops his file on the world. If that person is working with Drake, or if Drake is the donor, we need to know now. Not after he publishes."

The logic was sound. It was also the kind of calculated risk that made Wren's stomach tighten. She had spent a year learning to trust the process of documentation, the slow careful building of a record that could stand on its own. Theo operated on a different timeline. He operated on deadlines.

"Twelve days," she said again. "If we go to a reporter, we're handing someone else a piece of the story. We can't control what they do with it."

"We don't control what Drake does with it either. At least a reporter we choose might be on our side."

"Might be."

Theo capped his pen. "Your call. I'll make the request to the firm through official channels. That'll take weeks we don't have. The reporter route is faster. But it's your valley. Your history. You decide who gets to see it early."

The weight of the decision settled on her. This was the shape of the thing she had been learning: not just documenting the past, but choosing how the present would handle it. Every choice a small betrayal or a small defense. She thought of Mara, who had stayed in the valley without every reason handed to her. She thought of Denise, who had carried the bargain alone for fifty years and called it duty. She thought of her mother, who had kept the hard truths in a locked drawer and called it love.

"Let's try the official channel first," Wren said. "Theo, write the request. Put it on your letterhead. Millbrook County Archives. Make it formal. If they refuse, or if they stall, then we talk about the reporter. But I want the paper trail. I want the record to show we asked properly."

Theo studied her face. "That'll cost us days. The firm has no obligation to respond to a private archives request."

"Then we'll know they're stalling. That's information too. And we'll have the paper trail for when we go to court. Or when we publish." She met his eyes. "I'm not saying no to the reporter. I'm saying not yet. Not until we've exhausted the clean path."

A long pause. Then Theo nodded, once. "Clean path it is. I'll draft it tonight. We can file it tomorrow morning."

"Good."

They thanked the librarian and left the building into the cold afternoon. The sky had grayed further, the light thinning toward early dusk. The car's engine turned over on the first try. Theo pulled into traffic, merging toward the interstate.

The return drive was quieter. Wren watched the land roll by, the fields giving way to the first rise of the foothills. She thought about the folio. About Adelaide's handwriting, the slant of her letters, the way she pressed hard on the downstrokes. About the law firm and the anonymous donor. About the restriction that had sat on that folio for twenty years, waiting for someone to care enough to challenge it.

She thought about Drake, somewhere, assembling his partial record. Nineteen years of work lay behind him: the Millbrook case, the failed first attempt, the grandmother who couldn't remember. He held the folio, or access to it. And the deadline was his to use.

Twelve days.

"Your grandmother," Wren said. "Harriet. She asked about the garden. The old house. Which house was that?"

Theo's eyes stayed on the road. "The one in Millbrook. The one she lived in before the memory loss. Before 1988. She had roses. Climbing roses on the trellis by the kitchen door. She used to cut them for the table every June." His voice flattened. "She doesn't remember planting them. The trellis is gone from memory too. But the roses... the smell. She describes it exactly. Every time."

Wren absorbed this. "The memory of the smell survived. The memory of the planting didn't."

"Memory isn't a single thing. It's layers. The sensory layer. The narrative layer. The emotional layer. They can separate. The bargain takes the narrative. The story of what happened. But the smell of roses in June..." He shrugged. "That stays. Useless, but it stays."

"Is it useless?"

"No. It's proof. She was there. She lived there. She loved something there. The bargain couldn't take all of it."

The valley's bargain took memory as payment. Wren had seen the receipts: a childhood summer, a first kiss, the face of a dead husband proposing marriage. The cost was always specific, always personal. But the residue remained. Odette Reyes still held the joy of her husband's proposal, even without the words. Caleb had regained his two years, but the gap had left a scar he talked about in the kitchen late at night when he thought Mara was asleep.

"What does she say when you visit?" Wren asked.

"She asks if I'm eating enough. Asks about the archive. Tells me I work too hard. She doesn't know I'm trying to find what was taken from her. Drake. The valley. None of it." Theo's knuckles whitened on the wheel. "I haven't told her. I don't know if I ever will. What good would it do? She can't get it back. Knowing would only break the peace she's made."

"She deserves the truth."

"Does she? She's eighty-seven. She has good days and bad days. On the good days she remembers my aunt's name. On the bad days she thinks I'm my father. If I tell her that a man stole ten years of her life to study a bargain in a valley she's never heard of, what changes? She still can't remember. Can't testify. Can't help me." He exhaled hard. "The truth isn't always a gift. Sometimes it's just another burden."

Wren turned that over. The valley's new archive was built on the premise that truth was a gift. That documentation was protection. That the record would set them free. But Theo's grandmother lived in a different calculus. For her, the truth was a door that opened onto nothing but wind.

"Maybe," Wren said slowly. "But you're not her. You're the one carrying the search. And you deserve to know what you're carrying it for."

Theo didn't answer. The car crested a rise and the valley's ridge line appeared ahead, a dark smudge against the darkening sky. The interstate would take them past the turnoff for Amity Falls, onward to the county seat where Theo was staying. Wren had her own car parked at the valley's edge, hidden in the stand of pines where the GPS map showed nothing but trees.

"Drop me at the turnoff," she said. "I'll drive my car up. You head back to Millbrook. File the request tonight."

"You're sure."

"I'm sure. There's things I need to check in the valley archives before tomorrow. The Warden witness lists for the 2003 renewal. Adelaide's correspondence with the historical society. If there's a letter from her to Harlan & Associates, we need to find it."

Theo took the exit. The road narrowed, the pavement giving way to the two-lane blacktop that wound up into the hills. The trees closed in on either side, bare branches knitting overhead. The car's headlights cut a tunnel through the gathering dark.

"Twelve days," Theo said again, as if counting them off one by one.

"Eleven now. If we count today."

"Right. Eleven."

He pulled onto the gravel shoulder at the turnoff. The pines stood sentinel on either side. Wren unbuckled, gathered her folders. She paused with her hand on the door handle.

"Thank you for driving. For the historical society. For... listening."

Theo shook his head. "I should thank you. For letting me come. For letting me see how you work. For the roses."

She smiled, a small real thing. "The roses were yours. I just heard them."

She stepped out into the cold. The door clicked shut. Theo's taillights glowed red, then swept away around the curve, back toward the interstate, toward Millbrook, toward the archives and the law firm and the reporter he might call if the clean path failed.

Wren stood in the gravel until the lights disappeared. The valley breathed around her, the old familiar presence pressing close. She could feel the bargain's hum in her fingertips, the low constant vibration of a debt held in trust. Twelve days. Eleven. Ten by the time she reached the archive.

She walked to her car, unlocked it, slid behind the wheel. The interior was cold. The engine started. She turned onto the valley road, the tires crunching on frost-heaved asphalt.

The archive building waited at the valley's center, a converted barn with new insulation and climate control. Yusuf had pushed the renovation through the council in three meetings. The ribbon cutting was scheduled for the fifteenth. Drake's deadline.

Wren drove up the drive, parked, sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel. The building was dark. The valley was quiet. Somewhere in the hills, a coyote called once, answered by another.

She opened the door and stepped into the night.
