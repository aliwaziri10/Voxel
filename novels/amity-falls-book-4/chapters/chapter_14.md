Morning light slanted through the archive windows at a sharp angle, catching dust motes above the work table where Wren had spent three hours, fingers ink-stained, back aching. The transcription of Adelaide's correspondence was nearly done. She'd expected relief at the final page. Instead came the quiet that follows a long-held breath finally releasing - a quiet that leaves you listening for what comes next.

Theo sat across from her, his stack of county records reduced to a single folder. He'd been unusually still for twenty minutes, pen resting unmoving on the page. When he looked up, his expression held that careful quality it took on when he was turning something over before speaking.

"There's something in the Whitlock papers I haven't mentioned," he said. "A fragment. It wasn't with the letter. It was tucked into the back of the ledger Adelaide kept - the one with the bargain renewal dates. Separate from everything else."

Wren set down her pen. "You've had this since we catalogued the Whitlock boxes?"

"Since yesterday afternoon. I wanted to be sure of the handwriting first." He reached into his bag, withdrew a single sheet folded twice, edges softened by handling. He unfolded it carefully and slid it across the table.

The handwriting was familiar. Ambrose Whitlock's precise script, the same hand that had written the warning letter from the false bottom of his desk. But this note was shorter. Less formal. The ink had faded to a brown suggesting decades rather than years.

*The role does not pass by blood. It never did. The first Warden was chosen by the valley's consensus, and so shall the next be. The bargain recognizes what the people acknowledge. If the valley names a Warden, the bargain accepts that Warden. This is the administrative term the original record kept silent. This is what I should have used instead of trying to break what cannot be broken.*

Wren read it twice. A third time. The words settled into the space between her ribs, heavy and quiet.

"The first Warden was chosen by consensus," she said slowly. "Not appointed. Not inherited."

"Chosen," Theo said. "By the valley. By the people who live under the bargain."

"And the bargain recognizes what the people acknowledge." She looked up. "That's what the ring did. Ten volunteers. The valley named them, and the bargain accepted the distribution."

"Exactly." Theo's voice was low. "The ring was a consensus. The valley, in miniature, saying *we will carry this together*. And the bargain accepted it."

Wren's fingers traced the fold line. "Whitlock knew. He wrote this after he failed to end the bargain. After he hid the complete terms. He knew the mechanism existed all along."

"He knew, and he didn't use it. He tried to destroy the bargain instead of working *with* its terms." Theo leaned back, chair creaking. "Maybe he couldn't bring himself to trust the valley. Maybe he didn't believe consensus was real. Or maybe he just couldn't see past his own failure."

"Or maybe he left it for someone who could." The words came out before she could consider them. She looked at Theo - at the way light caught the bridge of his nose, the line of his jaw. "He hid this note. Separate from the letter. In Adelaide's ledger. He wanted it found. Just not by everyone. Not right away."

"By someone who would understand what it meant." Theo's gaze held hers. "By someone who had already seen consensus work. The ring. The volunteers. The valley choosing to carry the weight together."

Something clicked in Wren's chest. "The night of the ring. Yusuf said the valley had spoken. He meant it literally. The bargain heard the valley speak."

"And now the valley is speaking again. Through you. Through this publication." Theo tapped the paper. "If the Warden role passes by consensus, then the valley's consensus *is* the legitimizing force. Not a bloodline. Not a hidden appointment. The people of Amity Falls, acknowledging who holds the responsibility."

"My grandmother was a Finder. She never held the Warden role." Wren's voice thinned. "My father left. My mother never had the gift. There's no lineage to claim."

"There doesn't need to be." Theo said it flatly, like stating weather. "The note says the first Warden was chosen by consensus. It says the next shall be. It doesn't say the line was unbroken. It says the *mechanism* is consensus. If the valley consensus names you, the bargain accepts you."

"Names me for what? To publish a history?"

"To hold the complete record. To be the Warden of the first debt in its truest sense. The one who carries the true terms. The one who ensures the bargain is honored honestly." Theo's hand covered the paper, fingers brushing faded ink. "Drake wants the administrative terms to control the bargain. Whitlock's note says the administrative term *is* consensus. The valley's agreement. If you publish the complete history with the valley's consensus behind you, Drake's hold evaporates. He can't weaponize a partial record against a complete one the valley itself has acknowledged."

Wren stared at the note. The words blurred and sharpened. *The bargain recognizes what the people acknowledge.* The ring ritual. Ten volunteers in cold grass, each taking a piece of Dev's debt. The valley witnessing. The bargain accepting.

She thought of Odette at the general store counter, smiling through the gap where her husband's proposal words used to live. Of Denise on her porch, aging naturally for the first time in fifty years, hands folded in her lap. Of Yusuf at the council table, hesitation gone, voice steady when he said the valley would back Wren's publication. Of Walt Pruitt at the diner, asking questions because he wanted to understand, not expose.

Of Mara and Caleb in their orchard, two years of memory returned, building something new on ground that had finally stopped shifting.

Of Priya and Dev at a kitchen table, the weight of evidence work and deputy duties shared between them like firewood.

The valley had already spoken. In a thousand small ways, in a hundred conversations since the ring, the valley had been speaking. Naming. Acknowledging.

Wren had been waiting for permission. For a sign. For someone to tell her she had the right.

The sign was in Whitlock's handwriting, hidden in a ledger for decades, waiting for the moment the valley would be ready to use it.

"Whitlock didn't just leave this for anyone," she said. "He left it in Adelaide's ledger. She was the one who taught him the bargain. She wrote to him after the ring, after Denise was freed. She knew what consensus looked like."

"She did." Theo's thumb traced the paper's edge. "And she kept this note. Didn't destroy it. Didn't show it to anyone. She waited."

"For me?"

"For the valley to be ready. For the consensus to exist." He met her eyes. "She knew you. She watched you grow up. She knew what you were before you did."

Wren's throat tightened. Adelaide at her mother's kitchen every Thursday for tea. Gingersnaps pressed into Wren's palm with a wink. Questions about her finding work that went beyond politeness. Never once saying *you are the one* or *this will be yours.*

But she had taught Wren to read the gaps. To trust the pull. To understand that a Finder's debt was paid in memory, yes, but also in truth. In the willingness to look.

"She knew," Wren whispered. "And she let me find my own way to it."

"Because consensus can't be given. It has to be arrived at." Theo's voice was gentle. "She couldn't hand you the role. The valley had to reach it. You had to reach it. Together."

Wren looked down at the note again. *The role does not pass by blood. It never did.*

Never did. Not in the beginning. Not in the middle. Not now.

She thought of the first Warden, whoever they'd been. Standing before the valley. Chosen. Acknowledged. The bargain accepting the choice because the choice was real.

And she thought of the ring. Ten volunteers. No bloodline. No appointment. Just the valley saying *we will carry this.*

"The publication," she said. "The complete record. Adelaide's letters. Whitlock's letter. This note. The county records Theo brought. The testimony of everyone who lived it." She lifted her head. "That's the consensus. The valley speaking through the record. Acknowledging what happened. Naming the truth."

"Drake's partial record is a weapon because it's incomplete. It tells a story the valley never agreed to." Theo gathered the note, refolding it along the original creases. "Your complete record is a shield because it's whole. It tells the story the valley *is* agreeing to, by letting you tell it."

"By letting me tell it." Wren repeated the words, testing them. "Yusuf said the council backs me. The valley backs me. He didn't say *I* back you. He said *we* back you."

"Consensus," Theo said. "Not permission. Recognition."

The distinction settled into Wren's bones. Permission implied a grantor. Recognition implied a truth already existing, waiting to be seen.

She had been looking for someone to give her the right. The valley had been waiting for her to claim what was already hers by the only metric that had ever mattered: the willingness to carry it.

"When do we publish?" she asked.

Theo checked his watch. "The printer in Millbrook needs final files by noon tomorrow for Friday distribution. That gives us tonight and tomorrow morning to assemble the complete package. Adelaide's correspondence. The Whitlock letters. The county index gap documentation. The ring ritual record. Dev's evidence work on the toll redirection. Your finding notes on the hidden letters. Everything."

"Everything." The word was large in the small room. "No gaps. No held-back pieces."

"Nothing held back." Theo's jaw set. "Drake gets the complete record at the same time as everyone else. No advance copies. No exclusive access. The valley speaks all at once."

"And if he tries to publish his partial version first?"

"Then he publishes a fragment against a whole. And the valley has already seen the whole." Theo stood, stretching. "His hold was always the threat of revelation. You can't threaten someone with what they've already acknowledged."

Wren stood too, body protesting after hours in the chair. She rolled her shoulders, feeling joints pop. The archive was quiet around them, shelves full of boxes and binders and a year's slow accumulation. The new Archive. The one Yusuf had approved, the council had funded, the valley had agreed to build.

The valley had agreed. Consensus.

"One more thing," Theo said, hand on the door frame. He didn't look at her. "The note. Whitlock's note. It doesn't name you. It names the mechanism. Consensus. The valley has to do the naming."

"I know."

"Do you?" He turned, face open in a way that made her chest ache. "Because I've watched you carry this for a year. The documentation. The warning project. The finding work. The nights you didn't sleep because someone's lost thing mattered. The days you stood before the council and said *here is what I found* without flinching. The valley didn't just arrive at consensus yesterday. It arrived at consensus every time you showed up and did the work."

Wren's breath caught. "That's not how consensus works. It's not a tally of deeds."

"Isn't it?" Theo's mouth quirked, not quite a smile. "The first Warden was chosen by consensus. How do you think the valley knew who to choose? They watched. They saw who carried the weight, who told the truth, who came back."

"And if the valley chooses wrong?"

"Then the bargain corrects it. That's what the bargain does. It protects the valley. It doesn't protect the Warden." Theo held her gaze. "But I don't think the valley has chosen wrong. I think it chose you a long time ago. You're just the last one to know."

He left before she could answer, boots quiet on the stairs.

Wren stood alone, the Whitlock note warm against her palm. Dust motes still floated in the slanted light. Shelves held the valley's memory, box by box, page by page.

She walked to the window. Below, the town moved through its morning. Odette unlocking the general store. Walt opening the diner. Priya's cruiser pulling out, Dev's truck following a minute later. Yusuf at the council building, flipping the sign to OPEN. Mara and Caleb in the orchard, already at work among the trees.

The valley. Her valley. Not because she owned it. Because she belonged to it. Because she had stayed. Because she had looked. Because she had told the truth when telling the truth cost something.

*The bargain recognizes what the people acknowledge.*

The people had acknowledged. In a thousand ways. In a hundred conversations. In the ring. In the council vote. In the quiet acceptance of a Finder who found things and brought them home.

Wren looked at the note in her hand. At the faded ink. At the words that had waited decades to be used.

She turned back to the work table. To the stacks of transcribed letters. To the county records. To the finding notes. To the complete record waiting to be assembled.

She sat down. Picked up her pen.

And began to write the introduction.
