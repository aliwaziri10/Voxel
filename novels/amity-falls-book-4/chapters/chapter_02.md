The basement of the old county building smelled of dust and iron and the slow rot of paper that had been wet once and never properly dried. Wren had spent the better part of a year learning that smell. She knew which shelves sagged under the weight of water damage, which boxes mice had chewed through, which ledgers held ink faded to brown ghosting before she was born.

Theo stood at the long table under the single bare bulb, gloves already on, a magnifying lamp clamped to the edge. His own tools from Millbrook sat beside him: a bone folder, Japanese tissue patches, wheat starch paste in a small sealed jar. He worked like a surgeon warned the patient might wake mid-operation.

"The 1847 ledger," he said without looking up. "The binding is shot. The spine cracked when I lifted it. Turn the pages wrong and we lose the gutter margins."

Wren set her notebook down on a clear patch of table. "The 1847 ledger lists the first renewal after the original bargain. Names in the margin, amounts in the column beside them. If we photograph it now, we get the names before the ink flakes off completely."

"We photograph it after I stabilize the spine. Two hours. Maybe three."

"Drake doesn't wait two hours."

Theo's hand paused over the cover. The leather was red-rotted, powdering under his fingertips. "Drake has had his partial record for nineteen years. He waited through the ring ritual. He waited through Denise's release. He waited while you decided what to do with Adelaide's letter and Whitlock's letter. He is not going to publish tonight because we took three hours to save a binding."

"He might publish tomorrow because he sees we're stalling."

"He sees us being careful. There is a difference."

Wren crossed her arms. Cold seeped through her wool socks, the kind that rises from stone. She could feel the weight of the valley above them, orchards, creek, houses where people slept without knowing the full history of what kept them safe. She had grown up in that not-knowing, resented it. Now she was the one deciding how much of it to hand over, and the resentment had curdled into something heavier.

"The margin notes on page twelve," she said. "The ones in pencil. They're not in the index. They're not in any of Dev's transcriptions. If that page tears while you're mending the spine, we lose the only record of who paid the 1847 toll and what memory was taken."

Theo turned the magnifying lamp toward the page. The pencil was faint, pressure uneven, as if the writer's hand had shaken. He adjusted the light, frowning.

"Three names," he murmured. "Two amounts. One blank where the memory should be described. The handwriting changes halfway through the second entry."

"Which means two different recorders. Which means the blank might not be an omission. It might be a refusal to write it down."

"Or the recorder didn't know what memory was taken. The Finder who paid the toll might not have told them."

"Finders always know. The cost comes due at the moment of finding. You feel the gap open. You feel what slides into it."

Theo looked up. His face was pale in the lamp light, shadows sharp under his cheekbones. Three weeks in the valley. He had slept above Odette's store, eaten breakfast at Mara and Caleb's table more mornings than not. Not once had he asked to see the bargain site, or meet a Warden, a Mender, or a Weather. He had asked for the ledgers, the correspondence, the council minutes, the index with its restricted gap. He had asked for the paper trail.

"You've paid a debt," he said. "A small one. You said a note in a drawer, a thank-you that puzzled you."

Wren's throat tightened. "A grocery list. My grandmother's handwriting. I found it in the kitchen drawer three days after she died. Apples, flour, tea. She had written it the morning she helped Mrs. Kowalski locate her wedding ring down the well. The thank-you note was from Mrs. Kowalski, dated the same day. My grandmother never mentioned the ring. Never mentioned the cost. The grocery list was the only thing missing from her memory that week."

"And you knew because..."

"Because I found the list. Because I'm a Finder. Because the gap showed me the shape of what was gone before I even understood what a gap was."

Theo set down the bone folder. He removed his gloves slowly, finger by finger, and laid them flat on the table.

"That's a small debt," he said. "You've never paid a large one."

"No."

"And you've never redirected a toll."

"No. My grandmother taught me that redirecting is the oldest taboo. She taught me that before she taught me how to hold a finding rod."

"Dev redirected a toll. He paid Caleb's debt with someone else's memory. He served a sentence for it. Now he does evidence work for the substation."

"I know Dev's history. I was there for the ring ritual. I held one of the stones."

Theo was quiet. The basement settled around them, old timbers creaking, a drop of water hitting stone somewhere in the dark behind the shelves.

"The blank on page twelve," he said. "If it's a redirected toll, the recorder might not have known whose memory was taken. The Finder would know. The person who lost the memory wouldn't."

"Which means the gap in the record isn't an accident. It's a concealment."

"Or it's a gap the recorder couldn't fill. There's a difference between hiding something and not having the information to write down."

Wren moved to the table, leaning over the page. Pencil marks smudged, paper thin as onion skin. Pressure marks showed where the writer had pressed hard, then lighter, then hard again.

"The second name," she said. "The one with the blank. The handwriting changes right after it. As if the first recorder stopped and someone else took over."

"Or the same recorder changed grip. Changed pen. Changed posture."

"Finders don't change posture when they're recording a toll. The cost hits at the moment of finding. The recording happens after. The hand that writes the name is the hand that paid."

Theo reached for the magnifying loupe on its chain around his neck. He bent over the page, breath slow and controlled.

"The first name," he said. "Josiah Whitlock. The amount: seven years. The memory: his sister's face. The second name... the handwriting is different. The pressure is different. The name is..."

He stopped. The loupe hovered.

"The name is what?"

"Castellan. Not Castellano. Castellan. One 't'. Two 'l's. One 'n'."

Wren went cold. The basement seemed to contract, walls pressing inward. Castellan. The ring member from the earlier trouble. The one coerced by Ambrose Kell. The one who had served on the ring that distributed Dev's taking.

"That's not my family," she said. "My grandmother was Castellano. Two 't's. Two 'l's. One 'n'. My father was Castellano. I'm Castellano."

"I know. I checked the valley records when I arrived. Your line goes back to the 1820s. The Castellan line appears in the 1860s. Different families. Different gifts. The Castellans were Weathers."

"Then why is a Weather named Castellan paying a Finder's toll in 1847? Weathers don't pay Finder tolls. Weathers pay Weather tolls. The bargain tracks lineage."

"Unless the recorder made an error. Unless the name was misheard. Unless..."

"Unless the Castellan line married into the Castellano line and the gift shifted. Unless the recorder wrote what they were told and the teller lied."

Theo straightened, the loupe swinging against his chest. "You're jumping. One ambiguous entry in a damaged ledger. No corroborating record. No second source. A name that looks like yours but isn't, attached to a toll that doesn't match the lineage."

"Drake has this ledger. Or a copy. Or the index entry that points to it. That's why the index has a restricted gap. That's why the standing arrangement notation exists. Someone knew this entry was dangerous. Someone restricted access to it nineteen years ago, when Drake made his first attempt in Millbrook."

"Nineteen years ago the restricted notation was added. That doesn't mean Drake saw this page. It means someone in the county records office flagged it."

"Who? The county records office doesn't know about the bargain. They don't know about Finders or Weathers or tolls. They see ledgers with names and dates and amounts. A gap in the index gets flagged as restricted because they don't understand it."

"Then who flagged it?"

Wren stared at the pencil marks. The blank where the memory should be. The name that wasn't hers but looked like it could have been.

"Ambrose Whitlock," she said. "He held the Warden of the first debt role. He tried to end the bargain. He hid the complete terms. He taught Adelaide how to renew it. He left a letter warning his family not to share what he knew. His family buried the letter. Dev is his descendant. Dev found the letter."

"And Castellan?"

"Was on the ring. The ring that distributed Dev's taking. The ring that freed Denise. The ring that restored Caleb's memory. Ten volunteers. Castellan was one of them."

Theo's expression shifted, the dry professional mask cracking into something sharper. "You think Castellan knew about this entry. You think Castellan knew about a redirected toll in 1847, paid by a Weather with a name like yours, recorded by Josiah Whitlock."

"I think the ring ritual didn't just distribute Dev's taking. I think it distributed knowledge. The volunteers held the stones. They felt the weight. They saw what Dev saw. Castellan might have seen this ledger in that moment. Might have known what it meant."

"And if Castellan knew, Drake might know. If Drake knows, he has leverage on the Castellan line. On the Castellano line by confusion. On the valley's history of redirected tolls."

"Drake doesn't want to destroy the valley. He wants control of the bargain's administrative terms. He wants the true original terms. A redirected toll in 1847, hidden by Whitlock, concealed in the index for nineteen years... that's administrative terms. That's the shape of the bargain's corruption."

Theo was silent. He replaced his gloves, snapped them at the wrists, picked up the bone folder again.

"Three hours to stabilize the spine," he said. "Then we photograph every page. Then we transcribe the margin notes. Then we cross-reference with the council minutes, the correspondence, the index. Then we see if there's a pattern."

"And if there is?"

"Then we publish the pattern. All of it. Before Drake publishes his version."

Wren nodded, though her chest was tight. She had come down thinking they were building a shield. Now she wondered if they were just mapping the target.

The stairs creaked. Wren turned, hand instinctively moving to the finding rod at her belt. Theo didn't startle. He had learned the valley's rhythms, knew the difference between a settler's step and a stranger's.

Yusuf appeared at the bottom of the stairs, his frame filling the narrow stairwell. The council chair moved with the deliberate care of a man who had once hesitated and learned the cost of hesitation. He carried a thermos in one hand, a folded paper in the other.

"Coffee," he said. "And a message from the county clerk's office. Delivered by a deputy who looked like she'd seen a ghost."

Wren took the thermos. The coffee was hot, bitter, exactly what she needed. "Priya?"

"Priya's at the substation with Dev. This was a different deputy. A temporary transfer from Millbrook. She didn't give her name. Handed this to the receptionist and left without signing the log."

Yusuf unfolded the paper. Single sheet, county letterhead, text crisp and official.

"Notice of Records Audit," Wren read over his shoulder. "Pursuant to County Ordinance 44-12, the Millbrook County Records Division will conduct a comprehensive audit of all restricted-index holdings in the Amity Falls municipal archive, commencing fourteen days from date of service. All materials flagged with standing-arrangement notation are subject to review, duplication, and potential transfer to the county central repository."

The paper trembled in her fingers. Fourteen days. The county central repository. Duplication.

"Theo," she said. "The restricted gap in the index. The standing arrangement notation. This is what you came for. This is the professional reason."

Theo took the paper, read it once, read it again. His face went flat, the way it did when he was thinking through procedures, jurisdictions, chains of custody.

"The audit was scheduled," he said. "Before I arrived. The notice was prepared weeks ago. The deputy who delivered it, a temporary transfer from Millbrook. Drake has contacts in the Millbrook records office. He had them nineteen years ago. He has them now."

"Can we stop it?" Yusuf asked. "The council can petition. Claim local jurisdiction. Argue the materials are culturally sensitive, historically fragile, not subject to county ordinance."

"Petitions take months," Theo said. "The audit starts in fourteen days. The county central repository is in Millbrook. Drake's territory. Once the materials leave this building, we lose control of the chain of custody. He doesn't need to publish his partial record. He just needs to be in the room when the county makes copies."

Wren set the thermos down hard. Coffee sloshed over the rim, dark on the concrete floor.

"We have fourteen days," she said. "To photograph, transcribe, cross-reference, and publish the complete true history. Before the county audit hands Drake the leverage he's been building for nineteen years."

"Fourteen days," Theo repeated. "For the 1847 ledger alone: three hours for the spine, two for photography, four for transcription. Cross-referencing the council minutes, forty years of them. The correspondence boxes, Dev said seventeen. The index itself needs a full audit to find every restricted notation."

"We don't do it alone," Yusuf said. "Priya can compel truth from the deputies handling the transfer. Dev can map substation evidence to ledger entries. Mara can coordinate volunteers. Caleb handles logistics. Odette manages the store as a drop point. Denise knows the bargain's terms better than anyone alive. Walt can coordinate the town."

"Denise is seventy-eight," Wren said. "She only just started aging naturally. She shouldn't be carrying this."

"Denise carried the bargain for fifty years in secret," Yusuf said. "The weight of every renewal, every toll, every memory taken. She carried it alone. She doesn't need your permission to carry it now."

Wren looked at the ledger. The pencil marks. The blank where the memory should be. That name again, the one that could have been hers.

"What about my mother?" she asked. "She lives in the valley. She's alive. She doesn't know the full history. She knows I'm a Finder, that I do documentation work. She doesn't know about the bargain's terms. She doesn't know about the tolls."

"Your mother," Yusuf said gently, "raised a daughter who became the valley's lead documenter at twenty-one. A daughter who held a ring stone and felt the weight of ten volunteers sharing a debt. A daughter about to publish the truth the valley has hidden for generations. Whatever she knows, whatever she doesn't... she raised you. Trust that."

Wren's eyes stung. She blinked hard, looking down at the ledger.

"Three hours for the spine," she said. "Then we start."

Theo adjusted the magnifying lamp. He picked up the wheat starch paste jar, unscrewed the lid. The paste smelled of starch and time.

"One page at a time," he said. "One ledger at a time. One name at a time."

Yusuf set the audit notice on the table, weighted it with a paperweight from the shelf. "I'll tell the council. We'll mobilize. Fourteen days. We've done impossible things in less time."

He climbed the stairs, footsteps heavy and sure. The basement door closed above them, cutting off the sound of the valley waking.

Wren pulled on her gloves. She picked up her notebook, opened to a fresh page. The date went down first. Then: 1847 ledger. Josiah Whitlock, seven years, sister's face. Castellan (Weather?), amount illegible, memory blank. Handwriting change. Possible redirected toll. Index restriction added 19 years ago. County audit in 14 days.

"Your grandmother's grocery list," Theo said quietly. "The thank-you note. You said the list was the only thing missing from her memory that week. How did you know it was missing? If the gap took the memory of writing the list, how did you know the list existed to be missing?"

Wren's pen stopped. Ink pooled at the tip.

"Because I found the list in the drawer," she said. "Because I'm a Finder. Because the gap showed me the shape of what was gone. The list was there, in the drawer, in her handwriting. But when I held it, I didn't remember her writing it. Didn't remember her holding the pen. Didn't remember the morning light on the kitchen table. The memory of writing it was gone. The list itself remained. The object remained. The memory of its making was the cost."

"And the thank-you note?"

"Mrs. Kowalski brought it three days later. My grandmother read it. She smiled. Said, 'How lovely.' Didn't remember the ring. Didn't remember the well. Didn't remember the cost. Remembered the tea on the list. Remembered the apples. Remembered the flour. The gap was precise. Surgical. It took only the memory of the finding and the cost. It left the rest."

Theo was quiet. He applied paste to a strip of Japanese tissue with the bone folder, movements precise, unhurried.

"Precise," he said. "Surgical. That's how the bargain works. It takes exactly what's owed. No more, no less."

"Unless someone redirects the toll. Then it takes from someone who doesn't owe. Someone who didn't ask. Someone who doesn't know."

"Like Harriet Marsh. My grandmother. Ten years of her memory taken in Drake's first attempt. She doesn't know where she was. Doesn't know what she lost. Only knows there's a hole in her life shaped like a decade."

Wren looked at him. Really looked. The dry archivist. The patient researcher. The grandson who had built his adult life around a hole in his grandmother's mind.

"She's alive," Wren said. "You said she's alive. She's in Millbrook."

"In a care facility. Her childhood comes back. Her marriage. My mother's birth. Nothing from 1997 to 2007. The decade before 2007. The decade of Drake's first attempt. She cannot say where she was, what she did, who she was with. The memories aren't suppressed. They're gone. The bargain took them. Or Drake took them using the bargain's mechanism. We don't know which. We may never know."

"Does she know you're here?"

"She knows I work in records. Knows I travel. Asks me to bring the newspaper. Asks about the weather. Doesn't ask about the gap. Learned not to ask a long time ago. The doctors called it dissociative amnesia. The family called it a tragedy. I called it a clue."

"And now?"

"Now I have a ledger with a redirected toll from 1847. A county audit in fourteen days. An information broker who has waited nineteen years. A partner who feels the shape of every gap. And a grandmother who drinks tea and reads the newspaper and doesn't remember the decade that broke her."

Theo laid the tissue strip along the cracked spine. He smoothed it with the bone folder, slow strokes, firm pressure. The red rot powdered around the edges. The leather held.

"Three hours," he said. "Then we photograph. Then we transcribe. Then we cross-reference. Then we publish."

Wren watched his hands. The care. The patience. The knowledge that some things couldn't be rushed, that some repairs required time the world might not give.

"Fourteen days," she said. "We'll need coffee."

"I brought a case. It's in the trunk of the rental."

"Good. Because I have a feeling we're going to need more than coffee."

She picked up her finding rod. The weight was familiar, the wood worn smooth by her grandmother's hands before hers. She closed her eyes, let the gift extend, let it search the basement's dark corners, the shelves and boxes and ledgers. She felt the gaps. Small ones. A missing receipt. A forgotten conversation. A lost button. The ordinary losses of a building that had held records for a century.

And beneath them, deeper, older, a gap shaped like a name: Castellan. A gap shaped like a memory: a sister's face. A gap shaped like a decade, 1997 to 2007.

The rod warmed in her hand. The finding wasn't a pull. It was a knowing. The knowledge that something existed, somewhere, waiting to be located.

"Ready," she said.

Theo didn't look up from the spine. "Page twelve first. Then the rest. One at a time."

The basement settled around them. The bulb hummed. Somewhere above, the valley woke, ordinary and unaware, the orchards breathing in the morning light, the creek running clear, the houses full of people who didn't know the weight of the ground beneath their feet.

Wren opened the ledger to page twelve. The pencil marks waited. The blank waited. The name that wasn't hers stared up from the page, written in a hand that had shaken, recorded by a man who had tried to end the world and failed, hidden by a family that had buried the truth instead of sharing it.

Fourteen days.

She picked up her pen.
