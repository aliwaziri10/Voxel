The archive basement smelled of dust and old leather, the particular scent of paper that had spent decades breathing the same cool air. Wren had known it since she was tall enough to reach the lower shelves, but she had never seen it through someone else's eyes until Theo Marsh descended the stairs behind her.

He carried a canvas tool roll tied with twine. No wheeled case, no plastic bins. When he set it on the worktable, the twine came undone with a practiced flick, revealing bone folders in graduated widths, a soft hake brush, Japanese tissue strips in a glassine envelope, a magnifier on a stand, and a pair of cotton gloves folded precisely at the cuff.

"You don't wear the gloves," he said, not a question.

Wren shook her head. "The vellum's too fragile. Cotton catches. Bare hands, clean and dry, or nitrile if the ink's unstable."

"Bare hands," he repeated. He washed them at the enamel sink in the corner, scrubbing to the wrists, drying on a lint-free cloth. When he turned back, his sleeves were rolled to the elbow, the forearms pale and freckled. "Show me the oldest."

She led him to the back wall, where the ledgers lived in acid-free boxes labeled in Mara's careful script. *Bargain Records, 1823–1890. Bargain Records, 1891–1950. Bargain Records, Incomplete.*

"The first one's missing," Wren said. "Denise said it burned in the courthouse fire of '19. But the second volume starts mid-sentence, so someone copied from the original before it went."

Theo lifted the lid of the first box. The ledger inside was bound in cracked calfskin, the corners bumped soft as felt. He didn't open it immediately. He ran a fingertip along the spine, feeling the sewing structure beneath the leather.

"Oversewn," he murmured. "Nineteenth-century rebind. The original sewing's gone. That means the margins are trimmed."

"Which means we lose whatever was written in the gutters."

"Exactly." He opened the cover. The endpaper was a marbled swirl of rust and ochre, the paste-down lifting at the edge. He slipped a bone folder under the lifted corner, easing it flat. "May I?"

Wren stepped back.

He turned pages with a slowness that made her throat tight. Not reverence—competence. He read the structure before the text: the ruling pattern, the ink variation, the way the scribe's hand changed at page forty-seven, the pressure of the nib deepening as the day wore on. He dictated to her in a low voice, and she wrote in the catalog notebook: *Volume 2, 1824–1841. Iron gall ink throughout. Two hands. Watermark: J. Whatman 1822. Folios 1–12 water-damaged lower right, text partially lost.*

They worked through the morning. The light shifted across the high windows, dust motes drifting in the beams. Theo didn't ask about the bargain. He didn't ask about the valley's secrets. He treated the ledgers as objects with histories, each one a crime scene of paper and ink.

At noon, he closed the third volume and stretched, rolling his shoulders.

"Your grandmother's handwriting," he said.

Wren looked up from her notes. "What?"

"Volume three. The hand that takes over at folio 203. That's Adelaide Whitlock."

She stared at him. "You've never seen her writing."

"I've seen the letters she wrote to the Millbrook County Clerk between 1912 and 1918. Property disputes, mostly. She had a distinctive *r*—the descender curls left instead of right. And she dots her *i* with a slash, not a dot." He tapped the page. "Right there. *The debt was paid in full.* See the *r* in *debt*?"

Wren leaned over. The word was faded, the iron gall eating through the vellum in places. But the *r* curled left, a tiny hook.

"How did you know to look for Millbrook letters?"

Theo wiped his hands on the cloth. "Your grandmother's name came up in the property records I pulled before I came here. She corresponded with the clerk's office about a boundary dispute on the Whitlock parcel. Forty-two letters over six years. I photographed them all."

"You didn't mention that."

"It didn't seem relevant to the cataloguing."

"It's relevant to *me*."

He held her gaze, steady and unreadable. "Then I'm glad I told you now."

The disagreement came after lunch, over a single page in Volume Four.

The vellum was thin as skin, the ink spiderwebbed with cracks. A previous repair—someone's good intention, decades ago—had used adhesive tape that had yellowed to the color of old tea, pulling fibers away from the substrate every time the page turned.

"We need to remove the tape," Theo said. "It's actively destroying the page."

"We can't," Wren said. "Not without a conservator. The ink's flaking. If we lift the tape, we lift the text with it."

"Then we digitize it now, before it gets worse. I have a copy stand in the car. Raking light, UV, infrared. We'll get the text underneath."

"And if the page tears while we're positioning it?"

"It won't. I've done this. Hundreds of times."

"Have you done it with *this* ink? This vellum? This specific degradation?"

Theo went still. "No. But the principles don't change."

"The principles don't change," Wren repeated, her voice rising. "That's what you said about the cataloguing, too. But you didn't know Adelaide's handwriting was in Volume Three until you saw it. You didn't know the '19 fire took the first volume until I told you. You're applying principles to things you don't actually know."

The basement went quiet. The hum of the dehumidifier, somewhere in the walls.

Theo set down the bone folder. He didn't cross his arms. He didn't step back. He looked at the page, then at her.

"You're right," he said. "I don't know this collection. I know *collections*. There's a difference. And I should have said that instead of assuming my experience transferred." He exhaled. "What would you do?"

Wren surprised herself by softening. "I'd leave the tape. I'd photograph it as-is, with a note in the catalog that the repair is failing. Then I'd ask Mara to contact the state archive in Augusta. They have a conservator who specializes in iron gall on vellum. She comes up twice a year."

"And if she can't come for six months?"

"Then we wait six months. The page has survived a hundred years under tape. It can survive six more."

Theo studied her. "You're more conservative than I expected. For someone who wants to publish the whole history."

"Publishing doesn't mean being reckless with the source. It means being honest about what we *don't* know."

He was quiet a moment. Then he picked up his notebook. "Honest about what we don't know. I'll write that in the methodology section."

Wren let out a breath she hadn't realized she was holding. "Good. Let's shoot the page as-is. Raking light from the left, like you said."

They worked side by side at the copy stand, Theo adjusting the lights, Wren turning the pages with a spatula. The camera clicked, a soft mechanical sound. Each image appeared on the laptop screen: the tape's grid shadow, the cracked ink, the ghost of text beneath.

Theo didn't say *I told you so* when the raking light revealed three lines of writing the tape had hidden. He just zoomed in, adjusted the contrast, and whispered, "There."

*May 1847. The Warden refused the renewal. The debt was transferred to the Mender line. The cost was not recorded.*

Wren's fingers tightened on the spatula. "The Warden refused. That's—"

"Ambrose Whitlock," Theo said. "The first Warden of the first debt. Adelaide's grandfather."

"The letter Mara found said he *tried* to end the bargain. Not that he refused a renewal."

"Refusing a renewal *is* trying to end it. If the bargain requires annual renewal, and the Warden says no..." Theo trailed off, scrolling. "The next entry is June 1847. Different hand. *The Mender accepted the burden. The valley stands.*"

"Denise's line," Wren said. "The Menders. They took the debt when the Warden wouldn't."

"And they've been paying it ever since."

The camera clicked again. *October 1847. The Finder was asked to locate the missing terms. He returned empty-handed. His memory of the search was taken as payment.*

"Finder lineage," Theo said. "Your line."

"Dev's line. And mine. We're both Whitlock descendants." The words tasted strange, still. The ring ritual had made it real, but the knowing lived in a different place than the feeling.

Theo didn't ask how that felt. He saved the file, labeled it *Vol4_folio087_raking*, and moved to the next page.

The council member arrived at three-fifteen.

Walt Pruitt didn't look like a council member. He looked like a man who fixed furnaces and cleared snow from widow's driveways, which he was, and had been for forty years before Yusuf asked him to serve. His jeans were clean but worn at the knees. His flannel shirt had a stain near the cuff that no amount of washing would remove.

He stood at the top of the basement stairs, one hand on the rail, not descending.

"Yusuf sent me," he said. His voice was rough, the kind that came from decades of talking over machinery. "He didn't want to pull you out himself. Said you'd take it better from me."

Wren set down the spatula. "Take what?"

"Drake's deadline. He sent word through his lawyer. Millbrook firm. Certified letter to the town office, copy to Yusuf." Walt descended two steps, then stopped. "He's giving us ten days. Ten days to hand over the *complete original terms of the bargain*—his words—or he publishes what he has. His 'partial, damning record.'"

The basement air seemed to thin.

"What he has," Theo said from the copy stand, "is incomplete. He knows it's incomplete. That's why he wants the rest."

"Doesn't matter what he knows," Walt said. "What matters is what he prints. He's got names. Dates. The fact that the bargain gets renewed every year by someone paying a memory toll. He's got the list of who paid what, going back to the sixties. Denise's name is all over it. So is Caleb's. So is Dev's."

Wren felt the blood drain from her face. "Denise is *free*. The ring ritual—"

"Freed her from the bargain," Walt said. "Didn't erase the record of what she did. Drake has the ledgers Denise kept. The ones she hid in the false bottom of her Hope Chest. He's had them since before the ring. He copied them."

"Then why the deadline?" Theo asked. "If he already has the damaging material, why not just publish?"

"Because he doesn't have the *original terms*," Wren said, the words clicking into place. "He has the *renewal records*. But the original terms—the first bargain, the administrative structure, the rules for how the debt gets calculated and who can pay it—those were never written in Denise's ledgers. Ambrose Whitlock hid them. Adelaide's letter said so. Whitlock's own letter confirmed it."

"And you're the only ones who know where they are," Walt said. "Or might be."

"We don't know where they are," Wren said. "We're *looking*."

"Drake doesn't know that. He thinks you found them. Or that you're close." Walt's eyes moved to Theo. "His lawyer's letter says 'the Castellano woman and her outside associate.' He knows you're here. He knows what you're doing."

Theo's face didn't change. "Then we work faster."

"Yusuf said to tell you: no leaks. None. Not a photograph, not a transcript, not a conversation overheard at the general store. Drake has someone watching. Maybe more than one. The letter was specific: *any unauthorized disclosure triggers immediate publication.*"

"Unauthorized by whom?" Theo asked.

"By Drake. He's the arbiter. That's the point."

Walt climbed back up the stairs, his boots heavy on the treads. "Ten days," he called down. "Yusuf wants a progress report by morning. He's backing the publication. Said so at the emergency session. But he needs to know *what* you're publishing, and *when*."

The door at the top of the stairs closed. The latch clicked.

Wren looked at the ledger open on the cradle. *The Mender accepted the burden. The valley stands.*

"He's right," she said. "About the leaks. We've been careful, but careful isn't a system."

Theo saved the current image, closed the laptop. "We build a system. Today. Chain of custody for every file. Encrypted drive, air-gapped, one copy only. No cloud, no email, no phones in the basement. You and me, that's the access list. Yusuf gets a briefing document we write together, reviewed by both of us before it leaves this room."

"And Mara? Caleb? Priya?"

"They get what we decide to give them, when we decide to give it. Not before."

Wren nodded. "The state conservator. We need to ask Mara to contact her. But that's a leak risk—Mara talks to Caleb, Caleb talks to Odette, Odette talks to everyone."

"Then we don't ask Mara," Theo said. "We ask Yusuf. He contacts the state archive directly. Official channels. Paper trail. No informal conversations."

"Yusuf hates paper trails."

"Yusuf hates *uncontrolled* paper trails. This one he'll control."

She studied him. The light from the high windows caught the freckles across his nose, the faint line between his eyebrows where he frowned at the screen. He was tired, she realized. The kind of tired that lived in the bones, not the muscles.

"You've done this before," she said. "Not the bargain. But the—containment. The secrets."

Theo didn't look up. "I've worked with families who had things they needed to keep private while they figured out what to do with them. Estates with sealed correspondence. Municipal records with privacy restrictions. The principles are the same."

"Principles again."

He glanced up, a ghost of something—amusement, maybe, or exhaustion. "Principles are all I have. I don't have a gift. I don't have a lineage. I have methodology. And I have the thing your grandmother wrote in forty-two letters to the Millbrook Clerk."

"Which was?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"She was looking for something," he said. "Something lost. She wrote *I cannot find it, and I cannot afford the cost of looking.* Over and over. Different words. Same meaning."

Wren's breath caught. "The cost of looking. A Finder's debt."

"She was a Finder. Whitlock line. She paid the toll every time she searched for whatever she lost." Theo closed the laptop fully now. "She never found it. The letters stop in 1918. The clerk's replies stop too. The last one says *Miss Whitlock, we have no further record. I am sorry.*"

"What was she looking for?"

"He didn't say. The clerk didn't say. But the boundary dispute—the Whitlock parcel—it was about a strip of land along the northern edge. The deed description referenced a 'stone marker at the old oak.' The oak was gone. The marker was gone. Adelaide wanted the marker found."

"A Finder looking for a boundary marker," Wren said slowly. "That's... that's a trivial use of the gift. The cost wouldn't be more than a day's memory. A conversation. A meal."

"Unless she looked more than once. Unless she looked *repeatedly*, because the marker kept not being there." Theo stood, rolling his sleeves down. "Unless someone kept moving it. Or removing it. And each time she searched, she paid."

The implication hung between them, heavier than the dust.

"Drake," Wren said. "He's been watching this valley for nineteen years. Since the Millbrook case. Since before that, maybe. What if he—what if someone *before* him—"

"Let's not speculate," Theo said. "Let's document. We have nine days and about fourteen hours. We catalog. We photograph. We secure the digital files. We write the publication draft. And we find the original terms before Drake publishes his version of history."

He packed his tool roll with the same precision he'd unpacked it. Bone folders in graduated widths. Hake brush. Japanese tissue. Magnifier. Gloves folded at the cuff.

"I'm staying at the rooms above Odette's," he said. "Yusuf arranged it. I'll be there by seven tomorrow. We start at seven-thirty."

Wren nodded. "I'll bring coffee. The good kind. Odette keeps it behind the counter."

"Black. No sugar."

She watched him climb the stairs, the canvas roll over his shoulder. The door closed. The latch clicked.

She was alone in the basement with the ledgers, the dehumidifier's hum, and the weight of nine days.

*May 1847. The Warden refused the renewal.*

She turned the page. The next entry was in a different hand, the ink darker, the pressure heavier.

*June 1847. The Mender accepted the burden. The valley stands. The cost: the memory of her firstborn's first step. The Mender did not hesitate.*

Denise's line. The Menders. Fifty years of renewals, each one a memory taken, each one a silence kept.

Wren picked up her catalog notebook. She wrote: *Vol4, folio088. Transfer of debt from Warden to Mender line. Cost recorded: memory of firstborn's first step. Note: Mender 'did not hesitate.' Cross-reference: Denise Reyes, ring ritual testimony, Book 3.*

Her hand didn't shake. She was surprised by that.

Then she wrote: *Theo Marsh. Millbrook County. Records investigator. Not Kindred. No gift. Wound: grandmother's missing decade. Competence: high. Trust: building.*

She closed the notebook. Turned off the copy stand lights. Climbed the stairs into the afternoon sun.

The valley spread below the archive windows: the orchard rows, the general store's peaked roof, the church steeple, the river's silver thread. Ordinary. Peaceful. The kind of place people came to forget the world.

Ten days to tell the truth before someone else told a lie.

She walked toward Odette's, the notebook secure in her bag, the camera's memory card in her pocket. Behind her, the basement door locked with a sound like finality.
