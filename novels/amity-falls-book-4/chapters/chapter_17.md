The kitchen light threw a warm pool across the table where the proof pages lay in overlapping rows. Wren had lost count of how many times she'd read the same paragraph about the 1987 renewal, the one where Denise described the weight settling into her shoulders like a second skeleton. Each pass caught something new. A comma that should have been a semicolon. A date that needed cross-referencing against the town ledger. A sentence carrying more accusation than fact.

Theo sat across from her, his own stack anchored by a ceramic mug. He moved through them with the same methodical care he brought to the county index, pencil marking margins in a shorthand only he understood. The scratching of graphite on paper had become the room's heartbeat.

"You're frowning at the 1992 renewal," he said without looking up.

"The phrasing on the harvest clause. It reads like Denise chose the wording. She didn't. The council drafted it and she signed." Wren tapped the line with a fingertip. "If Drake gets hold of this, he'll frame it as her authorship. He'll say she shaped the terms to her advantage."

"Then we change the phrasing."

"To what? 'The council drafted and the Warden executed'? That sounds like we're hiding something."

"We're not hiding. We're being precise." He turned a page, paused, made a notation. "Precision is the only thing his version won't have."

The clock on the stove read 11:43. Outside, the valley slept under a sky so thick with stars it looked painted. No streetlights this far out. No traffic noise. Only the occasional creak of the house settling and the low hum of the refrigerator.

Wren's eyes burned. She rubbed them with the heel of her hand.

"When did you last eat?" Theo asked.

"Lunch. Maybe."

He stood, chair legs scraping the floor. "Stay there."

She watched him move through her kitchen like he knew where everything lived. The bread box. The cheese drawer. The jar of Mara's apricot preserves from last summer. He didn't ask. He assembled two plates with the efficiency of someone who had fed people in cramped archives during research marathons, who had learned that hunger made for bad decisions.

He set a plate beside her stack. Thick slices of sourdough, sharp cheddar, a spoonful of preserves. A glass of water beside the mug.

"Eat," he said.

She picked up a piece of bread. The cheese was cold against her fingers. The preserves tasted like August sun captured in sugar. She chewed slowly, forcing herself to taste instead of inhale.

Theo returned to his seat. He didn't resume reading immediately. He watched her eat with an attention that made her skin warm.

"What?" she asked around a mouthful.

"Nothing. You just. You forget to stop."

"I don't forget. I prioritize."

"Same thing." He picked up his own sandwich. "The 1992 clause. What if we attribute it to the council record? 'As recorded in the council minutes of March third, the harvest clause was drafted by the sitting council and presented for the Warden's signature.'"

Wren swallowed. "That's better. Honest without being defensive."

"Honest is the only defense that holds."

She reached for her pencil, then hesitated. The pages blurred slightly. She blinked hard.

A hand settled on her shoulder. Warm through the thin cotton of her sweater. Thumb resting near her collarbone. Fingers curved over the slope of muscle.

Theo didn't say anything. Didn't move closer. Just kept his hand there, a steady weight, while she breathed.

Her grandmother's hands had done this. Same spot. Same pressure. *You're carrying too much, little bird. Let the paper hold it for a while.*

Wren covered his hand with her own. His skin was rougher than she expected. Calluses on the index and middle fingers from pencils, from turning thousands of pages. A small scar at the base of the thumb she hadn't noticed before.

"Your grandmother," he said quietly. "She did that too?"

"Every time I stayed up too late with the finding ledgers. She'd say the debts wait for no one, but the living need sleep."

"She sounds like she knew you well."

"She knew the gift. She knew what it took." Wren's voice thinned. "She didn't let me pay the big ones. She stepped in. Every time."

The hand on her shoulder didn't flinch. Didn't pull away.

"I think about that," she said. "About what she gave up so I wouldn't have to. And I think about your grandmother. Harriet. Ten years gone. Nineteen years of not knowing where she was or what happened." She turned her head slightly. His face was close in the lamplight. The bridge of his nose. The line of his jaw. The faint crease between his eyebrows when he concentrated. "You've spent your adult life trying to give her back what was taken. Or at least to know the shape of the absence."

"My adult life," he repeated. "And the years before. I was seven when it happened. Old enough to know something was wrong. Young enough that no one would tell me what."

"Same," she whispered. "My father left when I was six. My mother said he had to go. That it was for the best. She didn't tell me why until I was seventeen. And even then, she didn't tell me everything."

"People think they're protecting you."

"They are. That's the problem." She laughed once, short and brittle. "Protection feels exactly like love until you realize it's also a cage."

Theo's thumb moved. A slow stroke across the fabric of her sweater. Back and forth. Once. Twice.

"I didn't come here to publish a story," he said. "I came for the gap in the index. For Harriet. But the longer I stay..." He stopped. His hand stilled. "The longer I stay, the less the gap feels like the point."

Wren's heart beat harder. Not panic. Something steadier. Something that had been building since the first day he walked into the general store with his county credentials and his careful questions.

"What's the point, then?" she asked.

He looked at her. Really looked. The way he looked at a document that finally yielded its secret after weeks of silence.

"You," he said. "This. The work. The valley. All of it."

The nearly said thing hung between them. She saw it in the way his breath caught. In the minute tightening of his fingers on her shoulder. In the raw honesty of his face, stripped of every archival defense.

*I want to stay. I want this to be where I stay.*

She knew because she felt the same. A terrifying, expanding certainty that had no precedent in her experience. Finders located things. They didn't plant roots. The gift pulled you toward what was lost, not toward what you could keep.

But Theo wasn't lost. He was here. Solid and patient and eating her cheese at midnight while the valley's complete history waited for its final polish.

"Stay," she said.

The word came out before she could shape it into something safer. A request. A question. A plea.

Theo didn't answer immediately. He studied her face like he was reading a damaged text, reconstructing meaning from fragments.

"Stay for how long?" he asked.

"For the publication. For the Archive opening. For whatever comes after."

"And if what comes after is me going back to Millbrook? To Harriet. To the county index and the quiet life I built?"

"Then you go." Her voice surprised her with its steadiness. "But you come back. Or I come to you. Or we figure out something that isn't distance." She swallowed. "I'm not good at this. I've never. The gift doesn't work on people. I can't find what I want. I can only find what's missing."

"And I'm not lost."

"No. You're here."

"I am." His hand slid from her shoulder to the back of his neck. Fingers curling into her hair. "Wren."

She leaned into the touch. A small movement. A large surrender.

"I want you to stay," she said again. "Not because the valley needs you. Not because the publication needs you. Because I need you. Here. With me. That's selfish. The gift doesn't work like that. None of this does." She laughed again, wet this time. "But it's true."

Theo's other hand came up. Cupped her face. Thumb brushing the corner of her eye.

"You're allowed to want things," he said. "You're allowed to keep things. The gift takes. That doesn't mean you have to give everything else away."

"I don't know how not to."

"Then we learn together."

He kissed her.

Not a question this time. Not a test. A statement written in pressure and warmth and the taste of cheddar and preserves. His mouth moved against hers with the same deliberation he brought to everything. As if he were memorizing. As if he were archiving.

Wren's hands found his shoulders. His arms. The back of his neck. She pulled him closer, and he came willingly, one hand still in her hair, the other settling at her waist.

When they separated, foreheads touching, breathing shared air, the clock read 12:17.

"Proofs," Theo murmured.

"Proofs," she agreed.

But neither moved immediately. The pages could wait. The history could wait. Drake and his partial record and his threats could wait.

For the first time in her life, Wren Castellano was not looking for something missing.

She had found it.
