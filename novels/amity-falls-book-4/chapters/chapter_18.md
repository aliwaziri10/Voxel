The council chambers smelled of pine oil and old paper, the same scent that had greeted every emergency session since Wren could remember. Yusuf stood at the front of the room, his hands flat on the podium, and the silence that held the fifty-odd chairs was the heavy kind that comes when people know the shape of what is coming before the words arrive.

Wren sat in the second row, Theo beside her, his notebook open but his pen still. She had not expected to feel this calm. The past year had taught her that panic was a luxury the valley could not afford, not when the alternative was Drake's version of the story hitting the wires first.

"Ten days," Yusuf said. His voice carried without amplification. "That is the window we have. Drake's contact at the *Millbrook Gazette* has a print deadline of the twenty-third. If we want the complete record in front of the county before his partial one lands, the press has to run by the thirteenth."

A murmur moved through the room. Walt Pruitt, three rows back, shifted in his seat. Odette Reyes, near the window, did not move at all.

"Thirteen days from today," Yusuf continued. "That means the final manuscript goes to the printer on the twelfth. Which means Wren and Theo have until the eleventh to deliver something that holds together. The archives team has until the ninth to verify every citation. The council has until the eighth to approve the final text."

He let that sit.

"I know what I am asking. I also know what happens if we do not meet it. Drake has spent nineteen years building his hold. He has a damning record, and he has the platform to publish it. The only thing he does not have is the full truth. We do. But the full truth only matters if it arrives first."

Wren felt Theo's shoulder brush hers as he leaned forward to write. The contact was ordinary, the kind that had become ordinary over months of shared tables and shared silence. She did not look at him. She watched Yusuf.

"The vote is simple," Yusuf said. "Do we accelerate the publication timeline to ten days from today, understanding that this will require every available hand, that mistakes are more likely, and that we will not have the luxury of a second draft? Or do we keep the original six-week schedule and accept that Drake's version will be the one the county reads?"

No one spoke for a long moment. Then Mara Voss stood. She did not look at Caleb, who sat beside her. She looked at Yusuf.

"We accelerate," she said. "The valley has survived worse on less notice."

Caleb stood a beat later. "We accelerate."

Priya Nair rose from the front row, her deputy's uniform crisp, her face unreadable. "We accelerate."

Dev Nair, seated beside her, did not stand. He raised his hand, a small gesture, and Yusuf nodded.

"Dev?"

"The evidence is ready," Dev said. His voice was quiet, the same tone he used when logging substation readings. "The gap in Drake's account is documented. The timeline of Denise's renewals is documented. Whitlock's letter is documented. We have the pieces. We need the time to assemble them. Ten days is tight. It is not impossible."

"Then it is settled," Yusuf said. "We accelerate."

The vote was not a show of hands. It was the sound of chairs scraping back as people stood, one by one, until the room was on its feet. Odette Reyes was the last. She moved slowly, her cane tapping the floor, and when she reached the aisle she turned to face the room.

"Forty-three years," she said. "That is how long I have kept the wording of my husband's proposal in a box I could not open. The joy stayed. The words went. If this valley can give the words back to everyone who lost them, then ten days is a gift. We take it."

She walked out. The others followed, a stream of neighbors moving toward the door with the purpose of people who had just agreed to do something difficult together.

Wren stayed seated until the room thinned. Theo stayed beside her.

"Ten days," he said, capping his pen. "That is not a lot of time for a manuscript that needs to survive a libel review."

"It is the time we have," Wren said. She gathered her own notes, a stack of index cards held together by a rubber band. "The archive has the bones. We have the narrative. The rest is stitching."

"You make it sound simple."

"Not simple. Necessary. There is a difference."

Theo smiled, a small thing that crinkled the corners of his eyes. "Right. Necessary. I knew there was a reason I liked your definition better."

He packed his notebook into his bag. Wren watched the motion, the familiar rhythm of his hands. She had memorized the way he organized things, color-coded tabs for source types, a separate pocket for original documents. She had memorized the way he paused before writing something difficult, the breath he took as if bracing against a current.

She had also memorized the way he looked at her when he thought she was not watching. The way his expression softened, the way something in his posture shifted from investigator to something else, something he had not named and neither had she.

Priya Nair waited by the door. She held a clipboard, her posture the same procedural stillness she wore on patrol. Her eyes moved from Theo's bag to Wren's index cards to the space between their chairs.

"Transport," Priya said. "The county van leaves for the print shop in forty minutes. If you need the original ledgers moved, now is the window."

"We don't need the originals moved," Wren said. "We need the scanned copies verified against the originals. Dev's team is handling that."

"Dev's team is three people and a high school intern," Priya said. "I am authorizing two deputies to assist with chain of custody. The ledgers stay in the archive. The scans go to the print shop. No originals leave this building."

"Agreed."

Priya nodded once. Her gaze flicked to Theo, then back to Wren. She opened her mouth, closed it, and wrote something on her clipboard instead.

"Forty minutes," she repeated. "I will have the van at the back door."

She left without another word.

Theo exhaled. "She noticed."

"Priya notices everything. It is her job."

"No, I mean — she noticed *us*." He lowered his voice. "The thing we have not named. She saw it and she filed it and she said nothing."

Wren felt a flush climb her neck. "Priya is my friend. She's yours too. She knows how to keep a confidence."

"Does she."

"She kept Dev's secret for years. Denise's for fifty. She can keep this one for ten days."

Theo looked at her, really looked, the way he did when he was reading a document for the second time and catching what he missed the first. "This one is different. This one is yours."

"All of them are mine. That is the point."

She stood, sliding her index cards into her coat pocket. The rubber band snapped against the cardboard, a small sharp sound in the quieting room.

"Come on," she said. "We have a manuscript to finish. And if we are very lucky, we might finish it before the valley decides to hold another emergency meeting about something else entirely."

They walked out together into the afternoon light. The valley spread below the council building, orchards and gardens and the silver thread of the creek, all of it ordinary in the way that only a place with secrets can be ordinary. The satellite maps still showed empty forest a few hundred yards off. The GPS still routed travelers around a town that did not exist on any official record.

Wren had spent her childhood thinking that made her special. She knew better now. It made her responsible.

"The press runs in ten days," Theo said, falling into step beside her. "Ten days to write the history that Ambrose Whitlock tried to end, that Denise renewed in secret, that Drake wants to weaponize. Ten days to get it right."

"We have been getting it right for a year," Wren said. "We just have to do it faster."

"That is not how writing works."

"Then we will write it the way the valley does everything else. One piece at a time. One hand at a time. And we will not stop until it is done."

They reached the archive building, a converted barn behind the general store. The door was already open, Dev's voice audible inside directing the high school intern — Jenna, Wren thought, Mara's niece — on the proper handling of the 1942 ledger.

Theo held the door for her. She walked past him into the cool dimness, the smell of dust and ink, and felt the familiar settle in her chest. This was the work. This had always been the work.

She did not look back to see if he followed. She knew he would. He always did.
