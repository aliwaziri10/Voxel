The ledger sat open on the table between them, pages yellowed at the edges and smudged with decades of fingerprint oil. Wren traced a column of entries with her fingertip, stopping at a name that meant nothing to her: *Holloway & Finch, Ltd.*

"The same company appears in the 1998 renewal," Theo said. He sat across from her, a magnifying glass in one hand and a legal pad in the other. "Three payments. Five thousand each. Listed as 'consulting fees.'"

Wren looked up. Late afternoon light slanted through the archive windows, catching dust motes above the stacks. "Consulting for what?"

"That's the question." Theo flipped a page in his notebook. "I checked the county business registry this morning. Holloway & Finch was incorporated in 1995. Dissolved in 2003. The registered agent was a law firm in Millbrook — Grayson, Mills & Associates."

"Which means?"

"Which means the same firm handled the property transfers for the land Drake bought outside Millbrook in 2001. The land where he built the house he never lived in." Theo set the magnifying glass down. "It's a shell. Always was. The money moved through it toward something else, and the something else leads back to him."

Wren leaned back, the chair creaking. The archive smelled of paper and lemon oil and something older, something that might have been the valley itself pressing against the walls. "So he's been building this for longer than we thought. Before the ring. Before Denise."

"Nineteen years, at least." Theo's voice flattened, the way it did when he was suppressing something. "Since Millbrook. Since my grandmother."

The words hung between them. Harriet Marsh. Ten years of her life gone, the decade before 2007 erased by whatever Drake had attempted in Millbrook. Nineteen years ago, he'd tried something there and failed, but not before he took something from Theo's family that could never be returned.

Wren reached across the table and covered his hand with hers. His fingers were cold.

"You didn't come here for the county index gap," she said quietly. "You came for her."

Theo didn't pull away. His thumb brushed the edge of her wrist, a small motion, barely there. "I came for both. The gap brought me. She kept me."

The ledger blurred at the edges of her vision. Wren blinked hard, swallowing against the sudden thickness in her throat. She had spent so long being protected from truths — her father's leaving, her grandmother's death, the nature of the debts she would inherit. Theo had spent just as long hunting a truth no one would give him.

They were not the same. But they rhymed.

"We need to know what the shell company was paying for," Wren said, pulling her hand back to steady herself. "If Drake has been moving money through Holloway & Finch for decades, there's a trail. Bank records. Tax filings. Something."

"Most of it's past the retention window," Theo said. "But not all. The law firm — Grayson, Mills — they were acquired by a larger practice in 2010. Their archives went to a storage facility in Harrisburg. I put in a request this morning. They'll pull the boxes by Tuesday."

"Tuesday." Wren nodded slowly. "And in the meantime?"

"In the meantime, we wait." Theo gathered his notes, stacking them with precise corners aligned. "Unless you have another way to find what we're looking for."

The words landed in the space between them like a stone in still water.

Wren looked at the ledger. At the name that meant nothing. At the dates that stretched back before she was born.

*Finder,* the voice in her blood whispered. *You are a Finder. Find it.*

She had done small searches before. A lost wedding ring buried in garden soil. A child's toy left behind at the market. A letter misfiled in the archive that turned up in a coat pocket three weeks later. Each search had cost her something small — a note in a drawer she couldn't remember writing, a thank-you from a stranger she couldn't recall helping, a gap the size of a breath held too long.

She had never paid a large debt. Her grandmother had warned her: *The gift takes what it's owed. You don't choose the price. You only choose whether to search.*

But that wasn't quite true. Wren knew it now, a year after the ring ritual, a year after watching Dev distribute the weight of his taking across ten volunteers, a year after learning that the bargain's terms could be faced and named and survived. You chose the search. You chose the reason. The price came after, but the choice was yours.

"Wren?" Theo's voice cut through the spiral. "You're doing that thing where you go somewhere else."

She met his eyes. Hazel, flecked with gold. Patient. Waiting.

"I could find it," she said. "The payments. What they were for. Where the money went after Holloway & Finch."

Theo went still. "You'd use the gift."

"Yes."

"For this." Not a question. A confirmation. "A financial trail. Corporate records. That's not a lost object, Wren. That's not a person. The gift finds *lost things*. Things that want to be found, or things that have been misplaced. This is deliberate concealment. Layers of it."

"I know." Her voice steadied. "But the money went somewhere. The records exist somewhere. They're lost to *us*, not to the world. That's enough."

"Is it?" Theo's jaw tightened. "You've never done a search this large. You don't know what it will cost."

"I know what the small ones cost. I know the gaps." She stood, pushing the chair back. "And I know that if we wait until Tuesday, Drake gains three more days. Three more days to shape his narrative. Three more days to decide what pieces of his partial record he releases first."

"He can't publish without the original terms," Theo said. "That's what he wants. That's his edge."

"And if he finds another way to get them? If someone else has them? If the law firm's boxes contain something we don't expect?" Wren moved to the window, pressing her palm against the cool glass. "We're not just racing his publication, Theo. We're racing his *control* of the story. Every day we wait, he decides what the valley looks like to the outside world."

The valley. Her home. The orchard roads and the creek and the general store where Odette still kept the memory of her husband's proposal intact even without the words. The council chamber where Yusuf had hesitated and then acted. The substation where Dev worked through evidence with the exacting care of someone who knew exactly what procedural rigor meant.

Her mother, alive and whole in the house on Maple Street, who had never once lied to her but had also never once volunteered the hardest truths.

Wren turned back. "I'm going to do it."

"Theo —"

"No." She held up a hand. "Not 'Theo, don't.' Not 'Theo, talk me out of it.' I'm telling you because you deserve to know. Because you're the one who taught me that records matter, that the truth survives best when it's documented and witnessed. Because if something goes wrong, you're the one who'll know what I was looking for."

Theo rose slowly. The magnifying glass caught the light, throwing a fractured rainbow across the ledger pages. "What are you searching for?"

"Everything Holloway & Finch touched. Every payment. Every transfer. Every name attached to the money after it left that company." Wren's fingers curled against the windowsill. "The complete financial architecture of Corwin Drake's nineteen-year investigation. I want to know what he knows. All of it."

"And the cost?"

Wren exhaled. "I don't know. That's not how it works. I'll know after."

The silence stretched. Theo's expression moved through calculation, through frustration, through something that looked like fear before settling into a quiet acceptance that terrified her more than any argument would have.

"Okay," he said. "How do you want to do this?"

The question undid something in her chest. Not *don't*. Not *be careful*. *How do you want to do this.*

"Here," she said. "In the archive. With the ledger as the anchor. The physical object connects to the trail."

Theo nodded once. He moved to the desk, clearing a space beside the ledger, setting out a fresh legal pad and two pens. "I'll document everything. What you say. What you do. The time. The conditions. If there's a gap — if you lose something — I'll write it down so you don't have to wonder."

Wren's throat clicked. "You'd do that."

"I'd do that." He didn't look at her. "I've spent nineteen years documenting what was taken from my grandmother. I know what it means to have the record when the memory fails."

The words settled between them: *when the memory fails.* Not *if.* *When.*

Wren moved to the chair. She sat, spine straight, hands flat on the table. The ledger lay open to the Holloway & Finch entries. Three payments. Five thousand each. 1998.

"Ready when you are," Theo said. His pen hovered over the paper.

Wren closed her eyes.

She breathed in the archive smell — paper, lemon oil, valley. She felt the wood of the table under her palms, the creak of the floorboards under Theo's weight as he shifted. She heard the distant hum of the refrigerator in the break room, the tick of the wall clock, the rustle of Theo's pen against paper as he wrote *15:47 - search initiated.*

*Find,* she thought, and pushed.

The sensation was familiar and entirely new. The small searches had felt like reaching into a dark closet — fingers brushing fabric, locating the shape of a coat, pulling it into light. This was different. This was stepping off a cliff into fog.

Her consciousness extended, not outward but *along* — following the thin silver thread that connected the ledger entry to the money it recorded, and from the money to the hands that had passed it, and from the hands to the accounts that received it, and from the accounts to the decisions that directed it.

*Shell company. Law firm. Holding account. Another shell. Another. A trust. A foundation. A name.*

The thread multiplied, branching, fraying, knitting back together in patterns that hurt to follow. Nineteen years of financial architecture unfolding in seconds that felt like hours. She saw Drake's hand in all of it — not always directly, not always visibly, but the logic of it bore his signature. Patient. Precise. Building a case file the size of a life.

*Millbrook property. Surveillance equipment. Payments to a private investigator named R. Calder. Payments to a genealogist named M. Voss — no, not Mara, a different Voss, a researcher in Connecticut. Payments to a document recovery specialist. Payments to a woman in Ohio who had once worked for a records office in a county that didn't exist on maps.*

The thread pulled her deeper. She saw the shape of what Drake had built: not just a record of the valley's bargain renewals, but a map of every person who had ever touched the bargain. Every Warden who had shifted. Every Mender who had healed. Every Caller who had compelled. Every Finder who had searched. Every debt paid. Every memory taken.

He knew the lineages. He knew the costs. He knew the names.

*Wren Castellano. Finder. Daughter of —*

The thread snapped.

Wren gasped, her hands clutching the table edge. The archive rushed back — the light, the dust, the smell, Theo's pen scratching *15:52 - subject reports sudden termination, respiratory distress.*

"Wren." Theo's hand on her shoulder. Warm. Solid. "Wren, breathe. Talk to me. What did you see?"

She sucked in air, her lungs burning. The images fragmented, dissolving like ink in water. She caught pieces — *Drake knows the lineages. He has a map. He has names. He has —*

"He knows," she gasped. "He knows the lineages. All of them. He has a database. Names. Dates. Debts paid. He's been building it for nineteen years."

"Okay." Theo's voice was calm. Anchoring. "Okay. That's good. That's important. What else?"

She shook her head, pressing her palms against her eyes. "I don't — the thread broke. I hit something and it broke. A wall. A —" She stopped. Her fingers found a gap.

A gap.

Not a small one. Not a note in a drawer or a thank-you from a stranger.

She couldn't remember her grandmother's face.

The realization hit her sideways. Her mother's face came clear as the ledger on the table. Dev's face, Priya's, Yusuf's, Odette's. Caleb laughing in the orchard, Mara wiping grease from her hands, Denise sitting in the sunlight on the porch.

But her grandmother — the woman who had taught her to read the threads, who had held her hand through her first search, who had died when Wren was sixteen — her face was gone. Smooth. Featureless. A blank oval where memories should live.

*Her voice,* Wren thought, panic rising cold. *I can't remember her voice.*

"Wren?" Theo's hand tightened on her shoulder. "What is it? What's wrong?"

She opened her eyes. The archive swam. "My grandmother. I can't — her face. Her voice. The memory of her is —"

She stopped. The gap yawned, vast and clean. Not a note in a drawer. Not a puzzled thank-you.

A person. Her grandmother. The person who had taught her what it meant to be a Finder.

*The gift takes what it's owed. You don't choose the price. You only choose whether to search.*

Her grandmother's voice. She remembered the *words*. She just couldn't remember the *sound*.

"Oh," Wren whispered. "Oh."

Theo's other hand came up, cupping her face. His thumbs brushed tears she hadn't felt falling. "Hey. Hey. Look at me."

She looked.

"You chose this," he said quietly. "You chose the search. You chose the reason. The price — the price isn't punishment. It's the cost of the truth. And you got the truth. Drake has a database. He knows the lineages. He knows names. That's what we needed."

"I lost her," Wren said, the words breaking. "I lost the only memory I had of her that was just hers. Not her teaching me. Not her warning me. Just — her. The way she smelled like lavender and ink. The way she hummed when she cooked. The sound of her voice saying my name."

"I know." Theo's forehead touched hers. "I know. And I'm so sorry. But you *chose* it. That's the difference. No one protected you from this. No one decided for you. You walked into it with your eyes open."

"Did I?" The question came out raw. "Did I really? Or did I just think I could afford it?"

"You afforded it." Theo pulled back, searching her face. "You're here. You're breathing. You're asking the question. That means you afforded it."

Wren let out a shuddering breath. Her hands found his wrists, holding on. The gap throbbed, a phantom limb sensation for a memory that no longer existed.

"What else?" Theo asked gently. "What else did you see?"

She closed her eyes again, sifting through the fragments. *Drake's database. The lineages. Names. Dates. A timeline of every bargain renewal since the founding. The original terms — he doesn't have those. He has everything *but* those. That's why he needs them. That's his edge.*

"He doesn't have the original terms," she said. "He has everything around them. The renewals. The payments. The people. But not the source document. Not the first bargain."

"Which means he still needs us to publish first," Theo said. "Or he needs to find the original terms another way."

"Which he won't." Wren opened her eyes. "Because they don't exist as a document. They exist as a *practice*. Ambrose Whitlock knew that. He tried to end it and failed and hid the truth instead of destroying it. The terms are the practice itself. The memory-for-protection exchange. That's all there is."

Theo's pen scratched. *15:55 - subject reports antagonist lacks original terms, holds comprehensive secondary record.*

"Good," Theo said. "That's good. That changes the timeline."

"Does it?"

"It means we don't have to find a document that doesn't exist. We have to publish the *truth of the practice*. The complete history. The renewals. The costs. The lineages. All of it. Honestly. Before he releases his partial version."

Wren nodded slowly. The gap in her mind pulsed, a dull ache behind her eyes. "We have what we need. The ledger connected to the shell company. The shell company connected to Drake. The scope of his knowledge. The gap in his knowledge."

"Then we're done here." Theo capped his pen. "For today."

He began gathering the ledger, his notes, the magnifying glass. Efficient. Methodical. The way he always was.

Wren watched him, the ache settling into something quieter. A hollow space where her grandmother used to live.

"You're not going to ask if I'm okay," she said.

Theo paused, the ledger half-closed in his hands. He set it down and turned to face her fully.

"Are you okay?"

"No."

"Then I won't ask." He pulled out the chair beside hers and sat, close enough that his shoulder brushed hers. "I'll stay. That's what I do. I stay."

The words were simple. Ordinary. The kind of thing anyone might say.

But Theo didn't say things he didn't mean. He didn't offer comfort he wasn't willing to sustain. He had spent nineteen years staying with a grandmother who couldn't remember the decade that shaped her, visiting her every week, reading her the letters she'd written but couldn't recall receiving, building a record of a life she couldn't hold.

He knew what staying meant.

Wren leaned into him, her head finding his shoulder. His arm came around her, steady and undemanding.

"I can't remember her face," she said into the fabric of his shirt. "I can't remember the sound of her voice. I paid for the truth with the only memory I had of her that was just *her*."

"I know."

"Was it worth it?"

Theo was quiet for a long moment. His fingers traced a slow pattern on her arm.

"You found what Drake has," he said finally. "You found what he doesn't. You found the shape of his advantage and the hole in it. That truth protects everyone in this valley. Mara. Caleb. Priya. Dev. Odette. Denise. Yusuf. Walt. Your mother. *Me.*"

He tilted his head, his cheek pressing against her hair.

"Was *she* worth it?"

The question caught Wren off guard. She pulled back enough to see his face.

"What?"

"Your grandmother." Theo's eyes were clear, direct. "Was she worth the memory of her?"

The answer came without thought. "Yes. Always. She was — she was everything. She taught me how to live with this gift. She showed me that the debts weren't punishments. They were the cost of doing something that mattered."

"Then yes." Theo's thumb caught a tear on her cheek. "It was worth it. She'd have said the same. The truth matters. The valley matters. *You* matter. And you chose it. That's what makes it yours."

Wren stared at him. At the man who had walked into her valley a year ago with a county index gap and a grandmother's stolen decade, who had stayed through the ring ritual and the publication planning and every long day in this archive, who had never once tried to fix her or protect her or decide what she could handle.

*With Theo beside her rather than instead of her.*

The thought crystallized, sharp and true. This was the choice. Not the search — that had been a choice too, but this was the one that mattered. Telling the truth herself. On her terms. With him beside her.

"I'm going to write it," she said. "The complete history. All of it. Adelaide's letters. Whitlock's letter. The ring ritual. Denise's fifty years. Caleb's two years. Dev's lineage. Drake's database. Everything. I'm going to write it and we're going to publish it before he can."

Theo's expression softened into something that might have been pride. "We are."

"And after?"

"After, we figure out what the valley looks like when it doesn't have to hide."

Wren let out a long breath, feeling something settle in her chest — not peace, not yet, but a kind of rightness. The shape of a decision made.

"Stay," she said. "Just for a while longer."

Theo's arm tightened around her. "I'm not going anywhere."

They sat in the archive as the light faded, the ledger closed between them, the shell company's trail documented, the first large debt paid. Outside, the valley moved through its evening — Odette closing the store, Priya finishing her shift, Dev logging evidence, Yusuf reviewing the next council agenda, Walt walking his dog past the orchard fence.

And Wren Castellano, Finder, sat in the circle of Theo Marsh's arm, missing her grandmother's face but holding the truth she had chosen to find.
