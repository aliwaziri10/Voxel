Theo spread the hand-drawn calendar across the kitchen table, each square dense with his cramped handwriting. Week one: *master manuscript assembly — Adelaide's letters, Whitlock's letter, Denise's testimony, the ring ritual record, Drake's known file.* Week two: *lineage review sessions — Wardens, Menders, Callers, Weathers, Finders, Wicks.* Week three: *first galley proof.* Week four: *distribution to valley households.* Week five: *Millbrook County recorder's office, Hollow Creek Gazette, three regional papers.* Week six: *second printing, corrections, annotations.*

He'd color-coded it in three ballpoint pens. Blue for tasks he could do alone. Red for tasks requiring Wren. Green for tasks requiring the council.

Wren stood at the counter, coffee cooling in her hands, reading upside down. Morning light through the window caught the steam rising from her mug, made it visible for a moment before it dispersed.

"Six weeks," she said.

"Six weeks from a finished manuscript," Theo corrected. "Which we don't have yet. But the timeline holds if we start Monday."

"Monday is three days away."

"Then we'd better finish the manuscript by Sunday."

She set the mug down. "You have 'serialized release' written in week four. What does that mean?"

Theo tapped the square. "Means we don't drop a three-hundred-page book on people all at once. Means we release it in sections. The bargain's origin. The renewals. The cost. The ring. Drake's file. Let people absorb one piece before the next."

"Or," Wren said, "it means you control the narrative arc. You decide what hits first, what lands hardest. You build tension."

"I build clarity."

"You build a story." She turned the calendar toward herself, studying the red squares. "The valley isn't a story, Theo. It's people's lives. My mother's life. Dev's. Denise's. You chop it into installments and you make it consumable. You make it *entertainment*."

The kitchen was quiet except for the refrigerator's hum. Outside, a truck rumbled past on Main — Walt Pruitt's old Ford, by the sound of it, heading for the orchard.

"I'm not trying to entertain anyone," Theo said evenly. "I'm trying to make sure the truth survives the first reading. You hand someone a phone book of atrocities, they shut down. They skim. They miss the parts that matter. Serialization forces attention."

"It forces a frame," Wren countered. "Your frame. You've already decided the bargain's origin is chapter one. Why not start with the ring? With ten people choosing to share a debt? Why not start with Denise walking free?"

"Because the origin explains the ring. The renewals explain the cost. Context before catharsis — that's not a frame, Wren. That's how people understand."

"It's how *you* understand. You're an archivist. You think in chronology. But the valley doesn't work in chronology. It works in — " She stopped, pressing her fingers to the table's edge. "It works in *obligation*. In what we owe each other. The ring isn't the end of the story. It's the *answer* to the story. If you put it at the end, you make it feel like resolution. It's not resolution. It's what we do now."

Theo was quiet. His pen hovered over the calendar, blue cap clicked on and off.

"Okay," he said finally. "Make a case for a different order. Right now. On this calendar. Red ink."

Wren picked up the red pen. Her hand hovered, then drew a line through week one. *Master manuscript assembly* became *Master manuscript assembly — thematic, not chronological.*

"Start with the cost," she said, writing in the margins. "Not the origin. The cost. What the bargain takes. Memory. Identity. Years. Start there because that's what every family in this valley knows in their bones. Then the renewals — how it kept happening. Then the origin — why it started. Then the ring — how we stopped it. Then Drake — who tried to weaponize it. Then the new terms — what we choose now."

Theo watched her write. His expression was unreadable, the one he wore when he was filing something away for later.

"That puts the ring in week three instead of week five," he said. "Compresses the review sessions."

"Good. The lineages don't need two weeks to read their own histories. They need two days. They've been living it."

"And if they disagree with your thematic framing?"

"Then they disagree in the record. Their objections go in the margins. That's the point."

Theo recapped his pen. "You're proposing we publish the arguments alongside the facts."

"I'm proposing we publish the *truth*. Which includes the arguments."

He was quiet longer this time. The refrigerator clicked off. Walt's truck returned, slower now, turning into the orchard drive.

"Fine," Theo said. "Thematic. Cost first. But the lineage review sessions stay two weeks. You don't get to rush the Wardens because you're impatient."

"I'm not impatient. I'm *careful*."

"You're terrified." He said it without heat, like he was naming a bird at a feeder. "You're terrified that if this moves fast, something slips. Something gets lost. Or worse — something gets *added*."

Wren's grip tightened on the pen. "Drake has a partial record. He's waiting for us to make a mistake. If we serialize, if we frame, if we *editorialize* — we hand him the correction. He publishes his version the day after ours, and his version says 'they lied, here's what they left out.'"

"So we don't leave anything out."

"We won't *know* what we've left out until someone tells us. That's why the review sessions matter. That's why they need time."

Theo stood, stretched. His back cracked. "Two weeks. Thematic order. Objections in the margins. Secure printing press. Distribution plan. You want to take the calendar to Yusuf, or should I?"

"I'll take it. You stay here and — " She gestured at the scattered papers, the half-assembled manuscript stacks. "Make sure the cost section is ready for Monday. The real cost. Not the ledger version. The *lived* version."

Theo's face shifted. A flicker of something softer. "I'll interview Mara. And Caleb. And Priya. And Dev. Separately. Their words. Not mine."

Wren nodded once. "Good. Good. Okay."

She gathered the calendar, the pens, her cold coffee. At the door, she paused.

"Theo."

"Yeah?"

"The first kiss square. You didn't put one on the calendar."

Theo didn't look up from the manuscript. "Didn't think it had a deadline."

Wren left before she could smile.

The council chamber smelled of lemon oil and old paper, the same scent Wren had known since she was tall enough to see over the table's edge. Yusuf sat at the head, his chair no higher than the others but positioned to catch the window light. To his left: Odette Reyes, ledger open, fountain pen poised. To his right: Mara Voss, one hand resting on the table, the other curled loose at her side. Caleb Reyes leaned against the back wall, arms crossed. Priya Nair sat straight-backed, uniform crisp. Dev Nair beside her, civilian clothes, one knee bounced once before he stilled it. Walt Pruitt occupied the designated citizen seat, a legal pad balanced on his knees.

Empty chairs for the Warden representative — Mara stood for both lineages now, a dual burden she'd accepted without ceremony — and the Wicks. The Wicks' chair had been empty for thirty years.

"Item one," Yusuf said. "Publication timeline. Wren."

Wren stood. The calendar felt heavy in her hand, not from paper weight. She set it on the table, smoothed it flat.

"Six weeks from manuscript completion," she said. "Thematic structure. Cost, renewals, origin, ring, Drake, new terms. Serialized release — one section per week to valley households, simultaneous release to Millbrook and regional outlets. Lineage review sessions begin Monday, two weeks total. All objections, corrections, and supplemental testimony filed as marginalia in the final record."

She looked up. Seven faces. Seven pairs of eyes.

"Questions?"

Odette didn't look up from her ledger. "Who determines when the manuscript is complete?"

"The lineages," Wren said. "When the last objection is filed, the manuscript is done. Not before."

"And if a lineage refuses to file?"

"Then we publish with their refusal noted. The record shows they were invited. The record shows they declined."

Caleb pushed off the wall. "And the printing press? You mentioned secure printing."

"Item two," Yusuf said.

Wren flipped the calendar. The reverse side held a budget, a vendor list, a security protocol drafted in Theo's hand.

"Reyes Orchard press," she said. "Caleb's equipment. Modified for security — numbered plates, watermarked paper, locked storage between runs. Total cost: four thousand, eight hundred dollars. Council fund covers three thousand. The rest from the Archive's operating budget, which Theo and I have been building since January."

"Archive operating budget," Walt repeated. "From what revenue?"

"Documentation fees," Wren said. "Finder consultations. Warning project contracts with Hollow Creek and Millbrook emergency services. The valley gets paid for what we do now. Not much. But enough."

Yusuf tapped the budget. "Four thousand eight hundred. Secure press. Numbered plates. Watermarks. This is... thorough."

"It needs to be," Priya said. Her voice carried the room's attention without volume. "Drake has a partial record. If our print run has errors, gaps, inconsistencies — he publishes corrections. His version becomes the authoritative one by default. We don't get a second first impression."

"Which brings us to item three," Yusuf said. "Lineage vetting."

The room shifted. Chairs creaked. Walt's pen stopped mid-hover.

"The timeline proposes two weeks for lineage review," Yusuf continued. "But the council's authorization for funding comes with a condition. The record must be vetted by *all* lineages before any section goes to print. Not just invited to review — *vetted*. Signed off. Every lineage representative agrees the section is accurate, complete, and fair."

Wren's fingers curled around the table edge. "Every lineage."

"All six. Wardens, Menders, Callers, Weathers, Finders, Wicks."

Wren's mind raced. Five lineages. Two weeks. Six sections. That was two and a half days per section for five people to read, discuss, object, negotiate, and sign. The Wardens and Menders — Mara, doing double duty. The Callers — Priya, who'd have to read material that compelled her gift to activate every time she encountered a falsehood. The Weathers — she didn't know who held that seat now, old Hannah something, retired to a cabin above the falls. The Finders — herself, and Dev, though he'd recuse from his own testimony sections.

And every objection went to marginalia. Which meant every objection had to be *resolved* before the signature.

"Theo's timeline assumed sequential review," Wren said. "One section at a time. But with five signatories per section — "

"In two weeks."

Wren felt something loosen in her chest. Walt. Ordinary Walt, who'd sat at the counter eating pie while the valley rewrote itself around him, now offering to check their work against the town's memory.

"Thank you, Walt," she said. "That's — yes. Please."

Silence. The refrigerator hum of the building's old cooling system.

Seven hands rose. Seven. Including Walt's, though he wasn't a voting member — he just raised it anyway, and no one corrected him.

Wren exhaled. "Monday. Right. Okay."

The room stilled further.

"He'll move when we publish," Priya said. "Or before, if he thinks we're close to something he can't counter."

"Then we don't give him 'close,'" Yusuf said. "We give him 'done.' Fast. Clean. No gaps. The vetting condition isn't just about accuracy — it's about speed. Five signatures means no one can claim we rushed, we hid, we manipulated. The record stands on its own legs."

"And if a lineage *won't* sign?" Wren asked. "If someone refuses?"

"Then we publish their refusal," Yusuf said. "In the margins. With their name. The record shows we asked. The record shows they said no. Let the public decide what that means."

Wren thought of Dev. Of Priya. Of Mara carrying two lineages. Of the Weathers representative she didn't know, living alone above the falls. Of the empty Wicks chair.

"What if the Weathers representative won't engage?" she asked. "What if they're unreachable? What if they're — "

"Then we document the attempt," Yusuf said. "Registered mail. Personal delivery. Witnessed notification. We prove we tried. That's the standard."

Wren nodded slowly. "Okay. Monday. Two weeks. Five lineages. Thirty signatures. Secure press. Serialized release. Public record."

Drake. Gas station security footage, Route 9 northbound, forty minutes ago. Driving a rental. Pennsylvania plates."

She stopped at the Archive's threshold. Turned. The sun caught the sign's letters, threw them bright against the brick.

"The rupture," she said, "is when you realize that publishing the truth *is* the threat. That Drake isn't the danger. *We* are. The record we're building — it doesn't just expose him. It exposes everyone. Every family. Every choice. Every memory taken, every debt redirected, every year Denise renewed alone. It exposes *me*. My mother. My gift. The warnings I've issued, the things I've found, the memories I've paid."

Theo didn't move. Didn't speak. Waited.

"And you," she continued, "your publishing instincts — they're not wrong. They're *right*. Serialization works. Framing works. Narrative arc works. That's how you make people *read*. And I can't let you do that. But I can't stop you either, because if I do, Drake wins. So the rupture isn't between us. It's *in* the work. It's the fact that the only way to save the valley is to expose it. And the only way to expose it is to shape it. And the only way to shape it is to betray it."

Theo was very still. The Archive door swung shut behind her, but she stayed on the step, facing him, the heat rising between them like a visible thing.

"That's the rupture," she said. "Right there. You want to publish the truth so people understand. I want to publish the truth so people *survive*. Those aren't the same thing. And somewhere in the next two weeks, one of us is going to have to choose which one matters more."

Theo's face didn't change. But his hands, at his sides, curled into fists. Once. Released.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. That's — yes. That's it. That's the rupture."

"Good," Wren said. "Now we both know. Monday. Two weeks. Thirty signatures."

"Monday," Theo repeated. "I'll see you at the Archive. Six a.m. Manuscript assembly."

"Six a.m."

He turned. Walked toward the orchard road. Didn't look back.

Wren sat. Opened the folder. Began to read.

Outside, the valley continued. Walt's truck at the orchard. Odette's store bell. The falls' constant roar, muted by distance and brick. Drake somewhere on Route 9, or the interstate, or a motel room with a laptop and alerts set, waiting.

Monday came whether they were ready or not.
