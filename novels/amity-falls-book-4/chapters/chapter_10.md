Theo spread the county index pages across the kitchen table, the early light catching the pencil marks he had made in the margins. Three columns. Tasks. Dependencies. Deadlines. He had drawn them the night before, after Wren went to bed, the structure settling something in his chest that had been loose since the drive from Millbrook.

Wren came down the stairs in her wool socks, mug in hand, the steam curling past her face. She stopped at the doorway. The table had been clear yesterday. Now it held the shape of a campaign.

"What is this?"

"Publication timeline." Theo did not look up. He tapped the first column. "Research complete by end of week. That means every document we have. Adelaide's letter. Whitlock's letter. The ring ritual transcript. The county gap notation. Dev's toll-redirection evidence. Your grandmother's notes. The census records from the first renewal."

"And the second column?"

"Drafting. Two weeks. I write the narrative. You annotate the lineage sections. We cross-check against each other's work. No single voice carries the whole thing."

Wren set her mug on the counter. The ceramic clicked. "And the third?"

"Review. Vetting. Printing. Distribution." He finally met her eyes. "Eight weeks total if nothing catches. Ten if it does."

She crossed her arms. "Eight weeks. You've decided this on your own."

"I've drafted a timeline on my own. There's a difference." He capped his pen. "The council meets in three days. Yusuf said he'd put the press funding on the agenda. But he also said the funding depends on something. I wanted us to walk in with a plan instead of a request."

Wren moved to the table. She picked up the top page, turning it sideways. Her finger traced the pencil lines. "You've got the vetting phase at two weeks. Theo, the lineages don't work like that. The Wardens will want to see how their shifting is described. The Menders will need the healing costs named exactly. The Callers - Priya will read every word twice. The Weathers have three living members and they never agree on anything. Two weeks isn't vetting. Two weeks is a prayer."

"Then we build the timeline around reality." He reached for the page. "Give me the real numbers. I'll redraw it."

She handed it back. "The Finders will need the most time. My mother will read it. So will Dev. So will the three others in the valley. They'll want to know which memories are named and which are held back. They'll want to know if the cost of finding Drake's records is in there."

"It is."

"Is it? You wrote 'Finder lineage costs documented.' That's not the same thing."

Theo exhaled. He erased the two-week block. Extended it to four. "Better?"

"Closer. But you're still treating this like a county records request. You think if the paperwork is clean, the outcome is guaranteed."

"I think if the paperwork is clean, the opposition has nothing to grab onto. Drake's leverage is the partial record. He publishes gaps and calls them conspiracies. We publish the complete record and call it history. The difference is evidence."

"The difference is trust." Wren pulled out a chair. She sat without invitation. "You're asking people to hand over their family stories - the ones they've protected for generations - and you've scheduled it like a property survey."

"I'm asking them to hand over their family stories so the valley doesn't lose the right to tell them at all." Theo's voice flattened. "Drake has a printer lined up in Millbrook. He has a distribution list. He has a lawyer who specializes in historical disclosure claims. He is not waiting for consensus. If we don't move, he moves first, and the story he tells is the one the world remembers."

"Then let him publish. Let him print his gaps. We answer with the truth."

"Answering takes longer. Answering requires the very vetting you're saying we can't rush. And while we're being careful, his version becomes the default. That's how this works. I've seen it. In Millbrook, the first story out the door was the one people cited for twenty years. The correction never caught up."

Wren's fingers tightened on the table edge. "This isn't Millbrook. This is Amity Falls. The bargain holds because the people in it trust each other. You break that trust to beat a deadline, and you've already given Drake what he wants."

"I'm not breaking trust. I'm managing risk."

"You're managing me. You've decided the timeline, the structure, the voice. You've decided what gets vetted and what gets cut. You've decided the valley's history is a project to be completed."

Theo set the pen down. The scratch of graphite stopped. "I've decided the valley's history needs to be published before someone else publishes a lie. I've decided we need a plan to make that happen. I've decided you're the only person who can write the parts I can't, and I've decided I'm not going to pretend that's not true."

Wren stood. The chair scraped the floor. "And the parts you can't write?"

"The lineage costs. The debt mechanics. The ring ritual from inside. The way the bargain feels when it takes." He spoke to the table. "I can describe the documents. I can't describe the weight."

She didn't answer. She walked to the window, looking out at the valley floor where the orchard rows caught the morning light. The apple trees stood in their winter geometry, bare branches mapping lines she had walked a thousand times.

"My mother will ask why you're the one holding the pen," she said. "She'll ask why an outsider gets to frame the story."

"Then you tell her I'm not framing it. I'm building the container. You're filling it."

"That's not how it works. The container shapes what fits inside."

"Then we build a better container."

Wren turned. Her face was calm, but her eyes held the flicker she got when she was close to saying something she hadn't planned. "You want to know what I'm afraid of? Not that you'll get it wrong. That you'll get it right in the wrong way. That you'll write something so clean and clear and complete that people read it and think they understand. They'll think the bargain is a history book. They'll think the cost is a footnote. They'll think the valley is a case study."

Theo rose. He came around the table, stopping a few feet from her. "And if I write it messy? If I leave the gaps? If I honor the confusion?"

"Then Drake wins. Because messy looks like hiding."

"Then we find the middle. Clean enough to stand. Honest enough to hold." He held her gaze. "Wren, I have spent nineteen years watching the story of what happened to my grandmother get told by people who weren't there. The county report. The newspaper archives. The academic papers that cited the county report. Every version was clean. Every version was wrong. The only thing that ever mattered was what she actually lived. I am not going to do that to you. I am not going to do that to this place."

She studied him. The morning light caught the line of his jaw, the crease between his brows. "You're asking me to trust your instinct over mine."

"I'm asking you to trust that my instinct is to protect the truth the same way yours is. The methods are different. The goal isn't."

"My goal is that the people in this valley still speak to each other when it's over. That the lineages still sit at the same table. That the bargain holds because the trust holds."

"Mine too." He said it quietly. "But the bargain doesn't hold if the truth stays buried. That's what Whitlock's letter said. That's what Adelaide's letter said. That's what the ring ritual proved. The secret is what breaks it. The publication is what saves it."

Wren looked back at the table. The timeline lay there, pencil marks mapping a future she hadn't agreed to. "The council meeting is in three days."

"Yusuf said he'd back the press funding."

"Yusuf backs what the lineages agree to. And the lineages agree to nothing until they've vetted every word."

"Then we start vetting today."

"We start vetting when the council approves the press. And the council approves the press when every lineage signs off on the record. That's what Yusuf told me yesterday. He didn't say it in the meeting. He said it after, in the hallway. 'Every lineage vets the record. Then we talk about funding.'"

Theo was quiet. The pencil marks blurred in the shaft of light. "That changes the timeline."

"It doesn't change the timeline. It *is* the timeline."

He picked up the pen again. Erased the four-week vetting block. Drew a question mark in its place. "Then the timeline depends on them. Not us."

"It depends on us getting them to yes."

"And if they say no?"

"Then we don't publish. And Drake does."

The silence stretched. Outside, a truck rumbled past on the county road, the sound muffled by distance and trees.

"You're willing to let Drake publish," Theo said. "If the alternative is forcing the lineages."

"I'm willing to lose the race if winning it burns the village."

"That's not a strategy. That's a surrender."

"It's a boundary."

He laughed, short and sharp. "A boundary you can't enforce. Drake doesn't care about your boundaries. He cares about leverage. He has the partial record. He has the printer. He has the timeline. The only thing he doesn't have is the complete truth. And the only people who can give it to him are the ones you're trying to protect from the process."

Wren's voice dropped. "The process *is* the protection. The vetting *is* the protection. You want to skip it because you're afraid. I want to honor it because I've seen what happens when you don't."

"What happens?"

"People stop being people. They become sources. They become quotes. They become the story someone else tells about them."

Theo stepped closer. "I am not someone else. I am standing in your kitchen. I have been standing in your kitchen for six months. I know the names of the trees in your orchard. I know which floorboard creaks. I know how you take your tea. I know what your grandmother's handwriting looks like. I know what your mother's voice sounds like when she's worried. I am not an extractor. I am a witness. There is a difference."

"Is there? When the book goes to print, when the reporters call, when the academics cite it - are you still a witness? Or are you the author?"

"I'm both. And you're the one who decides which one shows up in the room."

Wren turned away. Her hand found the windowsill. The wood was cold. "I need to talk to my mother. Before the council meeting. Before any vetting starts. She needs to hear it from me, not from Yusuf, not from a council notice."

"Okay."

"And I need to talk to Priya. And Dev. And the Warden elder. And the Mender circle. And the Weather council. All of them. Separately. Before they sit in a room together."

"Okay."

"And you will not write a single word of the lineage sections until I tell you they're ready."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. The pen rested on the table. "I can work on the county records. The administrative history. The Millbrook connection. The Drake timeline. The structural pieces that don't require lineage testimony."

"That's not the heart of it."

"No. But it's the skeleton. And the skeleton has to hold before the heart has a place to beat."

Wren faced him again. Her expression had shifted, the hard edge softening into something closer to exhaustion. "You really believe that. That the structure protects the truth."

"I believe the truth doesn't survive without structure. I've seen the alternative. Nineteen years of it."

She nodded once. "Then build the skeleton. I'll bring the heart."

"Together?"

"Together." The word came out quieter than she intended. "But if you move faster than the lineages, if you push the timeline past the trust - I will stop you. I don't care what the schedule says. I will stop you."

Theo picked up the pen. He drew a line through the question mark. Wrote: *Lineage vetting - pace set by lineage leads. No exceptions.*

"Written," he said.

Wren let out a breath she seemed to have been holding. She picked up her mug from the counter. The tea had gone cold. She drank it anyway.

"I'll talk to my mother today," she said. "Priya tomorrow. Dev the day after. The Warden elder market day. The Menders at their circle. The Weathers..." She paused. "The Weathers I'll do last. They're the hardest."

"Why?"

"Because they don't believe the bargain should be written down at all. They think the wind remembers what the paper forgets."

"Then we give them a reason to believe the paper can hold the wind."

Wren almost smiled. "Good luck with that."

"I'll need it." Theo gathered the pages, aligning the corners. "Three days to the council. How many lineage leads can you reach before then?"

"Five of seven. The Weathers might take two weeks alone."

"Then we go to council with five signatures and a plan for the other two. Yusuf respects a plan."

"He respects a promise kept."

"Then we promise them the time they need. And we promise the council the record they voted for. And we deliver both."

Wren set the mug down. She looked at the timeline pages in his hands, the pencil marks mapping a path she hadn't chosen but might follow. "You're good at this. The planning. The structure. The way you make the impossible look like a calendar."

"I'm good at the calendar. The impossible is yours."

She laughed, a short surprised sound. "That's not reassuring."

"It's accurate."

"Since when do you care about accurate?"

"Since the alternative got my grandmother's life wrong for nineteen years." He tucked the pages into his bag. "I'll be at the archives. The county records need cross-referencing with the valley census. There's a gap in the 1942 renewal that doesn't match the ledger Dev found. I want to close it before the vetting starts."

Wren nodded. "I'll walk with you partway. My mother will be at the store by nine. Odette will have the back room open."

They walked out together into the morning cold. The valley spread below them, the river a silver thread through the orchard rows, the mountains holding the horizon in their familiar curve. Smoke rose from three chimneys. A dog barked somewhere near the general store.

Theo walked a half-step behind her, matching his stride to hers. The timeline pages rested in his bag, the pencil marks waiting for the names that would fill them. The vetting would be slow. The lineages would argue. The Weathers might never sign. Drake's printer in Millbrook was already warm.

But the path existed. Drawn in pencil. Changeable. Real.

Wren stopped at the fork where the path split toward the archives and the store. She turned. "Three days."

"Three days."

"Don't write the heart."

"I won't."

"Good."

She started toward the store. Theo watched her go, the wool socks silent on the gravel, the morning light catching the dark hair that had escaped her braid. He waited until she reached the porch. Until Odette's door opened. Until the warmth of the store spilled out around her.

Then he turned toward the archives, the bag heavy on his shoulder, the timeline burning in his mind like a promise he had made to someone who didn't make promises lightly.
