The council chamber smelled of lemon oil and old paper, the same scent that had greeted Wren every Tuesday since she was tall enough to see over the table's edge. Twelve chairs in a semicircle. Twelve chairs, though only eleven were filled tonight. Denise's spot at the far end remained empty, the wood still bearing the faint impression of where her cane had rested for fifty years.

Wren stood at the presentation board, her pointer resting against a map of the valley she'd drawn herself. Topographical lines in pencil. The creek's switchback in blue ink. The orchard's boundary in green. The general store, the substation, the archive building rising slow and steady on the old Miller lot.

"Thank you all for coming on short notice," she said. Her voice carried without strain — one of the small gifts that came with being a Castellano, the way sound bent toward a Finder when they needed it to. "I know harvest's eating everyone's evenings."

Yusuf shifted in the center chair, his shoulders broader in the new suit jacket Odette had insisted he buy. "Harvest waits. This doesn't." He glanced around the table: Odette herself, arms folded; Priya with her notebook open; Walt Pruitt leaning forward in his work coat, the only man in the room who'd never held a gift or a title. "Wren. The floor's yours."

She pulled the first page from her stack. A timeline, dense with dates.

"Corwin Drake has been quiet for twelve months. That doesn't mean he's gone. It means he's been building." She tapped the paper. "Nineteen years since his first attempt in Millbrook. He failed there because he didn't have the full picture. Since then, he's been assembling one. Property records. Census gaps. Newspaper archives from three counties. Court filings from a property dispute in 1923 that references 'the arrangement' without naming it."

Priya's pen stopped moving. "He has documentation."

"He has fragments. Enough to be dangerous." Wren set down the pointer. "He's not a ritualist. He doesn't want to break the bargain. He wants to own the terms of it. If he publishes what he has — a partial history, stripped of context, framed as conspiracy — the valley becomes a story the outside world can't ignore. Reporters. Researchers. People with questions and cameras and no concept of what it means to live here."

"Then we stop him," Odette said. Her voice was flat, practical. "We've stopped worse."

"This isn't something we stop with a ward or a working," Wren said. "He's not using the bargain. He's using the truth — or enough of it to look like the whole thing. The only way to meet that is with more truth. All of it. Organized. Sourced. Complete."

She turned the page. The timeline gave way to a table of contents.

"I'm proposing the Archive Project. A full public record of the valley's history, the bargain's terms, the lineages, the debts paid and owed. Every renewal. Every failure. Every name. Published on our terms, in our words, before Drake can weaponize his version."

Silence stretched. Walt Pruitt cleared his throat.

"Everything," he said. "You're talking about everything. The memory trades. The toll. The reason this valley doesn't show right on GPS."

"Yes."

"And the people who paid." Walt's eyes found Priya, then Odette, then the empty chair. "Their names. What they lost."

"Redacted where living family objects," Wren said. "But yes. The record has to be honest, or it's just another weapon."

Yusuf didn't hesitate. "You have the council's backing. Full access to municipal records. The archive building's yours once the roof's watertight — Odette tells me two weeks, maybe three."

Odette snorted. "Three if the weather holds. Two if it doesn't."

"Then we start in two," Yusuf said. "Wren, you lead. Priya, you're liaison for anything that touches active investigations. Odette, Walt — whatever she needs from the store or the town, she gets."

Wren exhaled, something tight in her chest loosening for the first time in weeks. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Yusuf said. "You're talking about putting the valley's throat in the world's hands. Drake's not the only one who'll read it. You're sure this is the right call?"

She met his gaze steadily. "The alternative is letting someone else tell our story. We've tried that. It cost us two years of Caleb's life. It cost Denise fifty. I'm not doing it again."

The meeting broke twenty minutes later. Walt lingered, shaking his head as he collected his coat.

"Girl's got steel," he muttered to Priya on his way out. "More than most. Reminds me of her grandmother, God rest her."

Priya waited until the door clicked shut before turning to Wren. "You held the room."

"I had the facts." Wren gathered her pages, hands steady. "That's the job."

"It's not just facts. It's the way you laid them out. No apology. No hedging." Priya capped her pen. "You're doing the thing your line does. Finding what's lost and dragging it into light. Even when the light burns."

Wren's fingers paused on the timeline's edge. The Castellano line. Finders, every generation back to the valley's founding. The gift to locate what was missing — objects, people, truths — and the cost: a memory of the search itself. Her grandmother had paid with the memory of finding her own husband's body in the creek. Her mother had paid with the memory of the night Wren's father left.

Wren had paid smaller debts so far. The memory of her first solo finding at fourteen — a lost wedding ring in the orchard mud, the satisfaction of handing it back. The memory of locating Priya's missing case file three years ago, the quiet pride of competence.

She hadn't yet paid for the big ones. The ones that mattered.

"Priya," she said quietly. "If Drake publishes first — if he gets to the state archives, the county clerk, the historical society before we do — "

"He won't." Priya's voice carried the Caller's calm, the low hum that settled nerves within five feet. "We're moving faster. Yusuf's already called the county recorder. The Millbrook courthouse. They're expecting our requests."

"And if he has copies already? Digital? He's had nineteen years."

"Then we publish louder. Cleaner. With the weight of the valley behind us." Priya's composure flickered, just for a second — the cost of the calm she'd just spent. "Wren. You're not doing this alone. That's the point of the project."

Wren nodded, but the weight didn't lift. It never did, not really. The Castellano expectation: protect the valley's secrets. Guard the bargain's mechanics. Keep the outside world at bay. Her grandmother had drilled it into her before she could read — *we find so others don't have to look*. The gift was a shield. The debt was the price of holding it.

Now she was being asked to lower the shield. To hand the world a map.

She walked Priya to the door, locked up the chamber, and made her way down Main Street toward the archive building. The October air carried woodsmoke and crushed apple, the orchard's late windfalls fermenting in the grass. Light spilled from Odette's store windows — the general store never truly closed, not really. Odette claimed she'd sleep on the counter if the town needed Band-Aids at midnight.

The archive sat three lots down, its new roof dark against the stars. Scaffolding still clung to the east face. Inside, the main room was a cathedral of bare studs and subfloor, smelling of cut lumber and drywall dust. Boxes lined the walls: municipal records, family donations, the valley's accumulated paper soul.

Wren set her stack on the folding table she'd claimed as a desk. She'd need a real one. A chair that didn't wobble. Shelving. A catalog system. Theo Marsh would need workspace too, when he arrived.

*When he arrived.*

The thought settled in her stomach, cold and quiet.

She'd requested him. Written the letter herself, on valley letterhead, addressed to the Millbrook County Records Office. *We require a records investigator with archival training. No gift necessary. Discretion essential.* She'd expected a refusal, or a referral, or silence.

She'd gotten a one-page reply, typed on county stationery, signed in ink that had bled slightly into the paper fibers.

*I accept. Two weeks' notice. My credentials are enclosed. — T. Marsh*

No title. No honorific. Just initials and a surname.

Wren pulled out the envelope she'd kept tucked in her bag. The credentials were photocopied, certified: Theo Marsh, age twenty-six. BA History, Millbrook State. Three years at the county records office, two at the state archives' special collections division. A published paper on "Administrative Gaps in Rural Pennsylvania Land Records, 1890–1920." A letter of recommendation from the state archivist calling him meticulous to the point of obsession, and the only researcher she trusted with fragile originals.

No mention of gifts. No mention of Kindred status.

He was ordinary. Completely, deliberately ordinary.

And he was coming tomorrow.

Wren sank onto her wobbly chair. The weight in her chest shifted, reshaping itself.

*Protect the secrets. Guard the bargain. Keep the world out.*

Her grandmother's voice, her mother's voice, the voice of every Castellano who'd held the line.

*We find so others don't have to look.*

But the looking had already happened. Drake had looked. He'd found fragments. He was assembling them into a weapon.

The only way to disarm it was to build something better. Truer. Complete.

And she couldn't build it alone.

---

The knock came at 8:17 the next morning.

Wren had been in the archive since six, running through the municipal ledgers with a magnifying lamp and a migraine building behind her eyes. The ledgers were a nightmare: three different hands, two numbering systems, and a gap from 1918 to 1922 that smelled like fire damage but had no scorch marks on the pages.

She opened the door.

The man on the threshold was taller than she'd imagined. Late twenties, maybe thirty. Dark hair cut short, practical. A wool coat worn soft at the cuffs. Hands in his pockets, posture relaxed but alert — the stillness of someone who'd learned to wait in rooms where waiting was the job.

He didn't smile. Didn't offer a hand.

"Theo Marsh," he said. "County records. You requested an investigator."

Wren stepped back, gesturing him in. "Wren Castellano. Valley documentation project."

He entered slow, eyes tracking the room: the boxes, the exposed studs, the single bare bulb hanging from a temporary wire. His gaze lingered on the magnifying lamp, the ledger stacks, the notebook where she'd been transcribing the 1917 entries.

"Rough conditions," he said.

"Temporary. Roof's finished Monday. Shelving next week." She closed the door behind him. "Coffee's instant. Water's from the tap. There's a portable toilet behind the building until the septic's connected."

"I've worked worse." He withdrew a folder from his coat — a plain manila thing, dog-eared at the corners. "Credentials. Background check. Non-disclosure agreement the county requires for sensitive municipal contracts."

He set it on the table beside her ledger. No flourish. No sales pitch.

"I also brought my own," he added, pulling a second folder from his bag. "Personal project. Millbrook area land records, 1880 to 1930. There's a gap in the index I've been chasing for six years. Thought I'd use downtime."

Wren picked up the official folder. The NDA was standard boilerplate: confidentiality, non-disclosure, penalties for breach. She skimmed it, then set it aside.

"The valley's not a municipal contract," she said. "We're not incorporated. We don't have a charter."

"The county treats you as a special district for record-keeping purposes. Has since 1873." He didn't look at her. "I checked. The paperwork's thin, but it exists. Someone maintained it. Quietly."

Her fingers stilled on the folder's edge. "Who?"

"Doesn't say. Just a notation: 'Per standing arrangement, Amity Falls records filed separately. Access restricted.' That's the gap I've been chasing. The arrangement. No one at the county knows what it means. The files are there, but the index just says *restricted* — no classification code, no retention schedule, no authorized personnel listed."

Wren's breath caught, just slightly. "You found that six years ago."

"Found the notation. Couldn't find the arrangement." His voice was flat, matter-of-fact. "Wrote my paper on it. The archivist hated it — said I was inventing conspiracy where bureaucracy sufficed. But the gap's real. Fourteen linear feet of records, zero metadata. Someone decided they didn't want those files found."

He pulled a chair from the wall, sat without invitation. "So when your letter arrived — *valley documentation project, discretion essential, no gift necessary* — I knew. Or I guessed. The gap has a name. Amity Falls. And you're the one finally asking for help cataloguing it."

Wren stood very still. The magnifying lamp hummed. Somewhere outside, a truck downshifted on Main Street.

"You're not Kindred," she said.

"No."

"You don't have a gift."

"Correct."

"And you want access to everything. The bargain records. The renewal logs. The debt ledgers. The names."

"I want access to the truth. All of it. Organized. Sourced. Complete." He echoed her own words from the council meeting. "That's what you told the council. That's what your letter asked for. Someone to help build a record that can stand up to scrutiny — before someone else builds a worse one."

The precision of it stung. He'd read her letter. Memorized it. Maybe investigated her before he agreed.

"Drake," she said.

"Corwin Drake. Information broker. Nineteen years since Millbrook. Partial record, weaponized." He ticked them off on his fingers. "You're racing him. You need someone who knows how to build an archive that holds up in court, in the press, in the court of public opinion. Someone who doesn't flinch at gaps. Someone who's spent six years staring at a hole in the record and refusing to let it stay a hole."

He leaned forward, elbows on knees.

"I'm that person. And I don't need a gift to do it. I just need the files."

Wren studied him. The steady gaze. The hands, still folded between his knees. The coat worn soft at the cuffs. The way he'd said *I've worked worse* — not complaining, not boasting. Just fact.

He was exactly what she'd asked for. Exactly what the project needed.

And he terrified her.

Because he was ordinary. Because he had no stake in the valley's survival, no blood tie to the bargain, no gift that would make him understand the cost of what she was proposing. He wanted the truth because the truth was a puzzle he'd been solving for six years.

He would publish it. All of it. Without hesitation. Without the Castellano instinct to shield, to soften, to protect.

*We find so others don't have to look.*

But he *had* looked. He'd found the gap. And he hadn't stopped looking.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Roof's done. Shelving starts. I'll have the municipal ledgers ready — the ones from 1917 forward. The gap years are in a separate box. Fire damage. Water damage. Some kind of damage." She swallowed. "They're not organized. They're not indexed. They're just... pages."

"Pages are my favorite thing," Theo said. "Pages I can read."

He stood, sliding the official folder toward her. "Sign the NDA. I'll sign yours, if you have one. Then we start."

Wren picked up the pen. Her hand didn't shake.

She signed *Wren Castellano* on the line for *Project Lead, Amity Falls Documentation Project*.

Theo signed *Theodore Marsh* on the line for *Contractor, Records Investigation*.

When he left at six — "Same time tomorrow. Bring the fire-damaged box" — the archive felt larger. Emptier. The single bulb threw longer shadows.

Wren stayed at the table, the signed NDA in front of her, the ledger open to 1917.

*Protect the secrets. Guard the bargain. Keep the world out.*

Her grandmother's voice. Her mother's voice.

But Theo Marsh had already looked. He'd already found the gap. He was already inside the walls.

And she'd handed him the key.

The weight in her chest didn't lift. It settled, heavy and cold, the shape of a debt she hadn't yet paid — the memory of the choice she'd just made, the moment she'd decided the valley's survival mattered more than the Castellano way.

She closed the ledger. Turned off the lamp. Walked out into the October dark, the archive door locking behind her with a sound like finality.

Above, the stars wheeled in their ancient patterns. The valley sat beneath them, a few hundred yards off the map, holding its breath.

Tomorrow, they'd start digging.

Tomorrow, she'd find out if the truth could be a shield, too.
