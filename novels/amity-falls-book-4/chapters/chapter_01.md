The council chamber smelled of lemon oil and old paper, the same smell that had met Wren every Tuesday since she was tall enough to see over the table. Twelve chairs curved around it in a half circle, and tonight eleven were taken. Denise's chair at the far end sat empty.

Wren stood at the presentation board with a wooden pointer in her hand. The map pinned behind her was her own work: the creek's switchback in blue ink, the orchard boundary in green, the store, the substation, and the archive building going up on the old Miller lot. She had redrawn it four times that week rather than think about what she was about to ask.

"Thank you for coming on short notice," she said. "I know harvest is eating everyone's evenings."

Yusuf shifted in the center chair, the new jacket Odette had made him buy pulling across his shoulders. "Harvest waits. This doesn't. Wren, the floor's yours."

She took the first page off her stack. A timeline, dense with dates.

"Corwin Drake has been quiet for twelve months. I don't think that means he's gone." She tapped the paper. "It's been nineteen years since Millbrook, his first attempt, and he failed because he only had part of the picture. He has spent the years collecting the rest. Property records, census gaps, newspaper archives from three counties, and a court filing from a 1923 land dispute that mentions 'the arrangement' without ever saying what it is."

Priya's pen stopped. "He has documentation."

"He has fragments, which is enough to be dangerous. He doesn't want to break the bargain. He wants to own its terms. If he publishes what he has, a partial history with the context stripped out and a conspiracy stapled on, the valley becomes a story the outside world can't ignore. Reporters. Researchers. People with cameras and questions and no idea what living here costs."

"Then we stop him," Odette said, arms folded. "We've stopped worse."

"Not with a ward or a working. He's using the truth, or enough of it to pass for the whole. The only answer to that is more truth, organized and sourced and complete." Wren turned the page to a table of contents. "I'm proposing the Archive Project. A full public record of the valley's history: the bargain's terms, the lineages, every renewal and every failure, the debts paid and the debts still owed. Published in our words, on our schedule, before Drake publishes his."

Nobody spoke. Walt Pruitt cleared his throat.

"Everything," he said. "The memory trades. The toll. The reason this valley never shows up right on a phone."

"Yes."

"And the people who paid." He looked at Priya, then at Odette, then at the empty chair. "Their names. What they lost."

"Redacted wherever living family objects. But yes. If the record isn't honest, it's just another weapon."

Yusuf didn't wait. "You have the council's backing and full access to the municipal records. The archive building is yours once the roof is watertight. Odette?"

"Two weeks," Odette said. "Three if the weather turns."

"Then we make it official in two. Wren leads. Priya, you're liaison for anything that touches an active investigation. Odette, Walt, whatever she needs from the store or the town, she gets it."

Wren let out a breath she had been holding since Sunday. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Yusuf said it gently. "You're proposing to put the valley's whole history in strangers' hands. Are you sure?"

"The alternative is letting someone else tell our story. We tried that. It cost Caleb two years of his memory and it cost Denise fifty years of keeping the secret. I won't do it again."

The meeting broke up twenty minutes later. Walt lingered over his coat.

"Talks just like her grandmother," he told Priya on his way out. "Never once asked whether we were comfortable."

Priya waited until the door shut. "You held the room."

"I had the facts."

"You had the facts and you didn't apologize for them. Most people apologize." She capped her pen. "What's bothering you?"

Wren squared her pages against the table. "If Drake gets to the state archives first. Or the county clerk, or the historical society."

"He won't." Priya's voice carried the low hum of a Caller, the calm that reached anyone within five feet. "Yusuf already called the county recorder and the Millbrook courthouse. They're expecting our requests."

"And if he has copies? Digital ones? He's had nineteen years."

"Then we publish louder and cleaner, with the whole valley behind us." The calm flickered for half a second, the bill for what she had just spent. "You aren't doing this alone, Wren. That's the point of a project."

Wren nodded. It helped less than it should have.

The Castellanos found things. That was the family trade, every generation back to the founding: lost rings, lost people, lost truths, and each finding cost the finder a memory of the search. Her grandmother had paid with the memory of finding her own husband in the creek. Her mother had paid with the night Wren's father left. Wren knew about her own small payments only from the gaps. A wedding ring in the orchard mud when she was fourteen, which its owner still thanked her for every Christmas. A missing case file she had located for Priya three years ago, remembered now only through the thank-you note in her desk drawer. She had never yet paid for anything large.

Her grandmother had drilled the family rule into her before she could read. *We find so others don't have to look.* The gift was a shield, and tonight Wren had asked the council to lower it and hand the world a map.

She locked the chamber and walked down Main Street toward the archive. The October air smelled of woodsmoke and windfall apples fermenting in the orchard grass. Light spilled from the windows of Odette's store, which never really closed. Odette claimed she would sleep on the counter if the town ever needed Band-Aids at midnight.

The archive stood three lots down, its roof still under blue tarps that snapped in the wind, scaffolding clinging to the east wall. Inside were bare studs and subfloor, the smell of cut lumber and drywall dust, and boxes stacked along every wall: municipal records, family donations, the valley's paper memory. One carton still wore the label *Pruitt Hardware, invoices, 1931* in Walt's father's handwriting, and another held nothing but church bulletins tied up with kitchen twine.

She set her pages on the folding table she had claimed as a desk. She would need a real one, and a chair that didn't wobble, and shelving, and a catalog system. She would need Theo Marsh, who was arriving in the morning.

She had written to the Millbrook County Records Office three weeks ago, before the council vote, because waiting for permission had already cost the valley enough. *We require a records investigator with archival training. No gift necessary. Discretion essential.* She had expected a refusal, or a referral, or silence. The answer had come on county stationery, one line in ink that bled a little into the paper: *I accept. Two weeks' notice. Credentials enclosed. T. Marsh.*

The credentials were still in her bag, photocopied and certified. Theo Marsh, twenty-six. A history degree from Millbrook State, three years at the county records office, two more in special collections at the state archives. A published paper on gaps in rural land records, 1890 to 1920. A recommendation from the state archivist calling him exacting to the point of obsession, and the only researcher she trusted with fragile originals.

Nothing about a gift. Nothing about Kindred blood. He was ordinary, entirely and on purpose, and he would be here in the morning.

Wren lowered herself onto the wobbling chair. Drake had looked, and he had found fragments, and he was fitting them together. The only defense was a record better than his, and she could not build it alone.

---

The knock came at 8:17 the next morning.

Wren had been in the archive since six with a magnifying lamp and a migraine gathering behind her left eye. The municipal ledgers were a mess: three different hands, two numbering systems, and a gap from 1918 to 1922 that looked like fire damage but left no scorch on the pages.

The man on the threshold was taller than she had pictured. Mid-twenties, though he held himself like someone older, hands in the pockets of a wool coat worn soft at the cuffs. Short dark hair. He had the stillness of a person who had spent years waiting in rooms where waiting was the job. He neither smiled nor offered a hand, and she found she didn't mind.

"Theo Marsh. County records. You wrote to us."

"Wren Castellano. Valley documentation project."

He came in slowly, reading the room: the boxes, the exposed studs, the single bulb on a temporary wire, the lamp and the ledger stacks and the notebook where she had been transcribing 1917. "Rough conditions."

"Temporary. The roof is tarped and shingles start in two weeks if it doesn't rain. Shelving next week. The coffee is instant, the water is from the tap, and there's a portable toilet behind the building until the septic is connected."

"I've worked in worse." He took a manila folder from his coat, its corners soft from handling. "Credentials, background check, and the county's confidentiality agreement for sensitive municipal work." From his bag came a second folder. "And a personal project, if there's downtime. Land records for the Millbrook area, 1880 to 1930. There's a hole in the index I've been chasing for six years."

Wren skimmed the agreement. Standard language. "The valley isn't a municipal contract. We're not incorporated. We don't have a charter."

"The county has treated you as a special district for record-keeping since 1873." He said it to the folder rather than to her. "The paperwork is thin, but it exists. Someone kept it up, quietly."

Her fingers stopped on the page. "Who?"

"It doesn't say. There's one notation: 'Per standing arrangement, Amity Falls records filed separately. Access restricted.' That's the hole. The files exist, fourteen linear feet of them, but the index gives no classification code, no retention schedule, no authorized staff. Nobody at the county knows what the arrangement is." He looked up at last. "I wrote a paper on it. The archivist said I was inventing a conspiracy where bureaucracy would do. Then your letter came with 'discretion essential' in it, and I thought, the hole has a name."

The lamp hummed. Outside, a truck downshifted on Main Street.

"You aren't Kindred," Wren said.

"No."

"No gift at all."

"None. I have patience and an eye for a bad index."

"You'll want to see everything. The renewal logs, the debt ledgers, the names."

"I'll read everything I'm given. What goes into the public version is a different argument, and I'd rather have it after I know what's there." He said it plainly, as if it were a shelf-mark. "Somebody else is building a record of this place, aren't they? Nobody asks for an archivist with discretion unless they're racing something."

She hadn't expected him to hear that in a line of stationery. "Someone is. We need to finish first."

"Then we should stop talking and start indexing."

Wren studied him. He was exactly what she had asked for. He was also a stranger with no blood in the bargain and no gift to teach him what its records had cost the people written in them. He would read every page, and he would want every page to be true. That was the job, and it frightened her a little anyway. *We find so others don't have to look.* But he had looked. He had found the hole without anyone's help, and he had not stopped.

"We start with the ledgers from 1917 forward," she said. "The gap years are in a separate box, and it's in bad shape. Fire, water, and a third kind of damage I haven't identified."

"Damaged pages are still pages."

He slid the county's agreement across the table. She signed *Wren Castellano* on the line for Project Lead. He signed *Theodore Marsh* under Contractor, Records Investigation, in a small square hand.

They worked through the day with very little talk. By noon her migraine had eased, and it took her a while to work out why. He had turned the lamp on its arm while she was in the back room, so the glare fell across the page instead of across her eyes, and he had not said a word about it. Odette sent her nephew over with two sandwiches in wax paper and a note that read *Feed the new one.* Theo ate his standing up, one hand still on a ledger, and asked what the town's fire records looked like for the winter of 1918. Wren told him there weren't any. He wrote that down as though it were an answer.

At six he put his coat back on and nodded at the door, and then he was gone. The archive felt larger without him, and quieter. The single bulb threw long shadows across the subfloor.

Wren stayed at the table with the signed agreement under her hand. She had been raised to keep the door shut, and this morning she had opened it to a man she had known for ten hours. She switched off the lamp.

On her way out she passed the fire-damaged box, which she had meant to save for tomorrow. He had already found it. The lid was off, the pages inside squared and interleaved with clean paper, and across the front ran a strip of masking tape lettered in small, neat pencil.

*1918 to 1922. Start here.*
