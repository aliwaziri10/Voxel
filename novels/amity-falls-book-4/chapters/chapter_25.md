The drive from Amity Falls to Millbrook took two hours if you hit the highway at the right time of day, but Theo had insisted on the county roads. He said the older maps showed property lines the GPS missed, and Wren had not argued. She spent the passenger-seat hours watching the tree line blur, her fingers curled around the strap of the bag that held the copies of Adelaide's letter and Whitlock's letter and the transcript of the ring ritual. The originals stayed in the valley, locked in the new archive cabinet that Yusuf had bolted to the floor of the council room. They had agreed on that before they left. Originals never traveled.

Theo drove with the same quiet precision he brought to the reading room. Hands at ten and two. Eyes scanning the shoulder for the turn he had marked on a printed MapQuest page taped to the dash. He had a file box on the back seat, the kind the county archives used for cold cases, and a second box in the trunk with the portable scanner and the backup drives. Wren had asked him once, three weeks ago, why he did not trust the cloud. He had looked at her across the kitchen table in the apartment above the general store and said, "Because the cloud has a subpoena address. A box in my trunk does not."

The storage facility sat two miles past the Millbrook city limit, behind a feed store and a shuttered diner. The sign read A-1 SECURE STORAGE in fading blue letters. The gate was a chain-link slide that rattled on its track when Theo pulled it back. No keypad. No camera dome. A padlock hung from the hasp, keyed alike to the one Theo had picked up from the county evidence locker that morning.

"Unit 47," he said, killing the engine. "Leased to a shell company called Meridian Consulting. Registered address is a UPS Store in Wilmington, Delaware. The signature on the lease is illegible, but the notary stamp belongs to a woman named Helen Grady. She notarized three other leases for Meridian in the last eighteen months. All of them within twenty miles of here."

Wren unbuckled. "And the folio?"

"Drake's partial record. The one he's been building for nineteen years. The one he threatened to publish if we didn't hand over the original terms." Theo opened his door. "The county index has a restricted gap flagged 'standing arrangement.' That notation appeared in the system nineteen years ago, the same week Drake's first attempt went sideways in Millbrook. The same week my grandmother lost her memory."

He said it flat. No emphasis. But Wren heard the thread pulling tight underneath. She had heard it in his voice before, late at night over the kitchen table when the scanner hummed and the coffee went cold. The way he said *my grandmother* like it was a case file he could not close.

"Harriet Marsh," she said. "She's still alive."

"She's seventy-five. She lives in a care facility on the north side of Millbrook. She remembers her childhood. She remembers her marriage. She remembers my father being born. She does not remember the decade before 2007. She does not remember where she was. She does not remember what happened to her." Theo stepped onto the gravel. "And the man who took it from her has been building a file on this valley for nineteen years while she sat in a room looking at photographs of a life she cannot name."

Wren followed him down the row of garage doors. The metal was dented in places, scratched by lawn mower blades and moving dollies. Weeds pushed up through the cracks in the asphalt. A wasp nest clung to the eave of Unit 43. The air smelled of dry grass and old gasoline.

"Here," Theo said.

Unit 47 looked like every other unit. Same corrugated steel. Same sliding door. Same padlock hasp. He knelt, pulled the pick set from his jacket pocket, and went to work. Wren stood behind him, her shadow long in the late afternoon sun. She did not offer to help. She had learned, in the weeks of cataloguing sessions and archive trips, that Theo's competence was not a performance. It was a habit. He did not need her to hold the light. He had a headlamp clipped to his collar.

The lock gave with a soft *snick*. He slid the hasp free, lifted the door, and the rollers groaned.

Inside: nothing. Concrete floor swept clean. Walls bare. No shelves. No boxes. No folio. No nineteen years of assembled ammunition. A single piece of printer paper lay in the center of the floor, held down by a rock the size of a walnut.

Theo picked it up. Wren moved closer, close enough to read over his shoulder.

*You're late. — C.D.*

She felt the breath leave her. Not surprise. Something colder. The recognition of a pattern she had seen before in the valley, in the letters Adelaide left, in the warnings Whitlock buried. The antagonist always moves first.

Theo crumpled the paper in his fist. His knuckles went white. The headlamp threw a sharp beam across the empty concrete, and for a moment the dust motes caught the light like something sacred.

"He knew," Theo said. "He knew we were coming. He knew we traced the shell company. He knew because he has someone inside the county system, or he has someone inside the valley, or he has both." His voice did not rise. It dropped. "Nineteen years. Nineteen years he's been waiting for this. Building this. And he walked away from it like it was nothing. Like it was a bluff he was happy to burn."

"Drake doesn't bluff," Wren said. "He presses. If he burned the folio, it's because the folio stopped being useful. Or because he has something better."

Theo turned to face her. The headlamp cut across his features, the line of his jaw, the tension at the corner of his mouth. "Something better than nineteen years of damning evidence? What's better than that?"

"The truth," she said. "The complete truth. The one we're trying to publish. If he has the original terms, if he found them somewhere we missed, he doesn't need the partial record anymore. He can just release the real thing and let the valley burn."

Theo stared at her. The silence stretched, filled only by the wind in the weeds and the distant hum of a tractor on the highway. Then he let out a breath that sounded like something breaking.

"My grandmother," he said. "She asked me once, when I was a kid, why I liked old records so much. I told her I liked the way they made sense of things. She laughed and said, 'Records don't make sense of things, Theodore. They just keep the mess where you can find it.'"

He unclenched his fist. The crumpled paper fell to the concrete.

"Nineteen years I've been looking for the mess," he said. "Nineteen years I've been cataloguing every gap in the county index, every restricted notation, every sealed file. I thought if I found the right gap, I'd find her. I'd find what happened to her. I'd find who did it." He looked at the empty unit. "And the whole time, the man who did it was building his own archive. A better one. A more complete one. And he just walked away from it because he doesn't need it anymore."

"Drake didn't take her memory for pressure," Wren said carefully. "He took it because he was trying to do what he's trying to do now. Access the bargain. Control it. The Millbrook attempt was the first try. It failed. He lost ten years of your grandmother's life and got nothing for it. So he spent nineteen years building a second attempt."

"And the folio was just the threat," Theo said. "The gun on the table. He never intended to fire it. He intended to use it to make us hand over the real terms. And now he has them, or he thinks he does, so he doesn't need the gun."

"Or he never had the folio at all," Wren said. "Maybe the folio was always a story he told. A bluff to make us move. To make us reveal what we know."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. Then he pulled his phone from his pocket, tapped the screen, held it to his ear. Wren heard the ring tone, tinny and small in the open unit. A voice answered on the third ring. Theo said, "Yusuf. It's Theo. The storage unit is empty. Cleared out. Freshly swept. There's a note." He listened. His jaw tightened. "Yeah. Understood. We're heading back. Tonight."

He ended the call. Put the phone away.

"Yusuf wants us back at the archive," he said. "He says Priya got a call from the state police. Drake's lawyer reached out to them twenty minutes ago."

Wren blinked. "His lawyer?"

"Offering to talk. Said his client had information about a pending publication that might constitute extortion, witness tampering, and theft of government records - if Wren's side kept moving forward." Theo's voice had gone strange. Hollow. "He's doing this because he knows we're about to publish. He knows the complete record is going live in forty-eight hours. He wants to control the narrative. He wants a deal before we ever get to a courtroom."

"A deal for what?"

"For the original terms. The ones he claims to have. The ones he says he'll hand over in exchange for immunity if the county ever comes after him." Theo started walking back toward the car. Wren followed. "The state police are treating it as an informal approach, nothing filed yet. The DA's office is listening, not negotiating. They don't know what the valley is. They don't know what the bargain is. They see a guy with a lawyer and a stack of documents claiming historical fraud and conspiracy. They see a chance to reopen a cold case from nineteen years ago."

The cold case. Harriet Marsh. The restricted gap in the county index.

"Drake's offering them the Millbrook file," Wren said. "He's offering the evidence from his first attempt. He's trading the past for leverage on the future."

"He's trying to trade my grandmother's life for his freedom," Theo said. He reached the car, yanked the door open, threw himself into the driver's seat. Wren got in on the passenger side. The door slammed. The engine turned over.

"He's doing this," she said, "because he's afraid of what we're about to publish. He wants to be the one holding the cards when the story breaks."

"He's doing this," Theo said, pulling the car around, back toward the gate, "because he knows exactly what we have. He knows exactly what we're going to release. And he knows that once it's public, his advantage evaporates. So he's trying to get ahead of it. He's trying to make himself the source. The cooperating witness he hasn't actually become yet. The man who brought the truth to light."

The gate rattled shut behind them. The feed store loomed, then the diner, then the highway on-ramp. Theo merged into traffic without looking at her.

"My grandmother doesn't know," he said. "She doesn't know his name. She doesn't know what he did. She looks at photos of herself from 1998 to 2007 and sees a stranger. She asks me sometimes, 'Theodore, who is that woman? She looks like me.' And I have to tell her, 'That's you, Gram. That's you at my father's wedding. That's you at my graduation. That's you holding me when I was a baby.' And she smiles and says, 'Well, she seems nice. I hope she was happy.'"

Wren's hand found his forearm on the gear shift. She did not think about it. She just did it. Her fingers pressed into the sleeve of his jacket.

"She was happy," she said. "Before. The photos show it. The records show it. She had a life. A real one. And he took it from her because he wanted something he couldn't have."

Theo's hands tightened on the wheel. The tendons in his forearms stood faint against the skin. "I used to think," he said, "that if I just found the right file, the right gap, the right notation, I could give her back what was stolen. I could hand her the memory like a returned library book. Overdue, fine paid, back on the shelf." A harsh sound escaped him, not quite a laugh. "But memories don't work like that. The bargain doesn't work like that. The debt is paid in memory, and the memory is gone. There's no receipt. There's no return policy. There's only the gap."

The highway stretched ahead, two lanes each way, the median striped with reflectors that caught the headlights and threw them back. Trucks roared past in the left lane. Theo stayed right, steady at sixty-five.

"Drake knows that," Theo continued. "He knows the memory is gone. He knows he can't give it back. He knows the only thing he can trade is information. The truth about what he did. The truth about what the valley is. He's trying to sell the truth because it's the only currency he has left."

"And we're giving the truth away for free," Wren said. "We're publishing it. All of it. The complete record. Adelaide's letter. Whitlock's letter. The ring ritual transcript. The original terms. Everything. No redactions. No deals."

"Yeah," Theo said. "We are."

"Then he loses."

"He loses the advantage. He doesn't lose the game." Theo's voice dropped again, quieter now, edged with something she had not heard before. "Because the game was never about the valley. The game was about control. He's been playing it for nineteen years. He played it on my grandmother. He played it on the county. He played it on every person who ever signed a restricted file or looked away from a gap in the index. And once the record's out, he'll keep playing it from wherever this ends up - a courtroom, a settlement, a quiet withdrawal. He'll write letters. He'll file motions. He'll talk to journalists. He'll make himself the expert on the valley that doesn't exist on maps. He'll be the man who knew. The man who tried. The man who almost succeeded."

The miles ate themselves. The sun lowered behind the western ridge, painting the sky in bruised orange and purple. Wren watched the light move across Theo's face, the set of his jaw, the way his throat moved when he swallowed.

"He took ten years from her," Theo said. "Ten years of her life. Ten years of my father's life. Ten years of my life, before I even knew there was a game. And he did it for nothing. He got nothing. The Millbrook attempt failed. The bargain held. The valley survived. And he walked away with a hole in his file where a victory should have been. So he spent nineteen years filling that hole with other people's secrets. With the valley's history. With the bargain's terms. He built a weapon out of what he stole, and now he's trying to sell it back to us."

"He's not selling it," Wren said. "He's trying to control the release. But we're releasing it ourselves. On our terms. On our timeline. With our names on it."

"Our names," Theo repeated. "Your name. My name. The valley's name. The names of every person who ever paid a debt they didn't choose." He glanced at her, brief and sharp. "My grandmother's name."

"Her name too."

"She won't read it," he said. "She can't. She doesn't remember how to read the long words anymore. The home reads to her sometimes, but mostly she just looks at the pictures. She likes the pictures of the garden. She says the flowers look real."

Wren's throat tightened. "We'll read it to her. When this is done. When it's published. We'll drive up to Millbrook and sit in her room and read her the whole thing. Every page. So she knows. So the record says her name."

Theo was silent for a long time. The truck in the left lane blasted its horn at a slow merge. The reflectors flashed yellow, yellow, yellow.

"She told me once," Theo said, "that the worst thing about forgetting isn't the forgetting. It's the knowing that you forgot. The feeling of a shape in the dark. You can't see it, but you know it's there. You reach for it and your hand closes on air." He exhaled, slow and ragged. "For nineteen years, I've been reaching for that shape. I've been building a career out of reaching. Every file I catalogued, every gap I flagged, every restricted notation I chased - it was all the same reach. The same hand closing on air."

The car slowed for a curve. The guardrail reflected the headlights in a long white line.

"And now," Theo said, "the shape has a name. Corwin Drake. The shape has a face. The shape has a lawyer making calls and a story he's trying to sell before anyone can charge him with anything. The shape is real. And it doesn't matter. Because the thing he stole is still gone. The memory is still gone. The decade is still missing. Whatever happens to him doesn't give it back. Publishing the truth doesn't give it back. Nothing gives it back."

"No," Wren said. "Nothing gives it back. But the truth stops him from taking anything else. The truth protects the next person. The truth says: this happened. This is real. This is the cost. And the cost has a name."

Theo's hands loosened on the wheel. His shoulders dropped, just a fraction. "The cost has a name," he repeated. "Harriet Marsh. Ten years. Nineteen years ago. Millbrook."

"Harriet Marsh," Wren said. "Ten years. Nineteen years ago. Millbrook."

She said it like a catalog entry. Like a record filed. Like a debt acknowledged.

Theo let out a long breath. "When we get back to the valley," he said, "I'm going to call the care home. I'm going to ask if she's having a good day. If she is, I'm going to drive up tomorrow. I'm going to sit with her. I'm going to show her the publication when it goes live. I'm going to read her the parts about Millbrook. I'm going to tell her the man who did it has a name, and the truth about him is finally out, and the valley is safe."

Wren nodded, though he could not see her. "I'll come with you."

"You don't have to."

"I know. I want to."

Another silence. Softer this time. The highway signs began to show the exit for Amity Falls. Twenty miles. Fifteen. Ten.

"Wren," Theo said.

"Yeah?"

"The note in the storage unit. *You're late. — C.D.*" He paused. "He used an em dash. In the note. He typed an em dash. Who types an em dash on a phone keyboard?"

Wren almost smiled. Almost. "Drake does. He's pretentious about punctuation. Priya caught him using Oxford commas in a threat letter once. Said it was the most revealing evidence in the file."

Theo actually laughed then. Short. Surprised. "Of course he does. Of course the information broker who spent nineteen years building a damning partial record of a magical valley's bargain history is pedantic about punctuation."

"He's not magical," Wren said. "He's just a man with a file. And the file is empty now."

"Empty," Theo agreed. "And we have the complete record. And we're publishing it in forty-eight hours. And he's somewhere in Millbrook with a lawyer, trying to negotiate a deal for intelligence he no longer controls."

The exit loomed. Theo signaled, moved right, slowed for the ramp. The valley road unwound ahead of them, the familiar curve through the trees, the sign painted by hand: *Amity Falls - Population 312*. The dash on the sign was a hyphen. Short. Honest.

"He'll talk," Theo said as they crested the rise and the valley opened below them, the orchards and the store and the council hall and the new archive building with its fresh paint and its bolted cabinet. "He'll talk to anyone who'll listen. He'll write his version. He'll claim he was a whistleblower. He'll claim he was a researcher. He'll claim he was trying to protect people from a dangerous secret."

"Let him," Wren said. "We've already written ours. The complete one. The true one. With every name. Every debt. Every cost."

"Every cost," Theo repeated. He turned onto the valley road. The tires hummed on the pavement. "Including mine."

The archive building came into view. Lights burned in the windows. A car was parked out front, Priya's cruiser; another, Yusuf's truck. The door stood open, spilling yellow light onto the porch.

Theo pulled in behind them, killed the engine. Sat for a moment with his hands on the wheel.

"Forty-eight hours," he said. "Then it's done. The race is over. The record is public. The valley stands on its own terms, for the first time in however many generations."

"For the first time ever," Wren corrected. "Because we're not hiding it anymore. We're not managing it. We're telling it."

Theo looked at her then. Really looked. The headlamps cut out. The interior light came on, soft and yellow. In that light, his face was tired and hard and something else. Something that looked like trust.

"Yeah," he said. "We're telling it."

He opened his door. Wren opened hers. The night air carried the scent of apples and pine and the faint metallic tang of the stream that ran behind the orchard. The porch light swung on its chain. Priya stood in the doorway, her badge catching the glow. Dev beside her, his evidence bag empty in his hand. Yusuf on the steps, his phone pressed to his ear.

Wren shouldered her bag. Theo grabbed the file box from the back seat. They walked up the path together, boots on gravel, shadows long behind them.

The door frame held the weight of everyone who had ever paid the debt. The room inside held the record of every payment. And on the table, waiting for the final edit, the complete history of the bargain sat in plain type on white paper, ready for the world.

No more gaps. No more restricted notations. No more standing arrangements.

Just the truth.

And the truth, Wren thought as she crossed the threshold, was the only thing that had ever belonged to them.
