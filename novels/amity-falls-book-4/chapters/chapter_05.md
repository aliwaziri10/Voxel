The drive to Millbrook took two hours on roads that straightened and widened the farther they got from the valley. Wren watched the tree line thin into orchards, then into fields stitched with irrigation ditches that caught the morning light like thrown wire. Theo drove a county-issued sedan that smelled of coffee stains and old paper, his hands loose on the wheel, one finger tapping a rhythm against the rim that didn't match the radio.

"You've done this before," she said. Not a question.

"Secured evidence under court order? Three times in the last year." He didn't look over. "Never for a box that might not exist, granted. But the process is the same."

"The box exists."

"Your confidence is noted." A pause. "The judge who signed the order, Hendricks — he's fair but he doesn't like his time wasted. If the box isn't where the inventory says it is, we leave empty-handed and I explain to the DA why a temporary order got burned on a hunch."

"It's not a hunch."

"I know." He signaled, merged onto the bypass that curled around Millbrook's center. "That's why I'm driving."

The records building sat three blocks from the courthouse, a low brick structure with windows narrowed against the summer heat. Inside, the air conditioning fought a losing battle against the smell of dust and leather bindings. Theo moved through the lobby like he owned it, nodding to a clerk who didn't look up from her crossword, guiding Wren past the public terminals into a corridor where the fluorescent lights hummed a steady, tired note.

"Hendricks gave us until noon," Theo said, stopping at a door marked *Archives: Authorized Personnel Only*. He swiped a badge, punched a code. The lock clicked. "After that, the order expires and we're just two people asking questions nobody wants to answer."

The room beyond was smaller than Wren expected, crowded with metal shelving that rose to the dropped ceiling. Boxes sat in neat rows, labeled in fading ink: *Millbrook County: Property Disputes, 1980-1995*; *Tax Assessment Appeals, 1972*; *Correspondence: Unincorporated Territories*.

"Unincorporated territories," Wren said, reading the last label. "That's us."

"Officially, Amity Falls doesn't exist on any county map," Theo said. He walked to the third shelf, ran a finger along the spines until he stopped at a box indistinguishable from the others — same gray cardboard, same handwritten label: *Misc. Sealed: Court Order 94-112*. "But the paperwork remembers."

He lifted it down carefully, set it on the metal table bolted to the floor. The box was heavier than it looked. Its lid was secured not with tape but with a thick red wax seal, the kind that required a knife or a deliberate thumb to break.

Wren stepped closer. The emblem pressed into the wax made her breath catch.

A circle bisected by a vertical line, with three smaller circles arranged along the left arc, not quite a face, not quite a symbol she could name. She had seen it once before, heated in a spoon over a candle flame in the valley's old meeting hall, pressed onto the wrists of ten volunteers who had agreed to share a debt none of them should have had to carry alone.

"The ring ritual," she said, very quiet. "Book 3. The wax Dev used — it was this same emblem."

Theo's head came up sharp. "You're certain."

"I held the spoon. Priya held the seal. It was..." She swallowed. "It was the only way to bind the distribution. The emblem had to be identical on every wrist, or the weight wouldn't split clean."

Theo stared at the box. His face went through something: calculation, then a deeper shift, like a fault line moving under ground. "The Millbrook case from nineteen years ago. The one Drake was tied to. The inventory says this box was sealed by Judge Hendricks himself, same judge who signed our order today. But the emblem..." He reached out, fingertip hovering over the wax without touching. "Hendricks doesn't use a personal seal. County clerks use a standard stamp. This is hand-pressed."

"Someone pressed it by hand. Nineteen years ago."

"Or someone gave Hendricks a seal to use. Someone who knew what this emblem meant."

The silence stretched. Outside, a siren wailed and faded. The fluorescent lights kept humming.

"We need to open it," Wren said.

"We need to document it first." Theo pulled his phone, opened the camera. "Chain of custody. Every angle. The seal, the box, the label, the shelf location. Then we break it in front of a witness. Hendricks' clerk, maybe. Someone with no stake in what's inside."

He moved with the precision Wren was learning to recognize, the archivist's instinct to preserve before revealing. She watched him photograph the wax from three sides, the label, the cardboard corners worn soft by decades of handling. Only when he was satisfied did he nod to the clerk who had appeared in the doorway, a woman with gray hair pinned up and a pen tucked behind her ear.

"Marilyn," Theo said. "Witness for the record."

Marilyn didn't ask what was in the box. She signed the log Theo handed her, watched him slide a letter opener under the wax, watched the seal fracture cleanly down the center. The lid lifted.

Inside, layered in acid-free sleeves: photographs, their edges curled; a sheaf of handwritten notes in ink faded to sepia; a folded map of the valley drawn with a surveyor's precision, marking boundaries that didn't match any official record; and at the bottom, a single thick envelope, its flap sealed with the same red wax, the same emblem.

Theo didn't reach for it. His hand hovered, then fell to his side.

"Drake's partial record," Wren said. "Or part of it. This is what he's been building for nineteen years."

"Or what someone built for him." Theo's voice was flat. "The Millbrook case went cold because the evidence vanished. The lead investigator, my grandfather's partner, swore the file was on his desk the night before the hearing. Next morning, gone. The only thing left was a wax seal on the empty folder. This emblem."

Wren felt the cold spread through her chest, the Finder's instinct stirring, the itch behind her eyes that meant *something lost is near*. She clamped down on it. Not here. Not now. The cost would be a memory of this moment: Theo's face in the harsh light, the smell of old paper, the weight of the box on the table. She couldn't afford to lose any piece of this.

"Your grandfather's partner," she said carefully. "That's the missing decade. Your grandmother's."

Theo didn't flinch. "My grandmother worked in this office. Clerk typist, 1978 to 1988. Then she didn't. The personnel file says she resigned. Her personnel file." He emphasized the possessive. "The resignation letter is in her handwriting, but the date is wrong. Postdated by six months. And the witness signature—" He stopped. Jaw tight. "The witness signature is Judge Hendricks. Same judge. Same seal."

The connection hung between them, unspoken but absolute. The valley's secrets and Theo's family secrets used the same wax.

"I didn't come here for this," Theo said, low. "I came for the valley's history. For the bargain. For Drake. My grandmother's decade — that's mine. It's separate. It has to be separate."

"Is it?" Wren asked. "The box was sealed in '94. Your grandmother's missing decade ended in '88. But the emblem —"

"Doesn't prove anything." He turned away, facing the shelves. "Emblems can be copied. Seals can be forged. Hendricks has been a judge for forty years; maybe he liked the design. Maybe it's a coincidence."

"Coincidence is what people call patterns they don't want to follow."

A sharp breath. "I've spent fifteen years building a career on evidence, not patterns. Patterns are how you get wrongful convictions. Patterns are how you convince yourself a story is true because it *feels* true." He faced her again, and his eyes were hard. "My grandmother vanished from her own life for ten years. Came back different. Wouldn't say where she'd been, wouldn't say why she left. My mother — her daughter — died never knowing. *I* have spent my adult life digging through archives, court records, property deeds, census data, anything that might tell me where a woman goes when she disappears from a town of twelve thousand people and comes back like nothing happened." His voice cracked, just once. "And now I'm standing here holding a box sealed with the same wax that bound a ritual I didn't believe in until last year, and I have to decide: do I follow the evidence wherever it leads, even if it burns the only family history I have left? Or do I stop? Walk away? Let Drake win because the alternative is finding out my grandmother was part of something that — that —"

He couldn't finish. His hands curled into fists at his sides.

Wren didn't move closer. She knew the shape of that fear; she had been raised inside it, protected from truths that might break her, told that ignorance was love. Her parents had hidden the valley's nature from her until she was old enough to inherit the documentation project, until the bargain's renewal fell to her generation. They called it shielding. She called it theft.

"I know what it costs to choose the truth," she said. "I know what it costs to have it chosen for you."

Theo laughed, a short, broken sound. "Do you. You were born into this. You didn't have to go looking for it. It found you."

"Because my parents decided I wasn't ready. They decided *for* me. Every year I grew up not knowing why the crops never failed, why the floods never reached the valley floor, why people got sick and sometimes didn't get better — they decided that was mercy. It wasn't. It was control." She met his gaze steadily. "When I finally found out, I had to rebuild everything I thought I knew about my family, my home, myself. The truth didn't set me free. It just gave me the pieces to build something honest. But I had to choose to pick them up."

Theo was silent. The fluorescent lights hummed.

"Your grandmother made a choice," Wren said quietly. "She might have been coerced. She might have volunteered. She might have redirected a toll that wasn't hers to redirect; we know that happens. But whatever she did, whatever she saw, whatever she agreed to — she *chose* to come back. She chose to stay silent. That's her truth to carry. But the box..." She gestured to the table. "The box isn't hers. It's the valley's. It's Drake's. It's the record of a bargain that affects every person in Amity Falls, Kindred or not. You didn't open it to find your grandmother. You opened it because it's evidence in a case that threatens the only home I have. That's why you're here. That's why *we're* here."

Slowly, Theo's fists unclenched. His shoulders dropped, not in defeat but in surrender: the surrender of a man who has held a line too long and finally lets someone else share the weight.

"Evidence," he repeated. "Right. Evidence."

He reached for the thick envelope at the bottom of the box. The wax seal cracked under his thumb.

Inside: a single sheet of paper, typed on brittle onionskin, carbon copy smudges at the edges. Theo unfolded it, read, and his face went pale.

Wren stepped close enough to read over his shoulder.

*Administrative Terms of the First Debt: Amendment VII*
*Ratified: October 14, 1923*
*Signatory: Ambrose Whitlock, Warden of the First Debt*
*Witness: Judge Arthur Hendricks, Millbrook County*

Below the header, dense paragraphs in legal language Wren only partly understood: clauses about memory allocation thresholds, debt transfer protocols, the conditions under which a bargain could be renewed, modified, or terminated. And at the bottom, a handwritten notation in faded ink:

*If the line fails, the debt returns to the source. The valley does not forgive. It collects.*

"Ambrose Whitlock," Theo whispered. "The Warden of the First Debt. The role, not the bloodline. Adelaide's letter said he tried to end the bargain. He didn't. He *amended* it."

"And Hendricks witnessed it." Wren's fingers found the edge of the table. "The same Hendricks who signed our court order today. The same Hendricks who witnessed your grandmother's resignation."

"Nineteen twenty-three." Theo did the math aloud. "He would have been... thirty. A young judge. Ambitious. Whitlock came to him. Why? To legitimize the amendment? To hide it in plain sight, filed under a court seal nobody would think to open?"

"Because nobody looks for the valley in Millbrook records. The valley doesn't exist on maps. It shouldn't exist in deeds."

"Except it does." Theo turned the page over. Blank. "One page. Just this one. The rest of the amendment, the original terms and the full text, is not here. Drake has a partial record. He has *fragments*. But this..." He tapped the paper. "This is the index. The pointer. It tells us what was amended. Not *how*."

"Drake wants the true administrative terms. The complete original bargain. He thinks this amendment, or the original it amended, gives him an edge. Control."

"Control of the debt allocation. Control of who pays and who collects." Theo's voice hardened. "If he publishes his partial version, he controls the narrative. The valley becomes a liability, a scandal, a story the world consumes. But if we publish the complete truth first: the amendment, the original terms, the full history. We take the weapon from his hand."

"We need the rest of the amendment. The original terms. Where are they?"

Theo stared at the paper. "Whitlock hid them. Book 3 said he buried his family's warning instead of sharing it. His descendants, Dev and the Nair line, they didn't know. But someone knew. Someone has been keeping the record. Updating it: nineteen twenty-three, nineteen ninety-four. The box was sealed in '94. Who sealed it?"

"Hendricks. Or someone using his seal."

"Someone who knew the emblem. The ring ritual emblem." Theo looked at Wren, and for the first time, she saw a crack in his face, a raw uncertainty beneath the archivist's distance. "The ring ritual was last year. The emblem is old. But the *use* of it on this box, in '94 — that's deliberate. Someone connected the ritual to the amendment. Someone knew the debt could be split, and they sealed that knowledge away."

"Dev's line. The Whitlock descendants. They're Finders now. Dev redirected tolls; he paid a debt with someone else's memory. That's the oldest taboo. But the ring ritual... it distributed *his* taking. The debt he ran by redirecting. The emblem bound that distribution."

"So the emblem represents distributed debt. Shared weight." Theo's mind was moving fast now, the archivist connecting threads. "And the amendment, Amendment VII, is about debt transfer protocols. Conditions for renewal. *If the line fails, the debt returns to the source.*" He looked at the notation again. "The valley doesn't forgive. It collects."

Wren felt the cold deepen. "The source. The original bargain. The first debt."

"Which Whitlock held. As Warden. Not by blood; by role. And the role..." Theo stopped. Blinked. "The role is hereditary *and* taught. Book 3 said it skips generations unpredictably. But it can also be *passed*. Transferred. Like a debt."

"Can it?"

"The amendment says 'debt transfer protocols.' Plural. Protocols." He met her eyes. "Wren. What if the Warden of the First Debt isn't a person? What if it's an *office*? And what if the office requires a holder, someone to carry the weight, and when the line fails, the debt *returns to the source*, meaning the valley itself collects from whoever holds the office?"

The implication landed like a stone in deep water.

"Ambrose Whitlock tried to end the bargain. Failed. Hid the terms. But he *amended* them first. He built in a fail-safe: if the Warden line fails, the debt returns to the source. The valley. Which means:"

"Which means the valley takes the memory," Wren finished. "From the Warden. Or from whoever the Warden designates. Or from everyone."

"Or from a redirected toll."

The word hung between them: *redirected*. The taboo. Dev had done it once, coerced by Ambrose Kell. The valley had taken Caleb's two years as payment. The ring ritual had split that weight across ten volunteers.

But the amendment — Amendment VII — suggested the valley's collection mechanism was built into the administrative terms. Codified. Legalized.

"Drake knows this," Theo said. "Or he suspects. His partial record; he's been building it for nineteen years. Since the Millbrook case went cold. Since the box was sealed. He knows there's an amendment. He knows it governs debt transfer. He wants the complete terms so he can *use* them. Control the debt. Control the valley."

"By threatening to publish."

"By threatening to publish *selectively*. His partial version. The parts that make the valley look like a cult, a conspiracy, a crime scene. He doesn't want to destroy the bargain. He wants to *administer* it. Be the one who decides who pays."

The fluorescent lights seemed to buzz louder. Marilyn the clerk had disappeared, leaving them alone with the box, the paper, the terrible clarity.

"We have to find the rest," Wren said. "The original terms. The complete amendment. Before Drake does."

"Agreed." Theo folded the onionskin carefully, returned it to the envelope. "But there's one more thing. The box: the inventory label. *Court Order 94-112*. I know that case number."

He pulled his phone again, not the camera this time: a notes app, dense with text. Scrolled. Tapped.

"Case 94-112. *County of Millbrook v. Unknown Parties: Sealed Evidence*. Filed June 1994. The plaintiff was the County. The defendant..." He stopped scrolling. "The defendant is listed as 'Unknown Parties.' But the attorney for the plaintiff — the DA who argued for the seal —"

He turned the screen toward her.

*Arthur Hendricks, Deputy District Attorney*

"Hendricks wasn't a judge in '94," Wren said. "He was the prosecutor. He sealed his own evidence."

"And the judge who approved the seal..." Theo scrolled further. "Judge Elias Voss."

The name hit Wren like a physical blow. "Voss. Mara's maiden name. Mara's family."

"Your wife's family," Theo corrected gently. "Mara Voss. Finder. Married to Caleb Reyes. Book 1 protagonist."

"Yes. Mara. Her family: they were Wardens. Shifters. But Elias Voss..." Wren's mind raced through the documentation she had inherited, the family trees she had mapped. "Elias Voss was Mara's great-uncle. The one who disappeared. The family never talked about him; they said he left the valley. Never came back."

"Disappeared in 1994?" Theo asked.

"Around then. The records are vague. Mara was young. She doesn't remember."

Theo set the phone down. His face was carefully blank, but his eyes held a new calculation. "The Voss line. Finders. Mara is a Finder. But Elias — if he was a Warden, a shifter... the lineages don't usually cross. But the Warden of the First Debt is a role, not a bloodline. What if Elias Voss *held* the role? What if he was the Warden after Whitlock? Or before?"

"Whitlock held it in 1923. The amendment. Then who? The role skips generations. Unpredictably. But it's hereditary *and* taught."

"Hereditary in the Whitlock line. Dev is a Whitlock descendant. But the role — the office — can be passed. Taught. What if Whitlock taught Elias Voss? What if the Voss line carried the Warden role for a generation? And in 1994, something happened. The line failed. The debt returned to the source. And Hendricks, the DA, not yet a judge, sealed the record of it."

"To protect the valley," Wren said. "Or to protect himself."

"Or to protect the woman who worked in his office. A clerk typist. 1978 to 1988. Then gone for a decade. Came back in '94, just in time for the sealing."

Theo's grandmother.

The silence this time was different. Heavier. Charged with the weight of converging threads: the valley's history, Theo's family, the box, the amendment, the wax seal that bound them all.

"Theo," Wren said slowly. "Your grandmother. What was her name?"

He hesitated. The archivist's precision warred with something older, rawer. "Elena. Elena Marsh. She married my grandfather in 1975. He died in '82. She worked here until '88. Then..." He stopped. "She came back in '94. Lived another twenty years. Died when I was twelve. I remember her garden. She grew rosemary. Said it was for remembrance. I thought it was a joke."

"Rosemary," Wren repeated. "For remembrance. The Finder's herb. We use it in the valley; we burn it when we search, to focus the gift. It doesn't grow wild here. It has to be cultivated."

Theo's breath caught. "She had a whole bed of it. Behind the house. My mother hated the smell. Said it gave her headaches. But my grandmother; she would sit out there for hours. Just... holding the sprigs. Like she was waiting for something to come back to her."

"A memory," Wren said. "Or the memory of a search. The cost of a Finder's gift."

"My grandmother wasn't Kindred. She wasn't from the valley. She was from Millbrook. Ordinary. There's no lineage in our family — I've checked. Census records, birth certificates, marriage licenses. Ordinary all the way back."

"Unless the lineage skipped, unless it was taught, not inherited. Unless—" Wren stopped. The thought was too large, too terrible: "Unless the Warden role *was* the lineage. And your grandmother held it. Or carried it. For a decade."

Theo went very still. "The missing decade. 1988 to 1994. Six years. Not ten. The personnel file says she resigned in '88. But the postdated letter... six months. So she left in early '89. Came back in '94. Five years."

"Five years," Wren echoed. "The same span as Caleb's missing memory. Two years stolen, but the debt ran longer. The bargain collects in increments."

"Five years," Theo repeated. "And in '94, Judge Elias Voss, Mara's great-uncle and possible Warden of the First Debt, approves a seal on a case filed by Arthur Hendricks, sealing evidence about 'Unknown Parties' with a wax emblem that matches a ritual that wouldn't happen for another twenty-five years."

"The ring ritual was new. Dev invented the distribution. But the *emblem*, the symbol, it's old. Ambrose Whitlock used it. He was the Warden in 1923. He must have created it. Or inherited it."

"So the emblem represents the Warden's office. The first debt. And someone used it in 1994 to seal a box that contains an amendment governing debt transfer. And your grandmother — ordinary, no lineage — disappears for five years and comes back the same year the box is sealed."

"She didn't just come back. She came back *different*. My mother said so. Said the woman who left wasn't the woman who returned. Said her eyes were wrong. Said she stopped sleeping. Said she would wake up screaming from dreams she couldn't remember."

"Nightmares of a search," Wren said. "The Finder's cost. The memory of the search is the payment. But if you redirect the toll; if you pay with someone else's memory, the searcher keeps their memory. But the *debt* still has to be paid. By someone."

"By the valley. By the source. *If the line fails, the debt returns to the source. The valley does not forgive. It collects.*"

The words from the amendment echoed in the small room.

"Theo," Wren said, and she reached for his hand, then stopped, fingers hovering. "If your grandmother was the Warden. Or carried the Warden's debt. And the line failed... the valley collected. From her. Five years of her life. Her memories. Her self. And she came back... empty. Or filled with something else."

"Or she redirected it," Theo said, barely audible. "She redirected the toll. Onto someone else. The valley collected from *someone else*. And she came back whole, but carrying the knowledge of what she'd done. The memory of the search she didn't pay for."

"Redirecting the toll is the oldest taboo. Dev did it once. He paid with Caleb's memory. The valley took two years from Caleb. Dev served a sentence for it. Substation evidence work. Priya's brother. He's the one who found the gap in Drake's account."

"Dev Nair. Finder. Whitlock descendant." Theo's hand found the table edge, gripping hard. "If my grandmother redirected a toll... she would have needed a Finder's gift. But she wasn't Kindred. Unless —"

"Unless the Warden role *grants* the gift. Or requires it. The Warden of the First Debt, the one who administers the bargain's terms. They would need to Find the debt. Locate the memory. Collect it."

"Or redirect it."

The fluorescent lights flickered. Once. Twice. Steadied.

"We need to tell Mara," Wren said. "About Elias Voss. About the amendment. About the possibility that her great-uncle was the Warden. She needs to know her family's part in this."

"Mara," Theo repeated. "And Caleb. And Priya. And Dev. And Yusuf. And everyone in the valley who has ever paid a debt or stood to inherit one." He looked at the box, the broken seal, the onionskin paper. "We publish the truth. All of it. The amendment. The original terms. The history of the Warden role. The redirected tolls. The ring ritual. Everything. We put it in the Archive. We make it public record. Before Drake can weaponize his partial version."

"Drake has the advantage of secrecy. We break the secrecy."

"Yes." Theo's voice strengthened. "We break it. Together."

The word hung between them — *together* — carrying more weight than the wax seal, more than the amendment, more than the decades of buried history.

Wren let her hand fall to the table, close to his but not touching. Not yet. The romance arc was at stage 2, working proximity, incidental competence. Stage 3 waited: shared wounds surface. This was the surface breaking.

"First," Theo said, "we get this box to the valley. Secure it. Catalog it. Then we find the rest of the amendment. The original terms. Wherever Whitlock hid them."

"Dev might know. He's the Whitlock descendant. He's been doing substation evidence work: procedural, exacting. He's the one who identified the gap in Drake's account."

"Then we start with Dev. And Priya. And the Archive."

"And my parents," Wren added. "They hid the truth from me for twenty years. They might know where Whitlock's papers are. The family warning he buried."

Theo nodded. "Your parents. My grandmother. The Voss line. The Hendricks line. All of it connected by wax and debt and silence."

He began repacking the box: photographs, notes, map, envelope, each item placed with deliberation. Wren helped, their fingers brushing once over the map's creased edge. Neither pulled away.

Outside, the Millbrook noon sun struck the brick building. Somewhere, a clock struck twelve. The temporary court order expired.

But the box was in their hands. The seal was broken. The truth had been touched.

Now came the harder part: deciding what to do with it.
