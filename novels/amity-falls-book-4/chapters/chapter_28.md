The proof copy sat on the table between them, its cover still carrying the faint chemical scent of fresh ink. Wren ran her fingers over the embossed title - *The Bargain of Amity Falls: A Complete Record* - and felt the strange lightness of a thing finished. Three hundred and twelve pages. Every renewal documented. Every debt named. Adelaide's letter and Whitlock's warning filed in appendices with their original handwriting reproduced beside the transcription. The index alone had taken three weeks to cross-reference against the county records Theo had brought from Millbrook.

"You've got a smudge on page eighty-seven," Theo said. He didn't look up from his own copy, a red pen poised over a marginal note. "Bottom paragraph. The date of the 1922 renewal. You've written March twelfth in the text but March eleventh in the footer."

Wren turned to the page. He was right, of course. She made the correction in pencil, her hand steady. "That's the last one."

"Last correction, or last error?"

"Both, I hope." She closed the cover. The binding cracked softly, a new book's first breath. "Yusuf said the press can start the run tomorrow if we deliver by noon."

Theo capped his pen. The scratch of the cap threading on was loud in the quiet of the workroom. "Then we deliver by noon. Nine days before his deadline."

"Nine days." Wren said the number aloud, as if testing its weight. "It feels like more time than it is. And also like none at all."

"Nine days is nine days." Theo gathered his papers, aligning the corners with a tap against the table. "It's enough for the print run. Enough for distribution to the county seat, to the state library, to the university archives he threatened to contact. Enough for the story to be ours before it's his."

"And if he moves faster?"

"Drake doesn't move fast. He moves when he has the advantage. His advantage was the threat of publication. We're taking that away." Theo looked at her, his expression calm in a way that never failed to unsettle her. "He's an information broker, Wren. Brokers need scarcity. We're flooding the market."

The workroom had been the valley's schoolhouse once, before the consolidated district bused the children to Millbrook. High windows, slanted light, the ghost of chalk dust in the floorboards. Wren had spent three months here with Theo, sorting through boxes of Adelaide's careful journals, Dev's evidence logs, the council minutes Yusuf had unsealed. The air still smelled of old paper and the mineral tang of the spring that ran beneath the foundation.

Her mother had brought lunch every Tuesday. Tamales wrapped in corn husks, still steaming. *Mija, you're too thin. You forget to eat when you're reading.* Wren had smiled and eaten and not told her that the reading was the only thing keeping the hollow place in her chest from expanding. The place where the questions lived. *Why didn't you tell me? Why did everyone decide what I could carry?*

Theo had noticed. Of course he had. He noticed everything. But he'd waited for her to speak first, the way he always did.

"Your mother asked about the launch," he said now, sliding his proofs into the leather satchel he carried everywhere. "She wanted to know if there would be cake."

"There will be cake. Odette promised. She's been testing frosting recipes for two weeks." Wren stood, stretching until her spine popped. "She said something about 'a historic occasion deserves buttercream that doesn't weep.'"

"Odette takes her buttercream seriously."

"Odette takes everything seriously. That's why the store still runs." Wren picked up her own copy of the proof, hugging it to her chest. "Walk me to the car? I need to stop at the orchard before the press run. Caleb wants to see the final version."

Theo lifted his satchel. "Lead the way."

The April air carried the sharp green scent of budding orchards, the muddy sweetness of the river waking from winter. Amity Falls sprawled along the valley floor, its rooftops uneven against the mountains, the church steeple still the tallest structure for miles. Satellite maps showed empty forest where the town sat - a glitch, a cartographic error that had protected them for generations. Now the error was documented in Appendix C, with coordinates and a surveyor's affidavit.

They walked past the general store where Odette was arranging a display of local honey jars, each labeled with the hive location and the season. She waved, wiping her hands on her apron. "Proofs look good, *querida*? Theo, you're not skipping lunch again, are you?"

"I ate at eleven," Theo said. "Thank you, Odette."

"Eleven is not lunch. Eleven is a suggestion." But she smiled, the crinkles at her eyes deepening. "Nine days. You'll both need your strength."

Wren felt the familiar tug in her chest - the Finder's awareness of things lost and things held. Odette's proposal memory, the exact wording gone but the joy intact. Denise's fifty years of secret renewal, now ended. Caleb's two years returned. The ring of ten who had shared Dev's taking, their memories knitted back together with invisible thread.

"Nine days," Wren repeated. "We'll be ready."

The orchard house smelled of apple wood smoke and lemon oil. Caleb met them at the kitchen door, flour on his forearms from a pie crust. "Come in, come in. Mara's in the office with the irrigation maps, but she'll want to see this too."

He led them to the kitchen table, clearing a space beside a bowl of rising dough. His hair had more gray at the temples than a year ago, but his eyes held the same quiet intensity, the memory of loss and return etched into his gaze. He took the proof copy from Wren with the reverence he gave a grafted sapling.

"Three hundred and twelve pages," he murmured, turning the cover. "All of it. The good years and the bad."

"All of it," Wren said. "No gaps. No 'standing arrangement' notation to hide behind."

Caleb's thumb traced the embossed lettering. "Denise cried when she saw the galley for the fiftieth renewal. She said she'd never seen her own name written in the record before. Fifty years, and she was always the one holding the pen for everyone else."

"She'll be at the launch," Theo said. "Yusuf invited her to sit on the stage."

"Good. She should be there." Caleb opened the book, flipping to the appendix where Adelaide's letter was reproduced. "She baked three pies yesterday. Apple, cherry, and something she called 'redemption rhubarb.' I didn't have the heart to ask what that meant."

Wren exchanged a glance with Theo. A private smile. *Redemption rhubarb.* Only Denise.

Mara appeared in the doorway, wiping her hands on a rag. Structural engineer, Finder, wife. She carried the blueprint tube she never went far without. "Caleb, the pressure readings on the east slope - oh. Hi."

"Hi yourself." Wren gestured to the book. "Final proof. Press run starts tomorrow."

Mara crossed the room in three strides, setting the tube down to take the book. Her fingers moved over the cover, the same reverence as Caleb's but sharper, more analytical. She was a Finder too, but her gift located structural weaknesses, stress fractures in stone and steel and earth. She read the valley's bones.

"It's heavy," she said. "Not just the paper. The truth of it."

"Truth is dense," Theo said. "That's why lies float."

Mara looked up, her dark eyes meeting his. "You're the archivist. You'd know."

"I'm the one who reads the footnotes. The footnotes are where the lies usually hide."

A laugh escaped her, surprised and genuine. "I like him, Wren. He's got teeth."

Wren felt warmth bloom in her chest, unrelated to the wood stove. "He does. He also has a red pen he's not afraid to use."

"Essential quality in a partner," Caleb said, not looking up from Adelaide's letter. "Editorial or romantic. Both, really."

The kitchen fell into a comfortable silence, the kind that settles between people who have survived something together. Wren watched Theo watch the Reyeses. He had no gift, no lineage, no claim to this valley except the one he'd built - grandmother by grandmother, record by record, question by patient question. Harriet Marsh, alive in a Millbrook care facility, her mind a house with a locked wing. The decade before 2007 gone. Drake's first attempt, nineteen years ago, the one that failed but didn't stop him.

Theo had told her the story one night over takeout containers in the workroom, the rain loud on the roof. *She remembers 1997. She remembers 2007. The ten years between are just... absent. Not forgotten. Taken. She can't say where she was. She can't say who she was with. She only knows something happened in Millbrook, and a man named Corwin Drake was there.*

Wren had asked, *Does she know about the valley?*

*She knows I come here. She knows I'm looking for something. She tells me to be careful, same as always. But sometimes...* He'd paused, his fork suspended. *Sometimes she looks at me with this expression. Like she's trying to place me. Like I'm a thing she lost and found again.*

Wren had reached across the table, her fingers brushing his. *We'll find what was taken. Both of us.*

Now, in the orchard kitchen, Caleb closed the book with a soft thump. "Nine days. You'll need witnesses for the delivery. Priya said she'd drive the first batch to the county seat. Dev's taking the university shipment. Yusuf's handling the state library."

"Priya's driving?" Wren asked. "I thought she had shift coverage issues."

"She traded with Walt Pruitt. He owes her from the snowstorm in '19. Said he'd walk barefoot over hot coals for her after she talked the downed power line into not killing them both." Caleb shrugged. "Caller's privilege. People remember the calm more than the cost."

Wren thought of Priya's cost. The composure debt paid after every use of her gift - the shaking hands, the cold sweat, the hours of quiet recovery where the world felt too loud and too bright. Priya never complained. She carried everyone, and no one carried her.

"Where is Priya?" Wren asked. "I haven't seen her since the council meeting."

"She's at the substation with Dev. Evidence cataloguing for the Drake file. They're building the chain of custody for his Millbrook records." Mara wiped her hands on her jeans. "Why? You need her?"

"I need to ask her something. Not official." Wren set her jaw. "Personal."

The three of them exchanged a look - the Reyeses and Theo, a silent communication Wren was still learning to read. Caleb's eyebrow lifted. Mara's mouth quirked. Theo's expression didn't change, but his shoulders shifted, the almost imperceptible straightening of a man who knows he's being discussed.

"Personal how?" Mara asked.

Wren hesitated. The question had been sitting in her throat for weeks, rising every time Theo stayed for dinner at her mother's table, every time he drove the mountain roads to Millbrook and came back, every time his hand found hers in the dark of the workroom after everyone else had gone home.

*Not Kindred. Not of the blood. Not bound by the bargain.*

"She's at the substation," Dev had texted her yesterday. *Priya's taking a break. Coffee in the break room. Come by if you can.*

The substation smelled of ozone and cold concrete, the hum of transformers a constant low vibration in the bones. Dev sat at a metal desk surrounded by evidence boxes, his Finder's gaze moving over documents with surgical precision. He looked up as Wren entered, his face tired but alert.

"Wren. Proofs done?"

"Press run tomorrow. Nine days."

"Good." He returned to a photograph, magnifying glass in hand. "Priya's in the break room. Second door on the left. She's been at the Millbrook chain since six this morning. Might be asleep."

"Thanks, Dev."

He didn't ask what she needed. He never did. That was Dev - procedural to the bone, evidence before emotion, but the emotion ran deep underneath, a subterranean river.

The break room smelled of burnt coffee and vending machine chocolate. Priya sat at the small table, her deputy uniform crisp despite the hour, a mug cradled in both hands. She looked up as Wren entered, her dark eyes sharpening.

"Wren. Proofs?"

"Done. Nine days."

Priya nodded, a single sharp movement. "Good. Dev's case is solid. Drake's Millbrook records show he knew about the bargain in '98. He's been building his file for nineteen years. Nineteen years, and he still only has the renewals Denise documented. He doesn't have the original terms. He doesn't have the Warden of the First Debt role definition. He has a partial record, and he's bluffing that it's complete."

"We're calling the bluff."

"Exactly." Priya set down her mug. "But that's not why you're here. You have that look. The one where you're about to ask something that isn't in the manual."

Wren pulled out the chair opposite her. The metal legs scraped the concrete floor. "There is no manual."

"There should be. I've been saying that for years." Priya's gaze was direct, unflinching. Caller's eyes. They saw the truth whether you spoke it or not. "Ask."

Wren exhaled. "Theo. He's not Kindred. He's not bound by the bargain. He doesn't carry a lineage debt. And he's... he's here. He stays. He chose to stay."

Priya waited.

"And I..." Wren's voice dropped. "I've never had someone stay who wasn't bound to. My father left. My grandmother died. The valley protects its own, but the protection comes with conditions. Debts. Memories given. Theo has none of that. He has a grandmother in Millbrook who doesn't remember him half the time. He has a career he put on hold. He has no reason to be here except the reasons he makes for himself."

"Reasons he makes for himself," Priya repeated. "Like Mara. Like me. Like Dev."

"Mara and Caleb are bound by the bargain. You and Dev are bound by blood and lineage. Theo is bound by..." Wren spread her hands. "What? Curiosity? Stubbornness? A petition he filed a year ago for a restricted index gap?"

"Was it a petition?" Priya asked quietly. "Or was it a man driving three hours on mountain roads because his grandmother lost ten years and the only lead pointed to a town that doesn't exist on maps?"

"He's an archivist. It's his job."

"His job is in Millbrook. His life is in Millbrook. His grandmother is in Millbrook." Priya leaned forward, her elbows on the table. "He's here, Wren. He's been here for a year. He helped you build an Archive from nothing. He learned your lineages, your debts, your history - not because he had to, but because you needed someone who could read the records without flinching. He sits with your mother's tamales and Caleb's pies and Odette's buttercream lectures, and he doesn't flinch."

"He should flinch. This place breaks people."

"Does it? Or does it reveal them?" Priya's voice hardened, the procedural tone dropping away. "I've known you since you were twelve years old, Wren Castellano. I've watched you carry the weight of being a Finder's granddaughter, the one who was protected from truths 'for her own good.' I watched you find your own gift, pay your own small debts, stare at the gaps in drawers and thank-you notes that made no sense. You hate being protected. You hate it more than anything."

Wren's throat tightened. "I hate that they decided what I could carry."

"Then why are you trying to decide for Theo?"

The question hit like a physical blow. Wren recoiled, her chair screeching back. "I'm not deciding for him. I'm asking - I need to know if he understands. If he *can* understand. What it means to love someone in a place where love is measured in memories given. Where the cost of staying is paid in pieces of yourself."

Priya was silent for a long moment. The transformer hum filled the space between them.

"Nine days," Priya said finally. "Nine days until Drake's deadline. Nine days until the world knows what this valley is. And you're sitting here asking me whether a man who chose you - chose *this* - is capable of understanding the cost?"

"He didn't choose this. He chose the records. He chose the story. I happened to be part of it."

"Did he?" Priya's head tilted, the Caller's assessment. "When he stayed after the ring ritual. When he turned down the Millbrook promotion. When he bought the secondhand desk for the workroom because he said the light was better on the left side - the same left side where you sit. When he learns your mother's tamale recipe by heart because he knows you forget to eat. When he drives the mountain roads in winter because the county archives have a gap only you can help him fill, and he calls it work but you both know it's not."

Wren's breath caught. "You're reading me."

"I'm reading *him*. I'm a Caller, Wren. I compel truth within five feet. But I don't need my gift to see this. I just need to watch." Priya stood, moving to the counter to refill her mug. The coffee gurgled, dark and bitter. "You're not asking if he understands the cost. You're asking if you're allowed to let him pay it. If you're allowed to be someone's reason to stay, when you've never had that yourself."

The mug clinked against the carafe. Priya turned, leaning against the counter, arms crossed.

"Mara stayed. Caleb stayed. Dev stayed. I stayed. None of us had every reason handed to us. We made reasons. We built them, day by day, debt by debt, choice by choice." Priya's voice softened. "Theo's not Kindred. So what? Kindred isn't a lineage. It's a practice. It's showing up. Again and again. Especially when it costs."

Wren stared at her hands, folded on the table. The Finder's hands - capable of locating lost things, lost people, lost truths. The cost written in the gaps of her memory: a note in a drawer, a thank-you that puzzled her. Small debts. She had never paid a large one.

*Yet.*

"Nine days," she whispered.

"Nine days." Priya's mug clicked on the table as she sat again. "In nine days, the world will know. The bargain, the debts, the lineages, the renewals. Drake's partial record will be drowned in the complete one. And after that..." She shrugged. "After that, you get to decide what kind of life you build. Not what kind of life you're allowed. What kind you *build*."

"With him?"

"With whoever you choose. That's the point, Wren. The bargain trades memory for protection. But protection from what? From the outside? From each other? From yourselves?" Priya's gaze held hers, steady and unyielding. "The ring ritual freed Denise. It restored Caleb. It distributed Dev's taking so no one person carried the weight. That was the old way - shared burden, shared survival. But the new way..." She gestured vaguely, encompassing the substation, the valley beyond, the Archive waiting to open. "The new way is yours to define. Theo's not Kindred by blood. He's Kindred by practice. The question isn't whether he's sure about you. The question is whether you're sure about him."

Wren felt something shift inside her, a tectonic plate sliding into new alignment. The hollow place in her chest - the one where the questions lived - didn't fill. But the edges softened. The sharpness dulled.

"He drove three hours in a blizzard last February," she said, the memory surfacing unbidden. "The pass was closed. He drove anyway. Said the county archives had a land grant document from 1847 that might clarify the original boundary survey. He showed up at the workroom at midnight, covered in snow, holding a faxed copy like it was a holy relic."

Priya's mouth curved. "There it is."

"He missed his grandmother's birthday dinner. He called her from the workroom phone, and she didn't remember who he was, and he still stayed to finish the cross-reference."

"There it is."

"And yesterday, when I asked him what he'd do after the publication - after Drake, after the Archive, after all of it - he said..." Wren paused, the memory warm in her chest. "He said, 'I have a desk in the workroom. The light's better on the left side. I think I'll keep it.'"

Priya laughed, a short surprised sound. "He said that?"

"Almost exactly."

"Stubborn archivist. Thorough. Dry. Patient." Priya ticked the qualities off on her fingers. "Sounds like a man who's already decided. The only question left is you."

Wren stood, her body moving before her mind caught up. "I should go. The press run. Nine days."

"Wren."

She paused at the door.

"Whatever you decide," Priya said, her voice stripped of procedure, bare and honest. "You don't have to carry it alone. That's the point of Kindred. By blood or by practice. You don't have to carry it alone."

The mountain roads unwound beneath Wren's tires, the valley spreading below - the orchard, the store, the church steeple, the schoolhouse-workroom where the proofs waited in their boxes. Nine days. The number pulsed in time with her heartbeat.

*Theo has no reason to be here except the reasons he makes for himself.*

*Kindred isn't a lineage. It's a practice.*

She thought of her mother's tamales, steaming on the workroom table. Theo's red pen, precise and ruthless. The way he'd said *We're flooding the market* with the calm certainty of a man who had already won.

The car crested the rise, and the Archive building came into view - the old library renovated, its new sign still wrapped in burlap. *Amity Falls Archive. Opening Spring.*

Nine days.

Wren gripped the steering wheel, and for the first time in a year, the hollow place in her chest didn't ache. It simply existed, a space shaped like a question she was finally ready to answer.
