The morning light came through the high windows of the meeting room at the county building, pale and thin as water. Wren had spent the night before laying out the manuscript sections across the long table, each lineage's portion clipped to its own board with the same careful attention she gave to every piece of paper that passed through her hands. The Wardens' section. The Menders'. The Callers'. The Weathers'. The Finders'. The Wicks, thin as tissue, barely a page between them all.

She stood at the head of the table now, her fingers resting on the edge of the Wardens' board. The door opened behind her.

"Morning," Theo said. He carried a thermos and a cloth bag that smelled of the bakery on Main. "Brought coffee. And those cinnamon things you liked last week."

Wren turned, the corners of her mouth lifting. "You remembered."

"I write things down." He set the bag down beside her notes. "It's a habit. Comes with the job."

The representatives arrived in ones and twos. First Mara Voss for the Wardens, moving with the quiet economy of someone who had learned to measure every motion. She wore a flannel shirt rolled to the elbows and carried a clipboard. Behind her came Dr. Aris Thorne for the Menders, his white coat exchanged for a cardigan, and Priya Nair for the Callers, her deputy's badge catching the light. Yusuf entered for the Weathers, his chair creaking as he settled into it. Odette Reynolds arrived last for the Wicks, her walker clicking on the linoleum, and Dev Nair slipped in behind her for the Finders, carrying a battered briefcase that had seen generations of use.

No one spoke at first. The weight of what they were doing sat in the room like a held breath.

"Thank you for coming," Wren said, her voice steady. "Each of you has the section that covers your lineage's role in the bargain. The costs. The renewals. The debts paid and the debts still outstanding. I'm not asking you to agree with how I've written it. I'm asking you to tell me where I've gotten it wrong."

Mara picked up the Wardens' board. She read slowly, her finger tracing each line. The others followed suit. The only sounds were paper turning and the occasional scratch of a pen in a margin.

Wren watched them. She had expected resistance. She had expected the old instincts to flare, the reflex to protect what had been hidden for so long. Instead she saw concentration. She saw the care of people who knew what it meant to have their history told by someone else.

Mara set the board down. "The first renewal after the founding. You have it as a voluntary offering. It wasn't. The founding families drew lots. My great-grandmother's name came up. She was sixteen."

Wren nodded, uncapping her pen. "I'll change the wording. Was it recorded anywhere?"

"Not in writing. My grandmother told me. She said the lots were weighted. The families with more land had fewer slips in the box."

Wren wrote it down. "Weighted lots. Noted."

Aris Thorne cleared his throat. "The Menders' section. The epidemic of 1918. You list the cost as seven memories. It was nine. Two of them were children. Their parents volunteered in their place. The bargain accepted the substitution."

Priya's pen moved across her page. "The Callers' compulsion range. You have it as five feet. It's five feet for truth. For calm it extends to twelve. The cost scales differently. You've conflated them."

Yusuf turned a page. "The Weathers' drought mitigation in '87. You have the cost distributed across three households. It was four. The fourth household left the valley before the debt came due. The bargain collected from the remaining three."

Odette Reynolds smiled, a small private expression. "The Wicks. You have us nearly died out in the thirties. We didn't nearly die out. We chose to stop passing the gift. Three generations. We started again when the valley needed light in the winter of '62. The cost was my sister's memory of her wedding day. She doesn't regret it. But she doesn't remember it either."

Wren wrote everything. Her hand moved fast, capturing each correction, each nuance. The manuscript grew heavier with truth.

Dev Nair had been silent, his briefcase closed on his lap. Now he opened it and withdrew a sheaf of documents, rubber-banded and annotated.

"Finder lineage," he said. "I've cross-referenced every debt entry in your draft against the substation logs and the family records I pulled last month. There are fourteen discrepancies. Most are minor. Dates off by a week. Names spelled two ways. Three are substantive."

He laid out three pages. "The 1942 search for the lost Watkins boy. You have the Finder paying with a memory of her own childhood. The log shows she redirected the toll. The memory came from the boy's mother. She didn't know. She still doesn't."

The room went quiet. Redirecting the toll. The oldest taboo.

"Who was the Finder?" Wren asked, her voice low.

"Elias Voss. Mara's great-uncle." Dev met Mara's eyes. "The log is in the substation. I photographed it. It's in the packet."

Mara's face didn't change. She picked up the photograph, studied it. "Elias. He died in '43. The winter flood. I never knew."

"The substation logs have entries going back to the founding," Dev said, addressing the table. "Every Finder debt. Every search. Every payment. I've been through all of them. The redirecting wasn't common. But it wasn't unknown. There are twenty-seven documented cases over two hundred years. The last one was nineteen years ago. Millbrook."

Theo set down his coffee. "Millbrook."

"Corwin Drake's first attempt," Dev said. "He coerced a Finder there. Made him redirect the toll for Drake's own record-building. The Finder was caught. The case went cold. But the substation log has the entry. I found it six months ago. It's how I knew where to look when Drake resurfaced."

Priya's hand rested on her brother's forearm, brief and light. "You've been carrying this."

"I've been verifying it." Dev's jaw tightened. "Every entry. Every name. I didn't want to bring something to this table that couldn't stand up to scrutiny."

Theo leaned forward. "May I see the Millbrook entry?"

Dev passed him the photograph. Theo studied it, his expression going still in the way it did when he was reading something that mattered. "Harriet Marsh," he said quietly. "My grandmother. She's listed as the source memory. The Finder was - "

"Silas Kettering," Dev said. "He served two years. Got out in '09. Lives in a town outside the county now. I talked to him last week. He remembers. He said Drake didn't ask. He told. Said he'd hurt Kettering's daughter if he didn't comply."

The air in the room shifted. The abstract threat of Drake became something with a name and a face and a daughter somewhere beyond the valley's edge.

"Does your grandmother know?" Wren asked.

Theo folded the photograph carefully. "She knows she lost time. She doesn't know why. She doesn't know who took it. I never told her about the valley. I thought - I thought if she knew what this place was, what it cost, she'd lose what little peace she has."

"You protected her," Priya said. It wasn't a question.

"I tried to. Same as everyone here protects someone."

Wren felt the familiar twist in her chest. Protected. The word she had grown up with. The word that had shaped her whole understanding of what love meant.

"Thank you, Dev," she said. "For the work. For the care."

Dev nodded once. "It's the job. The evidence doesn't care about feelings. It just is."

Theo laughed softly, a dry huff. "Finally. Someone who speaks my language."

Dev's mouth quirked. "Archivist?"

"Records investigator. Close enough." Theo extended his hand. "Theo Marsh."

"Dev Nair." They shook. "You're the one who found the standing arrangement notation."

"Took me three years. You?"

"Took me six months to trace the substation log to the Millbrook case. Another six to confirm the Kettering connection." Dev glanced at Priya. "My sister helped. She's good at making people talk."

"I'm good at making people tell the truth," Priya corrected. "There's a difference."

"Five feet for truth. Twelve for calm." Theo nodded. "Wren explained. I've been staying inside the five-foot line. Just in case."

Dev laughed, a real sound, surprised out of him. "Smart man. Most people don't know the range until they're already in it."

"I do my research."

The tension in the room had shifted. Not gone; the corrections still waited, the hard truths still sat on the table. But the sharp edge had dulled. People who had spent generations guarding secrets were sitting across from people who had spent careers uncovering them, and they were talking.

Wren watched Dev and Theo. Her gaze moved to Priya's hand staying near her brother's shoulder. Then to Mara tracing her finger over the photograph of her great-uncle's log entry. Odette Reynolds folded her hands on her walker and smiled at nothing anyone else could see.

This was what it looked like. Not unity. Not forgiveness. Something simpler: people doing the work together.

"Right," Yusuf said, closing his board. "The Weathers' section is clean. Two date corrections. One name. I'll email you the details."

"Menders are clean," Aris said. "With the epidemic correction noted."

"Callers," Priya said. "Range clarification. And the 1998 compulsion cost - you have it as a memory of the Caller's mother. It was the Caller's memory of her mother's hands. Different thing. The bargain takes the specific, not the general."

Wren wrote. "Specific, not general. Noted."

"Wicks," Odette said. "Just the one correction. My sister's wedding. And you might add that we don't mind the cost. We mind the forgetting. But we'd choose it again. Every one of us."

"Wardens," Mara said. "Weighted lots. And the 1973 renewal - you have the cost falling on the Warden who performed the ceremony. It fell on his wife. She wasn't Warden lineage. The bargain took from her anyway."

The room fell silent again. Heavier this time.

"The bargain doesn't care about lineage," Yusuf said quietly. "It cares about proximity. Connection. The weight falls where the bond is strongest."

"Then why do we pretend it follows blood?" Mara asked.

"Because pretending lets us sleep," Dev said. "Because if we admitted the bargain takes from anyone we love, not just the ones who carry the gift, we'd have to decide whether the protection is worth it. And some of us couldn't live with that decision."

Wren capped her pen. The manuscript sat before her, thick with corrections, dense with the weight of two hundred years of truth. Her hand rested on the Finders' board - her lineage, her grandmother's lineage, her own debt still small, still just gaps in drawers and puzzled thank-yous.

"Finders," she said. "My section."

She picked up the board. The pages were fewer than the others. The Finders had always been fewer. Their gift was quieter. Their costs more private.

"You have the 1985 search for the missing Miller girl," Dev said. "You have the Finder paying with a memory of her first kiss. The log says it was the memory of her mother's lullaby. She sang it to the girl while they waited for the search to end. The bargain took the song."

Wren's fingers tightened on the board. "My grandmother."

"Elena Castellano. She found the girl. The girl lived. Elena never sang that lullaby again. She couldn't remember it. She used to hum it while she worked in the garden. After the search, she just... stopped."

Wren had never known that. She knew her grandmother stopped singing. She knew the garden went quiet. She had thought it was grief, or age, or the thousand small losses that accumulate in a life. She had never known it was a specific memory, taken by the bargain her grandmother served.

"She never told me," Wren said.

"Finders don't talk about the costs," Dev said. "Not even to each other. Especially not to each other. It's the way we're taught. The debt is yours. The silence is yours. Speaking it doesn't lessen it."

"Maybe it should," Theo said.

Dev looked at him. "Maybe. But that's not how it works. Not yet."

Wren set the board down. She turned the page to the most recent entry. Her own first search. Six months ago. A lost wedding ring in the orchard. The memory taken: the smell of rain on hot asphalt, the summer she was twelve, riding her bike home from the library with a stack of books balanced on the handlebars.

She had noticed it fading three days ago. She had reached for the memory - the way she always did, the way Finders do, checking their own gaps like touching a bruise - and found only the outline. The smell was gone. The heat was gone. The books were gone. Just the fact remained: she had ridden her bike home in the rain. She knew it happened. She couldn't feel it happening.

It was a small debt. A small memory. The first one.

But it was fading faster than the others.

She had paid small debts before. A note in a drawer she couldn't remember writing. A thank-you from a stranger she couldn't recall helping. The gaps were familiar. But they had always been static. Frozen. The memory gone but the shape of it held in place like a fossil in stone.

This one was eroding.

She hadn't told anyone. Not her mother. Not Theo. Not even Dev.

"Wren?" Theo's voice. Close. His hand on her shoulder, warm through her shirt. "You went somewhere."

She blinked. The meeting room snapped back into focus. The representatives were gathering their boards. Mara was helping Odette with her coat. Yusuf was folding his notes. Priya was saying something quiet to Dev, who was packing his briefcase with the careful precision of someone who knew exactly where everything belonged.

"I'm here," Wren said. "Just - the Finders' section. The first search."

Theo's hand squeezed her shoulder. "The ring."

She nodded. "The memory's fading. Not gone. Fading. Like water through sand."

Theo's face went still. "How fast?"

"Three days. Half the sensory detail is already gone. The rest is... thinning."

"Does it hurt?"

She considered the question. "No. That's the thing. It doesn't hurt. It just... isn't. And the not-hurting feels worse than hurting would."

Theo didn't say anything. He just stood beside her, his presence a steady weight against the current pulling at her insides. After a moment, he reached into his bag and withdrew a small notebook, worn soft at the edges.

"When my grandmother's memories started going," he said, "the doctors called it early onset. They were wrong. But she knew. She knew something was wrong before anyone believed her. She started writing things down. Not the memories - she couldn't hold them long enough. She wrote the facts. 'I have a grandson named Theo. He was born in 1997. He likes mint chocolate chip ice cream. He became an archivist because he wanted to find me.' She wrote it over and over. Different notebooks. Different days. Same facts. She said if she wrote them enough, the shape would stay even if the feeling didn't."

He held out the notebook. "I started doing it too. Not for memory loss. For the work. The cases. The details that matter. The shape stays. The feeling comes and goes. But the shape is what you build on."

Wren took the notebook. The cover was cracked leather, the pages yellowed at the edges. She opened it. The first page held a single line in Theo's precise script: *Harriet Marsh. Born 1949. Lost 1997-2007. Grandmother. Still here.*

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me yet. You have to use it."

She smiled, a real one this time. "I will."

The representatives filed out. Mara paused at the door, looking back at the table where the corrected manuscript lay. "We'll send the family records. The ones that aren't in the substation. Photographs. Letters. The things that make it real."

"Thank you, Mara."

"Don't thank me. Just make sure it's right." She left.

Aris Thorne followed. Yusuf. Odette Reynolds, moving slowly but steadily. Priya and Dev went together, Dev's briefcase clicking against his hip, Priya's hand light on his elbow.

Theo stayed. He leaned against the table beside Wren, close enough that his arm brushed hers.

"Dev's good," he said. "Thorough. It makes my job easier."

"He's careful. He has to be. After what happened with Kell."

"Kell coerced him. That's not on Dev."

"I know. But the valley doesn't always distinguish between what someone chose and what was done to them. Dev knows that. He's been proving himself ever since."

Theo was quiet for a moment. "My grandmother. Harriet. She doesn't remember the decade before 2007. She doesn't know where she was. She doesn't know what was taken. I've spent fifteen years trying to find out. Every record. Every lead. Every dead end." He looked at Wren. "Dev gave me the first real lead in fifteen years. A name. A substation log. A man named Silas Kettering who remembers."

"Will you talk to him?"

"I already have. Yesterday. While you were at the orchard with Caleb." Theo's mouth twisted. "Kettering's angry. He's scared. He doesn't trust anyone from the valley. He doesn't trust me. But he talked. He told me what Drake did. How he did it. Why."

"And?"

"And it matches. The timeline. The method. The substation entry. Drake's first attempt wasn't about destroying the bargain. It was about learning how it worked. He used Millbrook as a test case. He took my grandmother's memory to see if he could. To see what the bargain would give up. He's been building his record ever since. Nineteen years of digging. Nineteen years of waiting."

Wren felt cold. "He's not bluffing."

"No. He's not. He has a partial record. It's damning. It's incomplete. And he's going to publish it unless we give him the complete terms first."

"Which we're doing. We're publishing first. The true history. All of it."

"Right. We are." Theo straightened. "But Wren - the Finders' section. Your first search. The memory fading."

She nodded. "It's the cost. Small debts, small memories. But this one's different. It's not holding."

"Different how?"

"I don't know. That's what scares me. Every other gap I've ever had - they're stable. Fixed. This one's moving. Like the bargain is... reconsidering. Like it's deciding whether to take more."

"Or like the bargain is changing."

She looked at him. "What do you mean?"

"The ring ritual. Denise freed. Caleb's memory restored. The weight split ten ways. The bargain has never been distributed like that before. Never. Two hundred years and it's always been one person carrying the full weight. Now it's shared. The terms are shifting. Maybe the costs are shifting too."

"Shifting how?"

"I don't know. But Dev found twenty-seven redirected tolls in two hundred years. Twenty-seven times the bargain accepted someone else's memory instead of the Finder's own. The bargain adapts. It negotiates. It's not a machine. It's... a relationship. A bad one. But a relationship."

Wren stared at the manuscript. At the corrections layered over corrections. At the truth they had spent a year uncovering, verifying, writing down.

"A relationship," she repeated. "With terms we're finally writing down."

"Terms we're publishing. On our terms. Before Drake does it on his."

She picked up the Finders' board. The first search entry. The fading memory. She opened Theo's notebook to a blank page and wrote: *First search: June. Lost wedding ring in Reyes Orchard. Cost: memory of rain on asphalt, summer twelve, bicycle, library books. Fading since November 3. Sensory detail 50% gone. Fact remains. Feeling going.*

She closed the notebook. Slipped it into her bag.

"Let's finish the draft," she said. "All of it. Every lineage. Every correction. Then we publish."

Theo picked up his thermos. "Coffee's cold."

"Mine too."

"Want to walk? There's a place on Third that makes decent tea."

Wren looked at the empty chairs. Sunlight moved across the corrected pages. The shape of the truth they had built was heavier and more honest than anything the valley had ever allowed itself to hold.

"Yeah," she said. "I'd like that."

They walked out together into the cold bright morning, the manuscript secure in its box, the first search fading in Wren's mind like a photograph left in the sun, and the shape of it holding steady in a worn notebook beside her heart.
