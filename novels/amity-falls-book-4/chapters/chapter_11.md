The morning light came through the Archive windows at a slant that made dust visible, and Wren had already catalogued three Warden testimonies before she realized she couldn't remember the name of the boy who'd taught her to track deer in the snow behind her grandmother's house.

She stopped, pen hovering over the page. The testimony described a shift gone wrong in '98, a Warden caught halfway between forms during a flash flood. She'd read it twice. She'd initialed the verification line. But the boy's face — freckles across the nose, a scar on his chin from the bicycle accident at twelve — had gone soft at the edges, like a photograph left in sunlight.

*First search. Summer I turned fourteen. Mrs. Gable's wedding ring, lost in the compost heap.*

The memory should have been crisp. The smell of rotting vegetables and copper. The way her gift had pulled her toward the gleam like a magnet, the satisfaction of brushing soil from gold. The memory she'd paid: her grandmother's hands kneading bread dough on a Tuesday afternoon, the flour dusting the veins. A fair trade. She'd thought so then.

Now the compost heap felt like something she'd read about. The ring's return — Mrs. Gable's tears, the pie she'd brought over — existed as fact without feeling.

"Wren?"

She blinked. Theo stood in the doorway of the workroom, a tray in his hands. Two mugs, steam curling. "You've been at the Warden section for three hours. Yusuf said the Menders are sending someone by noon."

She set the pen down. "Right. The Menders." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Did you — the cross-referencing on the flood accounts?"

"Dev's handling it." Theo set the tray on the clear corner of the table. "He's been at it since seven. Says the substation logs from the county line match the valley's weather records within a four-minute window. Which means the Menders' timeline for the '98 flood holds."

Wren wrapped her hands around the mug. The ceramic was warm, grounding. "Dev's good at that. The procedural side."

"He's thorough. Also he doesn't sleep, apparently." Theo pulled out the chair opposite her, sat. "Priya texted me at six. Said he'd already been in the evidence room two hours. She threatened to handcuff him to the couch if he wasn't home by eight tonight."

A smile tugged at Wren's mouth, small but real. "That sounds like Priya."

"Sounds like someone who knows her brother." Theo blew on his coffee. "The Callers sent Eloise. She's in the front room with the lineage ledgers. Wardens sent Tomas — you remember Tomas, right? The one with the —"

"Missing finger. Left hand. Bear trap when he was nineteen." The words came automatically. *Tomas. Warden. Left hand. Bear trap. Nineteen.* She filed them away deliberately. "He verified the shift accounts yesterday. Said the flood testimony matches what his uncle told him."

"Good. That's good." Theo watched her over his mug. "You okay?"

The question was simple. The answer was not. "I'm fine. Just — a lot of material. Six lineages, fifty years of renewals, every account needs two witnesses minimum." She gestured at the stacks. "Drake's version has gaps. We knew that. But the gaps are specific. He has the *what* without the *why*. The terms without the context."

"Which is why we're building the *why*." Theo's voice was steady. "Lineage by lineage. Witness by witness. Public record before he can weaponize the partial."

"I know. I know the strategy." She drank coffee, letting the bitterness settle. "It's not the strategy. It's the —" She stopped. The boy's face. The compost heap. Her grandmother's hands. *Flour on the veins.* "Never mind."

Theo didn't press. He never did. That was part of what made him — what made this — workable. He'd asked for the truth in Millbrook, gotten stonewalled, and still showed up here with his notebooks and his patience and his grandmother's missing decade sitting quiet in his chest.

The front door opened. Footsteps on the pine floor. A voice, familiar and brisk: "— told you, the temperature logs from the substation don't lie. If the Menders say the fever broke at midnight, the substation says the cooling systems kicked in at 11:56. Four minutes. That's not a discrepancy, that's confirmation."

Dev Nair walked into the workroom like he owned it, which in a sense he did — his fingerprints were on every piece of evidence architecture in the valley. He wore the same worn flannel he'd worn yesterday, and the day before, sleeves rolled to elbows. A smudge of ink marked his jaw.

He stopped when he saw them. "Oh. You're both here. Good." He crossed to the table, pulled out a folder thick with printouts. "Menders' fever accounts from '03 through '12. Cross-referenced with county hospital admission records — redacted, obviously, but the dates match. The Weathers' storm logs from the same period align with NOAA data within acceptable margins." He tapped the folder. "Eloise is verifying the Caller sections now. She says the calm-compulsion records are consistent with what she remembers from her mother's notes."

Wren stared at the folder. *Dev. Finder. Priya's brother. Substation evidence work. Book 4: his procedural evidence work identified the exact gap in Drake's account.* The facts sat in her mind, labeled and shelved. But the *feeling* of them — the night Dev had shown up at her grandmother's door, rain-soaked and desperate, the weight of what he'd done for Caleb pressing against his ribs — that felt distant. Like watching a movie of someone else's life.

"Wren?"

She looked up. Dev's expression had shifted. The procedural mask cracked, showing something warmer underneath. Concern, maybe. Or recognition.

"You're doing that thing," he said quietly. "Where you stare at a file like it's a crime scene and you're the detective who hasn't slept in three days."

Theo snorted softly. "That's not just a Wren thing. That's a researcher thing. I do it. Priya does it. Half the county clerk's office does it."

"Maybe." Dev didn't look at Theo. "But you're not just reading, Wren. You're checking. Yourself. Against the record."

The air in the room changed. Pressure dropping before a storm.

"I check everything," she said. "That's the job."

"Yeah." Dev set his hand on the folder, not opening it. "But you're checking *you*. The first search. The ring. Mrs. Gable's pie." He held her gaze. "I know because I do it too. Every Finder does. You use the gift, you pay the toll, and then you spend the rest of your life wondering what you gave away that you can't even name anymore."

The words landed in the space between her ribs. *The first search. The ring. Mrs. Gable's pie.* She could see the words written in her own handwriting in the Archive's Finder ledger. Entry one. June 14, 2017. *Cost: memory of grandmother's hands kneading dough, Tuesday afternoons.*

But the hands were gone. The flour. The Tuesday light through the kitchen window. Just the entry remained, proof of a transaction she couldn't feel.

"How do you —" She stopped. Swallowed. "How do you keep going? Knowing that?"

Dev's mouth twisted. "I don't know that I do. I just know that if I stop, the only thing left is the ledger. And the ledger doesn't care about the flour." He glanced at Theo, then back. "Your grandmother's name was Adelaide. She taught you to track deer behind the house. The boy was named Jamie. He moved to Portland in 2019. He's a carpenter now. Married. Two kids."

Wren's breath caught. *Jamie. Portland. Carpenter. Two kids.* The facts slotted into place, but the image — freckles, scar, bicycle — remained blurred.

"You remember that," she said. "You remember *my* first search details."

"I remember *mine*." Dev's voice dropped. "My first search was my mother's wedding ring. Lost in the garden. I was eleven. I found it in the tomato plants. The cost was my father's laugh — the real one, not the polite one. The one that started in his shoulders." He paused. "I remember the laugh. I remember the tomatoes. I *don't* remember the garden. The smell of dirt. The way the sun hit the marigolds. That's gone. Eleven years old and I traded a garden for a ring." His jaw tightened. "So yeah. I know your first search. Because I've memorized mine. Because if I don't hold the facts, there's nothing left to hold."

Theo set his mug down slowly. "That's the cost. The ledger entry without the life."

"It's the bargain," Dev said. "Not the valley's bargain. Ours. Finders' bargain. We trade the search for the finding. Every time." He looked at Wren. "You're not losing your mind. You're paying the price. The question is whether the price is worth what you're finding *for*."

The question hung. Outside, a car door slammed — the Menders' representative arriving, probably. Elias, if the schedule held. Middle-aged, gentle hands, a scar from the mill accident that had taken his brother.

Wren looked at her hands. The same hands that had brushed soil from gold. The same hands that had held Adelaide's letter, Whitlock's letter, the ring ritual's cooling ash. *What am I finding for?*

"Drake has the ledger entries," she said slowly. "He has the dates, the names, the terms renewed. He has the *what*. Fifty years of renewals. Denise's name on every one since '73. Caleb's two years. The split-weight ritual. The ring of ten." She met Dev's eyes. "He doesn't have the flour. He doesn't have the garden. He doesn't have the laugh."

"No," Dev agreed. "He has the administrative record. The bureaucracy of memory. Which is dangerous — bureaucracy looks like truth to people who don't know better."

"But it's not the *whole* truth," Theo said. The words came quiet, certain. "That's what we're building. The whole truth. The context. The *why*."

Wren felt something shift in her chest. Not resolution — too early for that. But alignment. The work had purpose beyond the race. Beyond Drake. *We're building the why.*

"Okay," she said. "Okay. The Menders. Elias. Fever accounts." She reached for the folder Dev had brought. "Let's verify the cross-references."

Dev didn't move. "Wren."

She paused, fingers on the folder's edge.

"Your grandmother's hands," he said. "The flour. Tuesday afternoons. You wrote it down. In the ledger. Entry one." His voice softened. "You wrote it down *because* you knew you'd forget. You wrote it down so the fact would survive the forgetting."

She stared at him. *I wrote it down. I wrote it down so the fact would survive.*

"Did I?" she whispered.

"You did." Dev tapped the folder. "I read it last week. When I was checking the Finder ledger against the substation logs. Verifying the lineage records for the Archive opening." He held her gaze. "You wrote: *Cost: memory of grandmother's hands kneading dough, Tuesday afternoons. Worth it. Worth it. Worth it.* Three times. You underlined it."

The words bloomed behind her eyes. *Worth it. Worth it. Worth it.* Her handwriting. Her conviction. A message from her past self to her future self — *this price, this purpose, this choice.*

The pressure in her chest eased. Not gone. But shaped. Named.

"Three times," she repeated. "Underlined."

"Yeah." Dev finally released the folder, pushing it toward her. "So when you check yourself against the record — check that part too. The part that said it was worth it."

She picked up the folder. Her fingers trembled, barely. "Right. The Menders. Fever accounts. Let's — let's do the work."

Theo rose. "I'll get Elias settled in the front room. Eloise is almost done with the Caller sections. Tomas is waiting on the Warden addendum for the '98 flood." He paused at the door, looking back. "Coffee's good. Drink it while it's hot."

He left. The door swung shut.

Dev exhaled, long and slow. "He's good for you. The waiting. The not-pushing."

Wren opened the folder. Pages of typed logs, handwritten annotations in margins. *Menders' fever accounts. Substation cross-references. NOAA alignments.* The work. The *why*.

"He reminds me of someone," she said quietly.

"Who?"

"Ambrose Whitlock. In the letters." She didn't look up. "Not the Warden of the first debt. The man who tried to end the bargain and failed. The one who wrote: *Tell them the truth. All of it. Even the parts that hurt. Especially those.*" She traced a line of text. "Theo doesn't have the gift. He can't compel calm. But he waits like someone who knows the truth takes time to speak."

Dev was quiet a moment. "Whitlock hid his letter. Buried it. His family buried it deeper." His voice roughened. "You're not burying yours. You're publishing it. That's different."

"Is it?"

"Yeah." Dev stood. "Because you're doing it *with* people. Not *for* them. Not *at* them. *With*." He jerked his chin toward the door. "Theo. Me. Priya. The lineage reps. The whole damn valley, eventually. You're building the record *with* us. Whitlock tried to carry it alone."

The distinction settled. *With. Not for. Not at. With.*

"Dev."

He paused at the door.

"Your father's laugh. The real one." She met his eyes. "You said you remember it. The way it started in his shoulders."

Dev's face did something complex. "Yeah. I do."

"Can you —" She stopped. *Can you describe it? Can you give me the words so I can write them down for you? So the fact survives the forgetting?*

But that wasn't how it worked. The gift didn't work backward. She couldn't find what he'd lost. She could only find what *existed* — lost things, lost people, lost truths waiting in the dark.

"Can I what?" Dev asked gently.

Wren swallowed. "Nothing. Never mind. Go. Elias is waiting. The Mender fever accounts need verifying."

Dev studied her. Then he nodded, once. "Yeah. Okay. Fever accounts. Substation logs. NOAA data." He smiled, crooked and tired. "Worth it. Worth it. Worth it."

He left.

Wren sat alone in the workroom. Dust motes danced in the slanted light. The folder lay open before her — Menders' fever accounts, substation cross-references, the careful architecture of truth Dev had built.

She picked up her pen. Turned to a fresh page in her notebook.

*Entry: Dev Nair. Finder. First search: mother's wedding ring, tomato plants, age eleven. Cost: father's laugh (real one, starting in shoulders). Remembers the laugh. Remembers the tomatoes. Does not remember the garden (smell of dirt, sun on marigolds). Notes: procedural evidence work identified gap in Drake's account. Substation logs. Four-minute window. "If I don't hold the facts, there's nothing left to hold."*

She wrote it all down. Every word. The facts. The context. The *why*.

Her hand didn't shake.

Outside, voices rose — Elias's gentle cadence, Eloise's precise tones, Tomas's rough humor. The lineages arriving, one by one, to verify their sections. To witness the record. To say: *This happened. This cost. This mattered.*

Wren drank her coffee. It had gone cold.

She didn't mind.

The boy's name was Jamie. He lived in Portland. He was a carpenter. He had two kids.

She wrote that down too.
