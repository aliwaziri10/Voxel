The archive room smelled of paper dust and the cold of things kept too long in darkness. Wren had spent three days learning that scent, learning the rhythm of Theo's breathing across the table, learning which boxes he pulled first and which he left for the afternoon slant of light through the high windows.

He worked without music. Without conversation, mostly. Just the scratch of his pencil on index cards, the soft *thup* of pages turned, the occasional creak of his chair when he shifted weight. She had expected to hate the silence. Instead she found herself matching her pace to his, slowing her own cataloguing until their page-turns fell into something like counterpoint.

*Box twelve. Correspondence, 1890 to 1912. Adelaide's handwriting, then her daughter's, then a gap where no one wrote anything down at all.*

Wren pulled the next folder. Her fingers knew the weight of it before her eyes confirmed it — too light. The string tie lay loose, the flap folded back on itself. Inside: three letters, a receipt for seed corn, and a blank space where a fourth document should have lived. The impression of its edges ghosted against the adjacent pages, a rectangle of slightly lighter paper fibers.

She stopped breathing.

"Theo."

He didn't look up immediately. Finished his notation. Capped his pencil. Then turned, the movement deliberate, patient in a way that still made her want to shake him sometimes.

"What did you find?"

She slid the folder across the table. Watched his eyes scan the contents, the empty space, the ghost rectangle. His expression didn't change — not the way Mara's would have, not the way Caleb's would have, not the way Priya's would have with that instant sharpening. Theo's face simply *collected* information. Filed it.

"Folio missing," he said. "Not removed. Never filed."

"How can you tell?"

"The string tie. It's the original. Never been cut and retied." He lifted the folder, turned it over. "And the dust pattern. See here?" He traced a finger along the inner spine. "Undisturbed. Whatever wasn't in this folder was never in this folder."

Wren exhaled. "So the gap is contemporary with the letters."

"Appears so." He set the folder down. "Which means someone decided, in 1903 or thereabouts, that whatever this was shouldn't enter the record. Not that it was removed later. That it was *withheld*."

The distinction settled in her chest, cold and familiar. Withheld. Not stolen. Not lost. *Decided against.*

"Can you tell what it was?"

Theo shook his head. "No reference in the adjacent letters. No cross-index in Adelaide's ledger — she was thorough about cross-indexing. This omission was intentional enough to extend to her own finding aids."

"Of course it was." The words came out flatter than she intended. "Because why would anything about this valley ever be straightforward?"

Theo's gaze sharpened. Just slightly. The way a needle moves on a compass. "You expected otherwise?"

"I expected —" She stopped. Started again. "I grew up here. My whole life, people told me things were for my own good. The bargain. The cost. The reason my aunt stopped speaking to my mother. The reason my cousin left and never came back. Everything was *for my own good* until the moment it wasn't, and then suddenly I was supposed to understand everything instantly, because now I was old enough to carry it."

She hadn't meant to say that much. The words had simply climbed out of her throat while she wasn't watching.

Theo didn't nod. Didn't offer the small sympathetic sounds she'd learned to dread. He just watched her, pencil loose in his fingers, waiting.

"The folio," he said. "What do you think was in it?"

Wren looked back at the empty space. The ghost rectangle. "The original terms. The actual bargain. Not the version Adelaide wrote down, not the version the council voted on in '23. The *real* one. The one Ambrose Whitlock held."

"Whitlock." Theo wrote the name on a fresh index card. "The Warden of the first debt."

"Who tried to end it. Failed. Hid the complete terms instead of destroying them." She recited the history like a lesson, like armor. "His family buried the warning. Dev is his descendant. Drake has a partial record he's threatening to publish. We need the complete version first."

"Right." Theo capped his pencil. "So we find the missing folio. Or we prove it never existed. Or we reconstruct what it had to say from everything around it."

"Or?" Wren pressed.

"Or we accept that the gap *is* the record. That the withholding itself tells us something about the terms." He stood, stretched — a long, economical unfolding of limbs. "I'm going to check the council minutes from 1903. See if there's a reference to a document withheld, a motion to seal, anything that admits the gap exists."

He walked out before she could answer. The door swung shut behind him.

Wren stared at the empty folder. At the ghost rectangle.

*Withheld. Not stolen. Not lost. Decided against.*

---

Her mother had kept a jar on the kitchen shelf. Blue glass, wide-mouthed, sealed with wax. Wren had asked about it every year on her birthday, from the age of six to the age of fourteen. *What's in the jar, Mama? When can I see?*

*When you're old enough, Wren. It's for your own good.*

She'd dreamed about that jar. Gold coins. A lock of hair. A map. A letter from her father, whoever he was. The deed to a house in the city. A curse written on parchment. The true name of the valley's protection.

On her fifteenth birthday, her mother had handed her the jar. No ceremony. No explanation. Just the blue glass in her palms, the wax seal unbroken.

Inside: a single dried flower. White, fragile, petals curled inward like a fist.

*It's a memory,* her mother had said, not meeting her eyes. *Your grandmother's. The day she paid the toll for your cousin's finding. She gave up the memory of her own wedding day so your cousin could find her way home. I kept this flower from the bouquet. So someone would remember what she gave.*

Wren had stared at the flower. At her mother's face. At the kitchen she'd known her whole life, suddenly strange and full of hollows.

*You kept a flower,* she'd said. *You didn't tell me. You didn't tell her daughter. You let her think her mother forgot her wedding day for no reason.*

*It was for your own good,* her mother had repeated. *You were too young to understand the cost.*

*And now?*

*Now you're a Finder. Now you'll understand every time you search. Every memory you lose. That's the gift. That's the bargain.*

The jar still sat on Wren's own kitchen shelf. She'd never opened it. Never broken the wax. The flower had crumbled to dust years ago, she knew, but she couldn't bring herself to confirm it.

---

Theo returned forty minutes later with a bound volume under one arm and a look that Wren was learning to read: *found something, not what I hoped, but something.*

"Council minutes, March 1903." He set the volume down, opened it to a page marked with a slip of archival paper. "There's a motion. Not to seal a document. To *acknowledge* one."

He read aloud: "'Councilor Whitlock moves that the original terms of the valley's protection, as conveyed by the Warden of the First Debt, be recognized as binding upon all current and future signatories, but not entered into the public record. The terms are to be held by the sitting Warden alone, transmitted only upon succession. Motion carried, four to one. Dissent: Councilor Reyes.'"

Wren leaned over his shoulder. "Reyes. Caleb's line."

"Your line too, probably. Everyone's line, if you go back far enough." Theo turned the page. "Next entry, April 1903. 'Councilor Whitlock reports that the Warden's ledger, containing the original terms, has been misplaced. A search is authorized. Councilor Reyes objects that a search implies the ledger exists to be found. Motion to search carried, three to two.'"

"Misplaced." Wren tasted the word. "Not lost. Not destroyed. *Misplaced*."

"June 1903." Theo's finger traced down the page. "'Search concludes without result. Councilor Whitlock proposes that the original terms be reconstructed from memory and witness testimony. Councilor Reyes objects that reconstruction is not preservation. Motion to reconstruct fails, two to three.'"

"So they didn't reconstruct it. They just... stopped looking."

"September 1903." Theo's voice flattened. "'Councilor Whitlock announces that the Warden's role will pass to his eldest daughter upon his death, along with whatever fragments of the original terms remain in the family's keeping. No further motions regarding the original terms are entertained.'"

The silence stretched. The archive's cold settled deeper.

"Whitlock's daughter," Wren said. "That's the line Dev descends from."

"Through his mother's side. Yes." Theo closed the volume. "So the original terms existed. Were acknowledged as binding. Were withheld from the public record by design. Then the physical ledger was 'misplaced.' The council voted not to reconstruct. And the Warden's role — with whatever fragments remained — passed into the Whitlock family."

"Which means Dev's family has pieces. Or had pieces. Or knows where pieces are."

"Which means," Theo corrected gently, "that the missing folio from Adelaide's correspondence might be one of those pieces. Or a reference to them. Or a letter *from* Whitlock to Adelaide about them."

"Or a letter *from* Adelaide to Whitlock refusing to hand them over." Wren stood, began pacing the narrow aisle between shelves. "She was the only one who voted against withholding. The only one who objected to reconstruction failing. She *knew*."

"Adelaide Voss." Theo wrote the name on his index card. "Mara's great-grandmother. Your ancestor too, probably."

"Everyone's ancestor." Wren stopped at the window. The valley spread below, ordinary in afternoon light. Roofs and trees and the orchard's neat rows. The river's silver thread. "That's the problem. We're all descended from the people who made this bargain. Or the people who voted to hide it. Or the people who *were* hidden from."

She pressed her palm against the cold glass.

"Theo. When you found out about your grandmother — the missing decade — did anyone tell you it was for your own good?"

Theo was quiet long enough that she turned. His face had gone still in a different way. Not the filing stillness. Something older.

"No one told me anything," he said. "That was the point. My grandmother vanished from the record in 1962. Reappeared in 1972. Ten years. No explanation. My mother refused to speak of it. My father said it wasn't our business. The town clerk said the records were sealed. The county archivist said there was nothing to find."

"And you became an archivist."

"I became the person who *finds*." His voice dropped. "Because no one would give me the answer. So I learned to take it from the paper. From the gaps. From what wasn't said."

Wren felt a shift in her chest. A recognition. The same hollow, differently shaped.

"My mother gave me a jar with a dead flower in it," she said. "And told me it was for my own good. Theo, the missing folio — it's not just a document. It's the same shape. The same withholding. Adelaide knew. Caleb's missing two years — Denise took them, *for his own good*, she said, *for the valley's good*. And when we got them back, there was still a gap. A space where the memory of *how* the bargain worked should have been. The original terms. The real cost."

She turned back to the table. To the ghost rectangle.

"The missing folio mirrors Caleb's gap. Exactly. The same shape of silence. The same decision: *you don't need to know this. It's for your own good.*"

Theo didn't speak. He didn't need to. His pencil moved, *scratch, scratch*, capturing her words on a fresh card.

*Missing folio = gap in Caleb's restored memory = shape of withholding.*

"Okay," he said when he finished. "So we have three gaps. The folio. Caleb's memory. Your grandmother's wedding day. All the same shape. All decided by someone else."

"Four gaps," Wren corrected. "Your grandmother's decade."

Theo's pencil paused. "That one's different. I don't know who decided it. Or why. Or if it was a bargain at all."

"Yet." The word surprised her. "We'll find it. You've been finding it for twenty years. I've been finding things for —" She did the math. "Six years, properly. Since my gift settled. But my whole life, really. Finding the things people hid from me."

"Finding, or being handed?"

The question landed somewhere behind her ribs. She didn't answer immediately. Let it sit in the cold air between them.

"Both," she said finally. "Mostly handed. Until recently. Until —" She stopped. *Until you. Until this project. Until Drake forced the timeline.*

"Until?" Theo prompted.

"Until I stopped waiting to be old enough." She met his eyes. "There is no old enough. There's only the decision to look."

Theo held her gaze. Something passed between them — not the spark she'd read about in novels, not the lightning strike. Something quieter. The click of two gears engaging. The recognition of a shared method.

"Then let's look," he said. "At the Whitlock family papers. If the Warden's role passed to Whitlock's daughter, and Dev descends from that line —"

"Dev has the substation evidence work now. Since Book 3." Wren caught herself. "Since the ring ritual. He's been cataloguing the physical evidence from the old substation site. Fragments of the original infrastructure. If the ledger was *anywhere* —"

"It would have surfaced in his evidence review." Theo was already moving, pulling a fresh box toward him. "Box twenty-three. Substation artifacts. I saw the inventory. There's a tin box, sealed, marked 'Warden — Do Not Open.'"

Wren crossed the room in three strides. "Why didn't you lead with that?"

"I was being thorough. Cataloguing in order." A ghost of something — not quite a smile — touched his mouth. "Also I wanted to see if you'd reach the same conclusion without me pointing."

She stared at him. At the pencil still loose in his fingers. At the index cards spread in neat rows, each one a piece of the valley's truth captured in his precise script.

"You're infuriating," she said.

"I've been told." He stood, gesturing at the door. "Come on. The substation evidence is in the basement of the municipal building. Dev gave me the key yesterday."

"You have a key to the municipal building basement."

"I'm a records investigator. I have keys to *many* basements."

She laughed — surprised herself with it, a real laugh, not the tight bark she'd been producing for months. "Of course you do."

They walked out together. The archive door swung shut behind them, and for the first time in three days, Wren didn't feel the cold following her.

---

The municipal building basement smelled of concrete and groundwater and the must of things buried too long. A single bare bulb hung from the ceiling, casting long shadows across metal shelving units crowded with boxes, crates, and the occasional inexplicable object: a bicycle wheel, a child's sled, a trunk bound with rusted iron straps.

"Box twenty-three," Theo said, consulting his notebook. "Third shelf, left side. Tin box, sealed, marked 'Warden — Do Not Open.'"

Wren found it immediately. Her Finder's gift hummed at her fingertips, a low vibration like a tuning fork struck against bone. *Here. This one. This is the one.*

She didn't say that part aloud. Just pulled the box down, set it on the nearest clear surface. The tin was rectangular, about the size of a shoebox, its lid sealed with a strip of lead pressed into the seam. The label had been stamped into the metal: *WARDEN — DO NOT OPEN. A. WHITLOCK. 1903.*

"Ambrose Whitlock," Theo said. "His own hand."

"Or his clerk's. His wife's. His daughter's." Wren traced the letters with her thumb. "Sealed in 1903. The same year the ledger was 'misplaced.' The same year the council voted not to reconstruct."

"Coincidence?"

"In this valley? There's no such thing."

She reached for the lead seal. Her fingers trembled — just slightly, just enough that she noticed. Theo didn't reach to help. Didn't offer to do it for her. Just waited, pencil ready, notebook open.

*This is it,* she thought. *The missing folio. The original terms. The thing Drake has a partial version of, the thing Whitlock hid, the thing Adelaide fought to keep hidden and failed.*

*This is the truth. The whole truth. The cost.*

The lead seal gave way with a soft crunch. The lid lifted.

Inside: a single sheet of paper, folded twice. No ledger. No folio. No elaborate document.

Just one page. Handwritten in faded ink, the script careful and cramped.

Wren unfolded it. Read.

*To whoever inherits this role:*

*I tried to end it. I failed. The bargain cannot be ended — only renewed, or broken. If broken, the valley dies. If renewed, the cost continues. There is no third option. I have hidden the complete terms because knowing them changes nothing. The cost is memory. The protection is real. The choice is only ever: whose memory pays.*

*Do not seek to end it. Seek only to make the choosing fair.*

*Ambrose Whitlock. Warden of the First Debt. 1903.*

That was it. The whole thing.

Wren read it three times. Four. The words didn't change.

"That's not the original terms," she said finally. Her voice sounded strange. Hollow. "That's a *letter*. A warning. He didn't hide the terms. He hid *this*."

Theo took the paper from her fingers. Read it himself. His face didn't change — the filing expression — but his pencil snapped against the notebook page.

"Son of a bitch," he said quietly.

"What?"

"He didn't hide the terms. He *became* the terms. The Warden of the First Debt — it's not a bloodline. It's a role. He's saying the role *is* the choice. The choosing. Who pays."

Wren stared at him. "The Warden decides whose memory pays."

"Every renewal. The Warden chooses. That's the 'administrative terms' Drake wants. Control of the choosing." Theo set the paper down carefully. "Whitlock tried to end the bargain. Couldn't. So he hid the truth — that the bargain *requires* a chooser. And he made sure the choosing would stay in his family line. His daughter. Her descendants. Dev."

"Dev." Wren's breath caught. "Dev is the current Warden. He doesn't know. He *can't* know — he was in prison during the ring ritual, he's been doing substation evidence work, he hasn't had time to —"

"To realize he's the one who decides who pays the debt." Theo's voice was flat. "Every time a Finder runs a debt. Every time a Mender heals beyond their limit. Every time any gift runs a toll — Dev chooses whose memory covers it. Or the role passes to the next Whitlock descendant."

"Which would be —"

"Dev's children. If he has any. Or his sister's." Theo's eyes met hers. "Priya. Or Priya's children."

The basement felt smaller. The air thicker.

"Drake knows," Wren said. "He has a partial record. He knows about the Warden role. He wants control of the *choosing* — not to destroy the bargain, to *administer* it. To decide who pays."

"Which means he needs Dev. Or Priya. Or the role transferred to him somehow." Theo was already pulling out his phone. "I need to call Yusuf. And Priya. And Dev. Now."

Wren caught his wrist. "Wait."

Theo stopped. The phone hovered in his hand.

"We don't know the full shape yet," she said. "We have Whitlock's letter. We have the council minutes. We have the missing folio that *wasn't* a folio. We have Caleb's gap. We have your grandmother's decade. We have my mother's jar." She breathed. "We need to understand the *mechanism*. How the choosing works. Whether Dev has been choosing unknowingly. Whether the ring ritual affected the Warden role. Whether —"

"Theo." She used his first name deliberately. "If we call everyone now, we panic the valley. Drake *wants* panic. He wants the council to hand him the choosing out of fear. Yusuf backs publication — but he needs the *complete* record to publish. Not fragments. Not panic."

Theo stared at her. The filing expression cracked — just slightly. Something warmer showed through.

"You're thinking like a publisher," he said.

"I'm thinking like a Finder." She released his wrist. "We find the rest. The mechanism. The history of every choosing since 1903. Every debt paid. Every memory taken. *Then* we publish. All of it. At once. Before Drake can."

"And if Drake publishes first?"

"Then we publish anyway. The truth doesn't expire." She picked up Whitlock's letter, folded it carefully, returned it to the tin box. "But we need the *whole* truth. Including the parts we don't like. Including the parts that implicate people we love."

Theo pocketed his phone. "Dev's evidence work. The substation artifacts. There might be more in this box — or in the others. Records of past choosings. A ledger of debts paid."

"Let's catalog the rest of the box," Wren said. "Then the next. Then the next. However long it takes."

"However long it takes," Theo agreed.

He pulled the next box toward him. She pulled the one beside it. The bare bulb flickered overhead.

*Working proximity,* she thought, the phrase surfacing from somewhere — a book she'd read, a conversation she'd overheard. *Incidental competence becoming visible to each other.*

She watched Theo's hands. Steady. Precise. Respectful of the paper, the dust, the history.

*This,* she thought. *This is not for my own good. This is for the truth.*

And for the first time in her life, she knew the difference.
