The county courthouse in Millbrook smelled of floor wax and old paper, the same scent that had greeted Theo every Tuesday for the past year when he drove up from the valley to sit in the records room. Today the room held more people than the fire code probably allowed. Yusuf sat in the front row beside a woman Theo had never met but recognized from photographs — Adelaide's daughter, the one who had inherited the letters. Priya stood near the door with her arms crossed, her uniform crisp, her face unreadable. Dev sat two rows back, a stack of folders on his lap. Wren sat beside Theo, her notebook closed, her pen resting on top of it. She had not opened it once since they walked in.

Corwin Drake stood at the podium where the county clerk usually presided. He wore a charcoal suit that cost more than Theo's car, and his hair was trimmed to a neatness that suggested a barber visited his home. He did not look like a man who had spent nineteen years chasing a ghost through county archives and forgotten ledgers. He looked like a man who had found what he was looking for and was now negotiating the price.

"Mr. Marsh," Drake said. His voice carried without amplification. "You've had the complete file for three weeks. Your investigator's credentials gave you access to the restricted index. The standing arrangement notation. The gap in the 1984 renewal. You know what the valley is. You know what it costs. And you know what happens if I release my version first."

Theo did not stand. He did not look at Wren. He kept his eyes on Drake.

"I know what you have," Theo said. "A partial record. Seventeen renewals documented from the outside. Names, dates, the memories taken. You have the ledger pages Ambrose Whitlock hid in his family's possession. You have the correspondence between Whitlock and Adelaide Reyes. You have the county index entries I helped you cross-reference before I understood what they meant. What you don't have is the context. The reasons. The choice."

"Context is what people use to excuse the inexcusable," Drake said. "The valley trades memory for protection. That's the headline. That's what the county board will see. That's what the state investigators will see when I hand them the file next Monday. Unless — "

"Unless what?" Theo asked.

Drake smiled. It was a thin expression, the kind that belonged in a boardroom. "Unless you deliver the complete original terms. The bargain's administrative structure. The mechanism. The names of every current Warden and Finder and Caller. The operational details that would let me — let us — assume the agreement ourselves. A clean transfer. No one needs to know the valley exists. The protection continues. The debt continues. Only the management changes."

The room was very quiet. Theo heard the hum of the building's ancient radiator. He heard Wren's breath, steady and slow.

"And if I refuse," Theo said.

"Then I publish on Monday," Drake said. "My version. The damning version. The one that says Amity Falls is a cult that steals memories from its own children. The one that triggers a state investigation, a federal inquiry, a media circus that will destroy every life in that valley before the week is out. Your grandmother's name will be in the first paragraph. Harriet Marsh. Memory loss, 1988 to 1998. Cause unknown. The county's own case file references an 'unexplained neurological event.' I have the medical records. I have the police report from the night she was found wandering Route 9 with no memory of the previous decade. I have the doctor's note suggesting environmental exposure. I have the sealed testimony from the investigator who quit two weeks later and moved to Arizona. All of it fits a pattern. My pattern."

Theo's hands rested on his knees. He had read those documents. He had held his grandmother's medical file in his hands three weeks ago, the paper thin as tissue, the ink faded to sepia. He had sat in the Millbrook records room for six hours straight, cross-referencing Drake's timeline with the valley's renewal dates. The match was exact. Every gap in Harriet's memory aligned with a renewal Drake had documented. The valley had taken ten years from her, piece by piece, and no one had told her why. No one had told Theo why. He had built his career on the absence of that answer.

"She doesn't know," Theo said. "She can't tell you where she was. She doesn't remember the valley. She doesn't remember the bargain. She only knows she lost time."

"Which makes her the perfect witness," Drake said. "A victim who cannot defend the people who hurt her. The press loves a sympathetic victim. So do prosecutors."

Theo stood up. The chair scraped the floor, loud in the silence.

"No," Theo said.

Drake's smile faltered. "Excuse me?"

"I said no," Theo said. "I'm not giving you the terms. I'm not giving you the mechanism. I'm not giving you the names. You want control of the bargain? You don't get it. Not through me. Not through leverage. Not through threats."

"You'll let the valley burn?" Drake asked. "You'll let your grandmother's story become a headline? You spent nineteen years looking for the truth. You finally found it. And now you're going to walk away because you don't like the shape of it?"

"I spent nineteen years looking for what happened to her," Theo said. "I found it. The answer is ugly. The answer is that the valley took her memories to pay a debt she didn't agree to owe. The answer is that the people who did it believed they had no choice. The answer is that they were wrong. But the answer is also that they built something that has protected hundreds of people for generations. Children who didn't drown in floods. Families who didn't starve when crops failed. Elderly who didn't die of pneumonia in winters that should have killed them. That protection is real. The cost is real. And the choice — the choice to keep going, or to end it, or to change it — belongs to the people who live there. Not to you. Not to me. Not to any outsider with a file and a deadline."

Drake's face hardened. "You're naive. You think the world works on consent. You think a community built on a hidden bargain can survive exposure. You think the county board will care about nuances when they see 'memory theft' in a police report. By Monday morning, this room will be full of reporters. By Wednesday, the state police will be at the valley's edge. By Friday, every lineage member will be in custody. Is that what you want? Is that what you've been working for?"

"I've been working for the truth," Theo said. "The whole truth. Not your version. Not the valley's version. The truth that includes what the bargain costs and what it buys and who decided it was worth it. The truth that includes Ambrose Whitlock's letter admitting he tried to end it and failed. The truth that includes Adelaide's letter naming him as her teacher. The truth that includes the ring ritual, and Denise's fifty years, and Caleb's restored memory, and the fact that the valley found a way to distribute the weight instead of letting one person carry it all. That truth exists. I have it. Wren has it. We've been assembling it for a year."

"You have a manuscript," Drake said. "A draft. Unpublished. Unverified. My file is documented. Sourced. Dated. Cross-referenced with county records. It goes to the press Monday. Your draft goes nowhere unless I allow it."

"Then you don't understand what we've built," Theo said.

He reached into his bag and pulled out a flash drive. Small, black, unmarked. He held it up so the room could see.

"This contains the complete record," Theo said. "Every renewal. Every memory taken. Every name. Every date. Every letter. Every ledger page. The original terms as Whitlock wrote them. The administrative structure as Adelaide documented it. The lineage lists. The debt ledger. The ring ritual transcript. The medical records of every person who paid a debt, including Harriet Marsh. All of it. Sourced. Verified. Cross-referenced. Three copies exist. One with me. One with Wren. One with the county clerk's office, filed this morning under a restricted access order that expires in forty-eight hours unless a judge extends it. Which means by Monday morning, the complete true history of Amity Falls will be a matter of public record. Filed by me. Authored by the valley. Not by you."

The silence stretched. Drake stared at the flash drive. His fingers curled around the podium.

"You filed it," Drake said. "This morning."

"Eight thirty," Theo said. "Before this meeting. The clerk's office has a timestamp. The file is encrypted. The key is held by three people. Me. Wren. The county clerk. If anything happens to any of us, the key releases automatically. If you publish your version, the complete record publishes simultaneously. Side by side. The world gets both. They can compare. They can decide."

"That's blackmail," Drake said.

"That's insurance," Theo said. "You wanted leverage. You thought you had the only copy of the truth. You don't. You never did. The valley kept its own records. We just spent the last year organizing them."

Drake was quiet for a long moment. Then he laughed, short and sharp. "You think this changes anything. You think the truth protects anyone. The truth is what destroys them. You've just guaranteed the valley's exposure. You've done my work for me."

"No," Theo said. "I've done the work the valley asked me to do. A year ago, Wren came to me and asked if I would help them tell their own story. Not hide it. Not spin it. Tell it. Honestly. Completely. On their terms. I said yes. Not because I agreed with everything they've done. I don't. I said yes because the alternative — the alternative is what you're doing. Someone else telling the story. Someone with an agenda. Someone who doesn't care who gets hurt. I've seen what that looks like. I've lived with it for nineteen years. My grandmother lost ten years of her life because someone with an agenda decided the truth was a weapon. I will not be that person. And I will not help you be that person."

Drake's jaw tightened. "And the bargain? The terms? The mechanism? You're just going to put it all in the public record? The administrative structure that lets the valley function? The names of every current lineage member? You'll destroy the protection to save the truth?"

"The protection survives exposure," Theo said. "Or it doesn't. That's not my decision. That's the valley's decision. They get to make it now. With all the facts. For the first time in two hundred years, they get to choose whether the bargain continues, or ends, or changes. They get to choose together. That's what the ring ritual was about. That's what Denise's freedom was about. That's what every conversation in that valley for the past year has been about. Choice. Real choice. Informed choice. You want to take that away from them. I won't let you."

"And if they choose to end it?" Drake asked. "If the truth breaks the bargain? If the protection fails? The floods come back. The blight returns. The sickness spreads. Children die. Is that on you?"

Theo looked at Wren then. She was watching him, her face calm, her hands folded in her lap. She gave him a small nod.

"If they choose to end it," Theo said, "they choose to end it. That's their right. It's always been their right. The bargain was never a suicide pact. It was an agreement. Agreements can be renegotiated. They can be dissolved. But only by the people who made them. Not by an information broker with a deadline."

Drake studied Theo's face. Then he looked at Wren. Then at Yusuf. Then at Priya. Then at Dev. His gaze moved across the room, taking in the valley's representatives — the council chair, the deputy, the Finder who had broken the oldest taboo and lived, the woman who had carried the bargain alone for fifty years and walked out of the ring ritual alive.

"You've all decided," Drake said. "You're going to publish. All of it. The names. The costs. The mechanism. Everything."

"We are," Yusuf said. His voice was quiet but it carried. "The valley voted. Three weeks ago. Unanimous. Every adult resident. They chose transparency. They chose to trust their neighbors with the truth. They chose to stop hiding."

"Even knowing what it costs?" Drake asked. "Even knowing the world will come for you?"

"Especially knowing that," Wren said. She had stood up. Theo hadn't heard her move. She stood beside him now, close enough that he felt the warmth of her shoulder against his arm. "Hiding didn't protect us. It just meant we couldn't defend ourselves. You proved that. Your file — your partial file — would have destroyed us because we had no answer. Now we do. The answer is the truth. The whole truth. Not your selection. Not our secrets. All of it."

Drake looked between them. Something shifted in his expression — calculation, reassessment, the cold recalibration of a man watching his leverage evaporate.

"Monday," Drake said. "I still publish Monday. My file goes to the press. Your file sits in the clerk's office. The press gets my version first. By the time yours is decrypted and verified, the narrative is set. The valley is a cult. The lineages are criminals. The bargain is a crime. You understand how media works. First story wins."

"Your file is incomplete," Dev said. He had stood too, moving to stand beside Priya. "It references the 1984 renewal as a gap. It doesn't name the Finder who paid that debt. It doesn't name the Warden who authorized it. It doesn't include the ledger page showing the debt was redirected. I have that page. I found it in the substation evidence locker three months ago. It names Ambrose Kell. It names the Finder he coerced. It names the memory taken — Caleb Reyes's two years. My file includes the signed confession Kell wrote before he fled. It includes the forensic analysis proving the redirect. It includes the medical records showing Caleb's memory restoration after the ring ritual. Your file has a gap. Mine fills it. When both files publish, the gap becomes the story. Not the valley. You."

Drake's eyes narrowed. "You have the Kell confession."

"I have the original," Dev said. "Handwritten. Witnessed by Priya. Filed with the county prosecutor's office last week. Kell is a fugitive. His confession reopens the Millbrook case from nineteen years ago. The one you were tied to. The one that went cold. The prosecutor's office is very interested in new evidence."

The color drained from Drake's face. He masked it quickly, but Theo saw it. The tightening at the jaw. The flicker of something behind the eyes.

"That case was closed," Drake said. "No charges filed. Insufficient evidence."

"Insufficient evidence then," Dev said. "Sufficient evidence now. The confession names you. Not by name — Kell didn't know your name. But by description. Information broker. Partial records. Nineteen years assembling a file. The prosecutor's office has a description. They have a timeline. They have the Millbrook police report from the night Harriet Marsh was found. The officer who took the report noted a man watching from a dark sedan. License plate partial. Make and model. The DMV cross-reference yields one registered owner in the tri-state area matching that description. Corwin Drake. Age forty-seven. Address in Hartford. Business license for 'Drake Archival Services.' The prosecutor's office made the connection two days ago. They're waiting for the file publication to move forward."

Drake was very still. "You've been busy."

"We've all been busy," Priya said. "Dev's evidence work. Wren's documentation. Theo's research. My testimony. Yusuf's council records. Odette's store ledgers. Denise's journals. Mara's engineering reports. Caleb's orchard records. The valley documented itself. Thoroughly. Methodically. Legally. You came here with a threat. We answered with a record."

Drake looked at the flash drive in Theo's hand. Then at the faces around the room. The calculation returned, but it was different now — defensive, not offensive.

"If I walk away," Drake said, "if I don't publish — what happens to me?"

"Nothing," Theo said. "You walk away. Your file stays unpublished. The valley's file publishes on schedule. The prosecutor's office gets Dev's evidence. The Millbrook case reopens. You get a lawyer. You face the consequences of whatever you did nineteen years ago. Or you don't. That's between you and the law. But the valley — the valley gets to tell its own story. On its own terms. Without you."

"And if I publish anyway?"

"Then both files publish," Theo said. "Side by side. The world sees your partial record and our complete one. They see the gaps you left. They see the evidence you ignored. They see the confession you didn't know existed. They see the valley's choice to be transparent. They decide. But either way, you lose control. You lose the leverage. You lose the narrative. The only question is whether you go down fighting or you walk away now."

Drake was quiet for a long time. The radiator hummed. Someone coughed in the back row. Wren's fingers brushed Theo's wrist — light, brief, a contact that lasted less than a second but anchored him more than anything else in the room.

"You're offering me nothing," Drake said finally. "No deal. No concession. No guarantee."

"I'm offering you the truth," Theo said. "That's all I have to offer. That's all any of us have. You wanted the bargain's terms? They're in the file. You wanted the mechanism? It's in the file. You wanted the names? They're in the file. You wanted control? You don't get that. No one does. Not anymore."

Drake exhaled slowly. His shoulders dropped a fraction. When he spoke again, his voice was flatter, stripped of the performative charm.

"Monday," he said. "I'll decide by Monday."

"There is no Monday," Theo said. "The file publishes Friday. The clerk's order expires Friday at five p.m. The encryption key releases automatically. The complete record goes live on the county server, mirrored to three news outlets, two university archives, and the Library of Congress. That's the deadline. Not yours. Ours."

Drake nodded once. A short, sharp movement. "Friday. Five p.m. I'll be gone before then."

He gathered his briefcase. He did not look at any of them as he walked out. The door closed behind him with a solid thunk.

The room exhaled.

Yusuf let out a breath that sounded like a groan. He rubbed his face with both hands. "God. I thought he was going to — I didn't think — "

"He folded," Priya said. She sounded almost disappointed. "I wanted him to push. I had a whole speech prepared."

"He didn't fold," Dev said. "He recalculated. He'll try something else. Before Friday. He has to."

"Let him try," Wren said. "We've got the record. We've got the evidence. We've got the prosecutor. We've got each other. What else does he have?"

Theo looked at her. The fluorescent lights caught the bridge of her nose, the curve of her jaw, the dark fall of her hair. She looked tired. They all looked tired. But she also looked steady in a way she hadn't a year ago, or six months ago, or even three weeks ago when they had started the final assembly.

"What else does he have?" Theo repeated softly.

"Nothing that matters," Wren said. She met his eyes. "Nothing we can't answer."

The tension in Theo's chest, knotted since the day he opened the restricted index and saw the standing arrangement notation, began to loosen. Not gone. Not resolved. But loosened. The leverage had shifted. The threat had been named and met. The valley's story was no longer a weapon in someone else's hand.

"Friday at five," Theo said. "The file goes live. The Archive opens. The valley tells its truth."

"Friday at five," Wren agreed.

She reached for his hand. Her fingers curled around his, warm and dry and certain. Theo closed his hand over hers and held on.

Outside the courthouse windows, the Millbrook afternoon was gray and bright, the sky a flat silver that promised snow. The town moved on the streets below — ordinary people, ordinary lives, no memory of bargains or debts or the weight of hidden things. Theo watched them for a moment, then turned back to the room, to the people who had become his people, to the work that waited.

"Let's go home," he said.
