CHAPTER TWENTY

The council chamber hadn't been full like this since the night they'd voted, finally, to move on Denise, and Priya found herself standing at the back of the room noting the echo of it, the same rows of folding chairs, the same water-stained ceiling tile above the third row that nobody had ever gotten around to fixing, the same nervous, over-attentive hush of people who understood they were about to hear something that would change how they slept.

Yusuf stood at the front table rather than sitting behind it, which Priya had learned over the past year meant he wanted the room to see his face clearly for whatever came next. He'd aged visibly since Book Two, not in any single dramatic way, just the slow accumulation of a man who had spent a year making harder decisions than he'd ever expected his quiet, unremarkable chairmanship to require.

"Thank you all for coming on short notice," Yusuf said, once the room had settled. "I'll keep the preamble brief, because I know most of you have already heard fragments of this and deserve the full account rather than more rumor. Dev Sharma is carrying something none of us fully understand yet, inherited through the Whitlock line, the same line Adelaide named in her final letter last year. It is accelerating. Three days ago it put him in the clinic overnight. We now believe it poses a direct physical danger to his life, not only a risk to memory, and we believe a faction with generations of history in this practice wants that danger to run its course rather than be stopped."

A ripple moved through the room, the hush of people absorbing something they'd feared without quite letting themselves name it. Dev sat in the front row between Priya and Caleb, spine straight, hands folded tightly enough that his knuckles had gone pale, and Priya kept one hand resting lightly against his back the entire time, a silent reminder that he wasn't facing the room alone.

"I've asked Wren Calloway and Mara Okafor to summarize their findings," Yusuf continued. "And I've asked Denise Hallworth, with real gratitude, to be here as well, since she has lived experience none of the rest of us can offer."

Denise sat near the front on the opposite side from Dev, smaller than Priya remembered her from a year ago, her hair fully gray now, her hands folded in her lap with a stillness that looked practiced rather than natural. She caught Dev's eye once across the room and gave him a small, private nod, two people sharing a moment neither the room nor its ordinary language could fully hold.

Priya let her gaze drift over the rest of the chamber while Yusuf finished his opening remarks, cataloguing faces the way she'd learned to catalogue a room on every shift she'd ever worked, out of old habit more than any real suspicion. Farmers who'd known her father. A pair of schoolteachers who'd taught half the valley's children, including Dev. Odette near the back, her cane resting against the chair beside her, watching the proceedings with the sharp, unhurried attention of someone who'd already lived through more of this valley's secrets than anyone else in the room. And near the council table, the same handful of clerks and aides who kept the valley's ordinary business running quietly in the background of every crisis, filing paperwork nobody else wanted to touch, present at every meeting the way furniture was present, seen so often they'd become nearly invisible.

Wren went first, laying out the acceleration curve on the projector Mara had rigged from an old classroom unit, four minutes to twenty to hours to a full physical collapse, the interval collapsing each time. Mara followed with the margin she'd built, the honest admission that they didn't actually know how much runway remained beyond the eclipse date, that the body itself might now be setting its own, unknown deadline. The room absorbed both reports in the same tight, frightened silence, and Priya watched several council members glance instinctively toward Yusuf, waiting for him to tell them how afraid they were allowed to be.

It was Elias Thorne who spoke first from the council table itself, a soft-spoken records clerk who had sat on the periphery of every council meeting since long before Priya's time, always with a notepad, always documenting, the sort of quiet, careful presence nobody thought twice about because he'd simply always been there. "Chairman, if I may. Given everything Ms. Calloway just described, shouldn't we be slowing down, not speeding up? A decision this size, made under this kind of pressure, is exactly the condition under which councils make their worst mistakes."

"With respect, Elias," Yusuf said, "that's precisely my own instinct, and precisely what worries me most about following it. A year ago I hesitated on Denise's case for months, waiting for a fuller record, a cleaner consensus, and that hesitation nearly cost this valley everything. I am not eager to make the opposite mistake with Dev's life on a much shorter clock."

"Which is exactly why I'd ask you to consider the opposite risk," Elias said, mild, reasonable, jotting something in his notepad even as he spoke, the gesture so habitual nobody in the room registered it as anything but background. "Moving in haste risks its own catastrophe. I only ask that whatever is decided here be documented fully and reviewed by the full council, not rushed through on five people's judgment alone, however trusted those five have become."

"What would you have us do instead, Elias," Yusuf asked, and there was no edge in it, only genuine curiosity, the question of a chairman who valued dissent even when it slowed him down. "Concretely. Not in principle."

"A subcommittee," Elias said, without hesitation, as though he'd already turned the answer over more than once. "Three members beyond the operational five, briefed fully, empowered to raise an objection formally if something looks wrong before it becomes irreversible. Not to slow the work. Only to make certain that five people under this much strain aren't the only eyes on a decision this size. I'd volunteer to serve on it myself, given how closely I've already been tracking the record."

It was, on its face, a reasonable offer, measured and institutional in exactly the way the council existed to provide, and Priya watched several heads nod around the room in quiet agreement before she caught herself nodding along too.

"That's a fair request," Yusuf said, "and I intend to honor it. Full minutes, full record, nothing decided in a back room. But I want to be clear with everyone in this chamber: fair process and slow process are not automatically the same thing, and I will not let the second one hide inside the first one again."

The room stayed quiet a moment, the tension in it shifting rather than dissolving, and Priya found her attention snagging, briefly and without quite knowing why, on Elias's steady, unhurried pen moving across his notepad, recording every detail of Wren's acceleration curve, every number Mara had put on the board, with a thoroughness that felt, for half a second, oddly disproportionate to a man whose only stated job in the room was to keep the minutes. She set the thought aside almost as quickly as it arrived. He'd always been thorough. That was simply who Elias was.

"I want to speak plainly," Caleb said, rising from his seat beside Dev, his voice carrying the same steady, plainspoken calm he brought to every hard moment. "I understand the argument for caution. I lived through what happened when this council waited too long once already, and I don't say that to score a point against anyone in this room, only because it's true and it matters. But Dev collapsed in the town square three days ago in front of half this valley's Saturday shoppers. Whatever process we build has to move at the speed of what's actually happening to him, not at the speed that feels comfortable to the rest of us."

"Nobody's disputing the urgency, Caleb," Elias said, gentle, reasonable, entirely unthreatening. "Only the wisdom of skipping the safeguards that exist precisely for moments like this one."

"Then don't skip them," Priya said, standing now too, unable to stay seated any longer. "Build them fast instead. My brother doesn't have the luxury of a council that needs weeks to feel comfortable. He has a body that's already telling us the eclipse might not even be the real deadline. I'm not asking anyone here to abandon caution. I'm asking you to be careful quickly, because quickly is the only version of careful we can actually afford right now."

Denise spoke then, for the first time, her voice quieter than the room but somehow carrying anyway, an authority earned the hard way and unmistakable for it. "I waited fifty years to be careful," she said. "I told myself restraint was mercy, that a small, hidden taking every few years was kinder than any louder, more honest reckoning. I was wrong for most of those fifty years, and it nearly cost all of you everything when it finally broke. I would not wish my version of careful on anyone, least of all a boy who never chose any of this in the first place."

The room went very still at that, the weight of Denise's own history landing harder than any argument either side had made. Yusuf let the silence sit a moment before he spoke again, his voice steadier now, some decision visibly settling into place behind his eyes.

"Here is what we will do," he said. "Full council briefings, twice weekly, minutes kept and available to any member who requests them. Elias, I'll ask you to keep that record personally, given your experience, and I'll take your subcommittee idea under real consideration rather than dismissing it outright. But I won't stand it up tonight, and I won't let its formation become a delay in itself while we debate who sits on it. Operational decisions, what actually happens day to day to keep Dev alive and this valley safe, stay with the five people who have already proven, over the last year and the last three weeks, that they act with exactly the care this room is asking for. I will not let this council's comfort with process become the reason we move too slowly a second time."

A murmur ran through the room, not full agreement, but something closer to grudging acceptance, the sound of people choosing to trust a chairman who had already been tested once and hadn't broken. Elias nodded along with the rest, his pen still moving, his expression giving away nothing at all beyond mild, professional attentiveness, and Priya, watching him from across the room, felt the odd, fleeting unease from earlier brush past her again before she let it go a second time, filing it away without a name, the way she'd learned to file every small, unresolved thing since this began.

Afterward, in the parking lot, Dev exhaled a long breath he seemed to have been holding since the moment Yusuf first opened his mouth. "That went better than I expected," he said.

"It went as well as it could," Caleb said. "Which isn't quite the same thing. Yusuf's holding two fears at once now, moving too slow and moving too fast, and he just told an entire room he's choosing to trust five specific people over his own council's comfort. That's a real risk for him, politically and personally."

"He's right to take it," Priya said, unlocking the truck. "But Caleb's right too. We need to be worth that trust every single day until the eclipse, not just tonight."

Dev climbed into the back seat slowly, his body still not fully recovered from the collapse three days before, and watched the council building's windows go dark one by one behind them as Priya pulled out of the lot. Somewhere in that room, he thought, someone had listened to every number Wren and Mara put up on the board with more attention than the room itself seemed to notice, and though he couldn't have said why the thought crossed his mind at all, some quiet, unplaceable part of him filed it away too, the way Priya had, and said nothing.

"You're quiet back there," Caleb said, half turning from the front seat.

"Just tired," Dev said, which was true enough not to feel like a lie, even if it wasn't the whole of what sat in his chest. He thought of Elias's steady pen, of the subcommittee offer that had sounded so reasonable nobody in the room had pushed back on it, of his own instinct, small and wordless, that something about the shape of the evening had not quite matched its surface. He had no name for the feeling and no evidence to hang it on, only a faint, persistent itch at the edge of everything else he was already carrying, and for now, with the eclipse six weeks out and his own body keeping a clock none of them could read, it seemed like one worry too many to speak aloud.

Priya drove the rest of the way home in silence, watching the dark hills roll past the windshield, and none of them said what each of them, in their own separate way, had already begun quietly to wonder.
