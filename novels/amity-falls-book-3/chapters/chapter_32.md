CHAPTER THIRTY-TWO

The final candidate signed on eleven days before the eclipse, bringing the ring's number to ten, and Mara marked it on her diagram with the same careful, unhurried precision she brought to every load calculation, though Priya noticed her hand trembled slightly as she wrote the name down, the only visible crack in weeks of relentless composure.

"Ten," Wren said, reading over her shoulder. "Right in the middle of your range. Not the bare minimum, not pushing past what we can safely brief and rehearse in the time we have left."

"Ten feels right," Mara agreed. "Enough margin that no single person carries more than they can hold. Not so many that we're stretching trust or timeline past what either can bear."

The silo had transformed over the past two weeks from an empty grain building into something resembling a war room, Mara's diagrams pinned along one curved wall, Wren's research notes filling three legal pads stacked neatly on an upturned crate, a rough circle marked out on the dirt floor in chalk, ten small stakes driven into the ground at even intervals around a larger one at the center where Dev would stand. A folding table near the door held a growing collection of coffee mugs and half-eaten sandwiches, the ordinary debris of people who'd stopped noticing how long they'd been working.

They'd rehearsed the standing positions four times already, walking through the choreography without the actual weight of the ritual behind it, learning where to place their feet, how far apart to stand, what the abort signal looked and sounded like so that no one would hesitate to use it if something started going wrong.

"Again," Mara said, and the ten candidates moved into position around the circle, Odette taking her place at the northern point with the same steady calm she'd carried since the day she volunteered, a retired schoolteacher named Farrow at the eastern point still visibly nervous despite three prior rehearsals, a young man Priya barely knew named Castellan standing at the southwest point with his jaw set tight, having told the group plainly during his briefing that he was doing this because his own grandmother had lost a summer once and nobody had ever explained why.

Dev walked to the center stake and stood there, arms loose at his sides, watching the faces arranged around him with an expression that had settled, over the past two weeks, into something Priya could only describe as grave gratitude, a man fully aware of exactly what these ten people were choosing to risk on his behalf and unwilling to take a single moment of it for granted.

"From the top," Mara called, and Wren began walking through the sequence aloud, the same words she'd refined across a dozen drafts, each phrase chosen carefully to hold as much of the true mechanism as language could carry without needing the actual bargain present to test it against.

They were three-quarters through the rehearsal when Yusuf's truck pulled up outside the silo, faster than usual, gravel spraying under abrupt braking, and he came through the door with an expression that stopped the whole rehearsal cold before he'd said a single word.

"Halloran's dead," Yusuf said, without preamble. "Found this morning at the outbuilding. A splinter faction, by the look of it, someone within his own circle who disagreed with letting Dev's refusal stand unchallenged."

The silo went utterly silent, the chalk circle and its ten waiting stakes suddenly looking small and fragile against the weight of that sentence.

"How," Priya asked, her voice tight.

"I don't have full details yet," Yusuf said. "What I do have is a warning, left deliberately where it would be found, addressed to this household specifically. Whoever did this doesn't share Halloran's restraint, and doesn't intend to wait for the eclipse to try something more direct than persuasion."

Dev had gone very still at the center of the circle, the ten candidates around him exchanging uneasy glances, the rehearsal's careful choreography suddenly feeling thin against a threat that no longer bothered pretending to be gentle.

"He told us he couldn't guarantee restraint from everyone in his circle," Dev said quietly. "I didn't expect it to mean this fast, or this final."

"Neither did I," Yusuf admitted. "Which means we accelerate everything. Security around this silo, doubled again. The candidates' identities protected more carefully than we've managed so far, because whoever did this to Halloran clearly isn't interested in reasoned argument the way he was, and I won't have any of these ten people exposed to that kind of danger for having volunteered to help."

Farrow, still visibly shaken at his position along the circle's eastern edge, raised a hand slightly, an old habit from decades of classroom instruction. "Does this change anything about what we're being asked to do," he asked. "The actual ritual itself, not just the security around it."

"It might change everything about the security around it," Mara said, already thinking several steps ahead, the engineer in her reflexively reaching for the concrete problem rather than sitting with the grief of it. "It shouldn't change the mechanism. The ring still works the same way regardless of who's trying to stop us from reaching the eclipse with it intact. Our job now is making sure we actually get there, past whatever this splinter group tries next."

"Eleven days," Wren said quietly, half to herself, the number landing heavier now than it had that morning.

Caleb had moved to stand near Dev at the circle's center, close enough to be a steadying presence without crowding him, and Priya watched her brother's face carefully, bracing for the same fear that had gripped him after the orchard, the same doubt that had surfaced at two in the morning on the porch step weeks earlier.

What she saw instead surprised her. Something harder had settled into Dev's expression, not fear exactly, something closer to the same cold clarity he'd found the day Mara discovered the schedule board, a refusal to let the ground keep shifting under him without answering it directly.

"Then we finish faster," Dev said. "Not sloppier. Faster. Whatever margin we were planning to spend on extra rehearsals or extra caution, we spend it now, before whoever killed Halloran finds another chance to try something worse than persuasion on any of the ten people standing in this circle for my sake."

"That's not your call to make alone," Yusuf said, not unkindly, but firmly. "These are ten volunteers who deserve a say in how fast we move, given the new risk."

"Then ask them," Dev said, turning to face the circle fully. "All of you. You signed up knowing there might be a permanent cost to standing here. Nobody told you that cost might now include whatever this splinter group decides to try before the eclipse even arrives. If any of you want to step back, now, today, I need you to know that's allowed, and it costs you nothing in my eyes, because you already gave more than I had any right to ask by saying yes in the first place."

The silo held its breath for a long moment, ten faces turned inward toward each other and toward the man standing at the center of the chalk circle they'd all agreed to fill.

Odette spoke first, her voice carrying the same folkloric steadiness she'd brought to every conversation since volunteering. "I buried a husband and outlived two of my own children," she said. "I did not come here because I thought this would be safe. I came here because this valley needed someone old enough not to be afraid of what's left to lose. Whoever did this to Halloran doesn't change my answer. If anything, it confirms why the doctrine he led needs an alternative that doesn't end in somebody's death, willing or not."

Farrow nodded slowly beside her, some of the visible nervousness settling into something steadier. "I taught this valley's children for thirty years," he said. "I watched Denise's whole ordeal from the outside, too frightened to offer more than casseroles and quiet sympathy. I don't intend to make that same mistake twice, watching from a safe distance while someone else carries the whole weight alone."

Castellan spoke next, quieter than the others but no less certain, his jaw still set tight the way it had been since his first briefing. "My grandmother never got to know what took her summer," he said. "Nobody explained it to her, nobody stood beside her the way we're standing beside Dev. If somebody dying at that outbuilding is supposed to scare me off, it does the opposite. It tells me this matters enough that somebody was willing to kill over it, which means it's worth being willing to stand here over it too."

One by one, the rest of the circle voiced some version of the same answer, no one stepping back, though Priya noted that several voices carried a new grimness that hadn't been there in earlier rehearsals, the abstract danger of Wren's careful briefings suddenly made real and immediate by a dead man's name.

"Then we don't waste the resolve you're all showing me right now," Yusuf said, once the circle had finished speaking. "We move the timeline up. Full rehearsals daily instead of every few days. Round-the-clock security on this silo and on every one of your homes, starting tonight. And I want whatever intelligence we can gather on this splinter faction, fast, because Halloran at least gave us the courtesy of explaining himself before trying anything. I doubt whoever killed him will extend us the same patience."

The rehearsal resumed within the hour, subdued but steady, ten candidates moving through their positions with a new seriousness that hadn't been present that morning, and Dev stood at the center of it, watching each face in turn, holding onto something that Priya recognized, watching him, as the same stubborn resolve that had carried him through the porch confession, through the orchard, through Halloran's fire-lit sitting room.

That evening, after the candidates had gone home under Yusuf's newly doubled security, the core group gathered in the silo's back corner, away from the chalk circle, and Wren finally said out loud what had been sitting unspoken in the room since Yusuf's truck first pulled up.

"Whoever did this to Halloran," she said, "believes in the doctrine enough to kill their own leader for refusing to force it on Dev. That's not the same threat we've been planning against. Halloran wanted to persuade. This is someone who's already decided persuasion failed and skipped straight to whatever comes after it."

"Then we treat the eclipse itself as the real danger point," Mara said. "Not just the ritual. The location, the timing, the possibility that whoever's leading this splinter group now tries to disrupt the ring directly, rather than simply talking Dev into walking away from it."

"Eleven days," Dev said quietly, testing the number again, the way he tested every number now, turning it over to feel its true weight. "Ten people in a circle, one dead man who tried to be gentle about all this, and whoever comes next who might not bother."

"Eleven days," Caleb agreed, his hand finding Dev's shoulder in the same steady gesture he'd offered on the porch weeks ago. "And every single one of us in this room intends to spend them making sure you're still standing at the center of that circle when the eclipse actually comes, whole and choosing it, exactly the way you chose it last night."

Priya looked around the silo one more time before they locked up for the night, the chalk circle pale against the dirt floor, the ten stakes standing patient and waiting in the dim light, and found herself thinking, not for the first time, that this valley kept asking its bravest people to stand in circles they'd never wanted to need, and kept finding, somehow, enough of them willing to answer.

Outside, the evening had turned properly cold, the first true frost of the season settling silver over the silo's approach, and somewhere beyond the tree line, in a farmhouse that had lost its patient, sorrowful leader sometime in the night, something colder and less willing to wait had already begun, unseen, to move.
