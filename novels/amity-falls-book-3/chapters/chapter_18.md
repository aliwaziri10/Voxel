CHAPTER EIGHTEEN

They let Dev go to the feed store himself for the first time in nine days, a small, ordinary errand that had taken nearly an hour of careful negotiation the night before. Priya had wanted to send Caleb with him. Dev had wanted, more than he'd been willing to admit out loud, to do one normal thing alone, to stand in a checkout line and buy chicken feed and exchange three sentences of small talk with someone who had no idea his blood carried the weight of five generations of a dying practice. In the end they'd compromised. Caleb would wait in the truck outside, close enough to reach him in seconds, far enough that Dev could have this one small piece of ordinary life to himself.

Mara had drawn up the route the night before with the same care she gave any of her structural maps, marking exactly where Caleb would park, how long the walk from the truck to the store door should take, which side of the square gave the clearest line of sight back to him the whole way. Dev had teased her gently about it over dinner, telling her a trip to buy chicken feed didn't usually require a diagram, and Mara had said, without any humor at all, that she'd rather be accused of overpreparing for a grocery run than underprepared for anything else this family had faced lately. Nobody had argued the point after that.

It was going fine. That was the part Dev kept turning over afterward, in the hours and days that followed, how ordinary the whole morning had felt right up until it wasn't. The frost had burned off by midmorning, leaving the town square bright and cold and busy with the usual Saturday traffic, farmers loading trucks, a knot of children chasing each other around the fountain that hadn't run water since before Dev was born. He'd made it through the checkout line without incident, exchanged the three sentences he'd promised himself he'd manage, even smiled at something the cashier said about the early frost being hard on the last of the squash.

He was halfway back across the square, feed bag balanced against one hip, when the world tilted sideways without warning.

It didn't feel like the blackouts. That was what would frighten Priya most, later, when he tried to describe it to her. The blackouts stole time cleanly, a door closing and opening again somewhere else. This felt like his own body turning against him in real time, his vision narrowing at the edges, his legs forgetting how to hold his weight, a sound rising in his ears like static swallowing the ordinary noise of the square. He had just enough awareness left to think, distantly, that he should call out, should do something, before the ground came up to meet him and the feed bag split open across the pavement in a wide, spilled fan of grain.

Caleb was out of the truck before the bag finished spilling, cutting across the square faster than Dev had ever seen him move, shoving past a startled farmer without apology. By the time he reached him, a small crowd had already started to gather, the hush of people who sense something is genuinely wrong replacing the ordinary Saturday noise.

"Dev." Caleb dropped to his knees beside him, hands already checking his pulse, his breathing, the same fast, practiced assessment Dev had seen him use once before, the night of the fourth blackout. "Dev, can you hear me."

Dev's eyes were open, unfocused, his skin gone grey and clammy in a way that had nothing supernatural about it at all, just a young man who looked suddenly, frighteningly ill. His lips moved, no sound coming out at first, then a thin, rasping breath. "Caleb."

"I'm here. I'm right here." Caleb's voice stayed steady, though Dev could hear the strain underneath it, the same controlled fear he remembered from every crisis this family had weathered together. "Someone call the clinic," Caleb said, louder now, to the gathering crowd. "Now, please. Tell them it's urgent."

Someone ran. Dev registered it distantly, the way he registered everything in that stretch of time, sound and light arriving a half-second late, as though the world had slipped slightly out of sync with his own senses. His chest hurt in a way he couldn't place, a deep, aching pressure that had nothing to do with the fall.

"It's happening again," he managed, each word costing him more than it should have. "Not a blackout. Something else."

"I know," Caleb said. "I know, Dev. Stay with me. Just stay with me."

Priya arrived at a dead run less than four minutes later, someone having reached her by phone before the clinic van even pulled up, her face white with a fear Dev had rarely seen her wear so openly in front of strangers. She dropped down on his other side, taking his hand in both of hers, her deputy's training visibly warring with something far more personal underneath it.

"I'm here," she said. "I'm here, Dev. You're not alone."

"Hurts," Dev said, the single word costing him what felt like enormous effort. "Everywhere. Not like before."

"The clinic's two minutes out," Priya said, glancing up at Caleb, some silent, practiced communication passing between them that Dev was too far gone to fully follow. "Hold on. Just hold on a little longer."

The clinic van arrived with Mara riding in the passenger seat, having intercepted the call on her scanner and beaten half the response time getting there herself. She was out of the vehicle before it fully stopped, crouching beside the two paramedics as they worked, her structural engineer's calm somehow steadying the scene even though there was nothing here for her actual skills to fix.

"Vitals are unstable," one of the paramedics said, low, professional, a voice trained not to alarm a frightened crowd. "Heart rate's elevated, blood pressure's dropping. We need to move him now."

Dev felt himself lifted, the world tilting again in a way that had nothing to do with blacking out, every motion registering as pain now, distant and sharp all at once. He caught fragments as they loaded him into the van. Priya's hand refusing to let go of his even as the paramedics worked around her. Caleb's voice, steady, relentless, telling him to keep his eyes open, just a little longer, just until they got him somewhere safer.

The last thing he remembered clearly, before the world finally went dark for real this time, was Wren's face appearing at the van door at the last second, breathless from running the whole way from Mara's house, her expression carrying something Dev had never seen on her before. Not her usual bluntness. Not even fear, exactly. Something closer to grief, the look of someone bracing for a loss she wasn't ready to name out loud.

He woke hours later in the clinic's single overnight room, fluorescent light too bright overhead, an IV line taped into the back of his hand. Priya was asleep in the chair beside the bed, still in the same clothes from that morning, her head tipped at an angle that promised a stiff neck when she woke. Caleb stood by the window, arms crossed, watching the last of the daylight fade over the square where it had happened.

"How long," Dev asked, his voice raw and unfamiliar to his own ears.

Caleb turned, some of the tension in his shoulders easing visibly at the sound of Dev's voice. "Six hours. You gave us a scare, Dev. A real one."

"What happened."

"The clinic doctor doesn't fully know," Caleb said honestly. "Your body's under a kind of strain she's never seen before, not in any textbook, something closer to what a person's system does under extreme, sustained stress than any illness she can actually name. She's careful with her guesses. But off the record, she told Priya it looked like your body is starting to run out of room to keep absorbing whatever's building in you."

Dev absorbed that slowly, the weight of it settling somewhere deep and cold in his chest. "It's not just my mind anymore. It's actually killing me."

"We're not going to let that happen," Caleb said, quiet and fierce all at once, crossing to sit on the edge of the bed. "But I'm not going to lie to you either, not after everything we promised each other about honesty. What happened today was real, Dev. Your body is failing under this, not just your memory. That changes the shape of the timeline we're working with."

Priya stirred at the sound of their voices, waking with the sharp, immediate alertness of someone who'd trained herself never to sleep fully deep in a crisis. Her eyes found Dev's, and something in her face crumpled with relief before she caught herself, the deputy's control reasserting itself even as her hand found his again.

"You're awake," she said, voice thick with exhaustion that came from hours of fear rather than hours of sleep. "Don't you ever do that to me again."

"I didn't exactly choose to," Dev said, managing something close to a tired, wry smile.

"I know." Priya pressed her forehead briefly against the back of his hand, a rare, unguarded gesture from a woman who usually kept even her worst fear tightly contained. "I know you didn't. I'm still allowed to be furious about it."

Mara appeared in the doorway a moment later, having apparently been pacing the hallway rather than sitting, a habit Dev recognized from every hard night this family had weathered so far. "You're awake," she said, something in her usually measured voice cracking slightly at the edges. "I was starting to run out of hallway."

"Sorry," Dev said, and meant it, though the word felt absurdly small for what he'd just put all of them through.

"Don't apologize for your own body failing you," Mara said, coming to stand at the foot of the bed. "Apologize for something you actually chose. This wasn't a choice." She glanced at Caleb, some silent, practical calculation passing between them. "I already spoke to the clinic doctor myself, once she stepped out. She wants to run more tests tomorrow, see if she can find anything that actually explains what she's seeing, though she was honest that she doesn't expect to find a textbook answer. She also said, off the record, that whatever this is, it's accelerating faster than any illness she's ever tracked in a healthy young man."

"How much faster," Dev asked.

"She wouldn't put a number on it," Mara said. "I don't think she could, honestly, even if she wanted to. But I think we all felt today what that acceleration actually looks like in practice, not just on Wren's charts. That changes how we plan the next four weeks. We can't treat the eclipse as the only deadline anymore. We have to treat your body as its own deadline too, one we don't fully understand yet."

Dev absorbed that in silence for a moment, the weight of it settling somewhere he hadn't quite made room for yet. "So we might not even have four weeks."

"We might not," Mara said, refusing to soften it the way some part of him had braced for her to. "I'd rather you hear that clearly from me now than hear it too late from someone trying to protect you from it."

"I'd rather that too," Dev said quietly.

Outside the clinic window, the square had emptied out for the evening, the fountain that never ran water standing silent and dark against the last purple light of dusk. Four weeks and three days, one long, terrible day closer than yesterday's count had promised, now stood between all of them and the eclipse, and for the first time since this had started, Dev understood with total clarity that the danger wasn't only theoretical anymore, wasn't only blackouts and strangers and old family arguments about mercy. His own body was keeping its own countdown now, quietly, urgently, and none of them yet knew exactly how much time it actually had left to give him.
