CHAPTER FIVE

The county records office kept its oldest documents in a back room that smelled permanently of dust and cold stone, and Priya had spent enough hours there over the years chasing property disputes and boundary claims to know exactly which drawers held which decades without needing to ask the clerk twice.

She hadn't expected to be searching, this time, for her own family.

"Your mother's maiden name was Halloway," she said to Dev, more to herself than to him, flipping carefully through a ledger of marriage records from the 1950s. "That's what's on her headstone. That's what's on every document I've ever seen with her name attached. But headstones only record what someone chose to carve, and I'm starting to wonder how much choosing went into that name at all."

Dev sat across the narrow table, pale and quiet, his hands wrapped around a mug of the clerk's terrible coffee more for something to hold than any real desire to drink it. He'd had no blackouts since the reading of the stone two days earlier, though Mara had warned them that meant nothing, that the pattern so far showed no predictable rhythm at all, only an unmistakable trend toward worse.

"You think she changed it," Dev said. "Mom. You think Halloway wasn't always the name."

"I think our grandmother might have changed it for her, a generation earlier, before Mom was even born." Priya turned another page, moving backward through the decades with the methodical patience she'd learned from Mara, who had, months ago now, taught her without ever quite meaning to teach her that the right document, found calmly and carefully, could say more than a dozen frightened conversations.

She found it an hour later, in a ledger so old the ink had faded to something closer to brown than black, a single marriage record from nineteen forty-seven.

*Eleanor Whitlock, m. Samuel Halloway.*

Priya sat very still for a long moment, staring at the page, feeling something settle into place with the same cold, inevitable weight Mara had described feeling when the drought file first revealed Denise's name.

"Found it," she said quietly.

Dev came around the table to look, and Priya watched him read the line twice, three times, his lips moving slightly around the word Whitlock like he was testing whether saying it himself might make it feel less like something happening to a stranger.

"Eleanor," he said finally. "That's Grandma's name. Mom always said Grandma never talked about her own girlhood much. Said she'd grown up somewhere near the old orchard land, moved into town after she married, never really explained why she didn't keep in touch with whatever family she'd had before."

"Whitlock," Priya said again, the name sitting strange and heavy in her own mouth now, no longer just a name Wren had pulled out of a stone belonging to someone else's bloodline. "Our grandmother was born a Whitlock, and she married a Halloway, and somewhere in the years between, she buried that first name so thoroughly that neither of us ever heard a whisper of it growing up."

"Why would she do that." Dev's voice had gone rough. "If it was just a name, why bury it that carefully. Why not just marry and move on the way everybody does."

Priya kept flipping pages, searching now with a real hunger she hadn't felt a moment ago, and found, three pages further back, a death record dated only two years before Eleanor's marriage.

*Ambrose Whitlock, d. 1945. Cause: unrecorded.*

"Ambrose," Priya breathed. "Dev, look. Ambrose Whitlock. The same man from Adelaide's letter. He died two years before Eleanor married and changed her name." She traced the date with one finger, working through the arithmetic slowly. "If he taught Adelaide the bargain in the autumn after her mother's death, and Adelaide's letter is from decades earlier than this, then this Ambrose in our own family's record could be the same man, decades older by the time he died, or it could be a son, a nephew, someone carrying the same name forward the way families sometimes do."

"Or it could be him," Dev said quietly. "If whatever this thing is doesn't age people the normal way. If Denise proved that's possible."

Priya felt the cold in her chest deepen further. "Eleanor would have been young when he died. Young enough to still be living in that family, still close enough to whatever he carried to be marked by it somehow, even if she never chose any of it herself."

"And two years later, she marries, changes her name, and never speaks of her girlhood again." Dev sat down heavily in the chair across from her, staring at the ledger like it might rearrange itself into something less true if he looked hard enough. "She was running from something. She had to have been. You don't bury a name that completely unless you're trying to outrun it."

"Maybe she thought she succeeded," Priya said softly. "Maybe she genuinely believed, marrying Samuel Halloway and building a whole new life under a whole new name, that whatever Ambrose carried had died with him, or at least wouldn't follow her into a life she'd worked so hard to build separate from it."

"And instead it just waited." Dev's hands had gone white-knuckled around his coffee mug. "Skipped her entirely, maybe. Skipped Mom too, since nothing like this ever happened to her that either of us ever knew about. And landed on me instead, two generations later, the first person in this whole line to ever stand close enough to an active release to wake it up."

Priya reached across the table and covered his hand with hers, the ledger still open between them, the ink of a marriage seventy years gone still holding the shape of a decision their grandmother had made long before either of them existed, trying, in whatever way she understood how, to protect a future she'd never live to see threatened.

"You didn't do this," Priya said, echoing Caleb's words from two nights before, needing Dev to hear it again, needing to believe it herself as much as she needed him to. "Eleanor didn't do this either, not really. She was young, and frightened, and she did the only thing she knew how to do with something too big and too strange to carry openly. She buried it and hoped it would stay buried. That's not a failure, Dev. That's just what frightened people do with things they don't understand and can't fight."

"It didn't work, though." Dev's voice cracked slightly. "Whatever she buried. It's not staying buried anymore."

"No," Priya agreed, quiet and steady despite the fear sitting heavy in her own chest. "But we're not her, Dev. We're not facing this alone in nineteen forty-seven with nothing but a new last name and a hope that distance would be enough. We have Mara and Caleb and Wren. We have Denise, who's lived through the closest thing to an answer any of us have access to. We have a name, and a record, and the start of an actual map of what we're dealing with." She squeezed his hand once, firm. "Eleanor ran. We're not going to run. We're going to understand this properly, and then we're going to end it, the way the last version of this ended, except faster, and with a lot fewer people paying a price for the delay."

Dev looked down at the ledger, at the faded ink recording a marriage meant to erase a name that had apparently refused to stay erased, and something in his expression shifted, fear still present but joined now by something steadier underneath it.

"Eleanor Whitlock Halloway," he said slowly, testing the full weight of the name for the first time in his life. "I wonder what she'd think, knowing it finally found its way back to one of us anyway."

"I don't know," Priya said honestly, closing the ledger with careful hands, the way Mara always handled anything old enough to have already survived one century of secrets. "But I think, whatever she'd think, she'd want us to fight it better than she did. Not just hide from it and hope. Actually fight it."

Outside the records office, the afternoon had turned gray and cool, autumn settling more firmly into the valley with every passing day, and somewhere out past the county line, in whatever quiet corner of the world had once held whatever remained of the Whitlock family's true history, something old and patient had, perhaps, just felt a name stir back to life after seventy years of careful, deliberate silence.
