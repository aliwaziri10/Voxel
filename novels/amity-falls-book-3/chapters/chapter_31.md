CHAPTER THIRTY-ONE

Dev couldn't sleep that night, the outbuilding's warm fire and Halloran's tired, sincere voice replaying behind his eyes every time he closed them, and sometime around two in the morning he gave up trying, pulling on a coat and slipping out onto the back porch where the cold at least gave him something honest to feel instead of the tangled thing sitting in his chest.

He wasn't surprised, twenty minutes later, when the screen door creaked open behind him and Priya settled onto the step at his side without asking permission, the two of them sitting in silence for a while before either spoke.

"You're doing the thing where you go quiet instead of talking," Priya said finally. "I used to think that meant you were fine. I know better now."

Dev huffed out something that wasn't quite a laugh, watching his own breath cloud faintly in the cold air. "I keep hearing him," he admitted. "Halloran. Not what he said exactly, more the way he said it. Like a man who's actually spent his whole life grieving something instead of a man selling me a lie he doesn't believe himself."

"Does that change your answer," Priya asked carefully, no pressure in the question, just genuine curiosity.

"No," Dev said, immediate and certain in a way that surprised even him a little, hearing it out loud. "It doesn't change my answer. It just makes the no cost more than it did an hour before I met him. I thought refusing a liar would feel clean. Refusing someone who actually believes he's offering mercy feels like taking something away from him too, not just protecting myself."

Priya was quiet for a moment, considering that. "I think that's probably what it's supposed to feel like," she said eventually. "The real, hard choices. Not clean. I don't think you'd trust your own answer as much if it came easy."

Dev turned that over, watching the dark line of the orchard he could no longer see clearly in the moonless night, only sense as a deeper shape against the slightly-less-dark sky. "He said the ring might not close the line," he said. "That it might just delay this same terror onto some grandchild of mine two generations from now, the way it delayed onto me from Eleanor. What if he's right about that part. What if I choose the ring, survive it, live a whole life, and fifty years from now some kid who doesn't even exist yet wakes up exactly the way I did."

"Then that kid gets a valley that already knows how to fight this," Priya said, an edge of fierce conviction entering her voice. "That's the part Halloran's doctrine doesn't seem to account for. He's measuring success by whether the debt disappears entirely, forever, in one motion. We're not promising that. We're promising the next person who inherits this, if there ever is one, gets Mara's structural map instead of starting from nothing the way we did. Gets Wren's understanding of the doctrine. Gets a ring method already tested once instead of invented from scratch under a six-week countdown. That's not nothing, Dev. That might even be better than an ending that isn't guaranteed to actually end anything, the way his own doctrine already failed once, by his own admission."

Dev sat with that for a long moment, something in his chest loosening slightly for the first time since they'd walked back across that dark field. "I hadn't thought of it that way," he admitted. "I've been so focused on whether the ring saves me, I hadn't really considered what it leaves behind for whoever comes after, if anyone does."

"You don't have to carry that weight too," Priya said gently. "Saving yourself is enough of a task for one person to hold. But if it helps, even a little, I don't think choosing to survive is the selfish option here. I think it might be the more generous one, in a way neither of us expected when this all started."

They sat together in the cold for a while longer, the silence comfortable now in a way it hadn't been earlier, and eventually Dev spoke again, his voice smaller than it had been all night.

"Can I tell you something I haven't told anyone," he said. "Not even Caleb."

"Always," Priya said.

"For a second, sitting in that room, watching the fire, listening to him talk about ending it forever instead of scattering it thin the way we're planning, I felt something that scared me more than the orchard did. Not the pull to say yes, exactly. Something quieter than that. A kind of tiredness that made his ending sound almost peaceful, compared to nine more weeks, and then a lifetime after that, of never quite knowing if I'm fully myself or just the version of me that survived something enormous. I didn't want to die. I want to be clear about that. I just, for a second, understood why someone exhausted enough might choose an ending over an uncertain, aching survival."

Priya's hand found his in the dark, gripping tight, and when she spoke her voice was steady despite the fear Dev could hear trembling just beneath its surface. "Thank you for telling me that," she said. "I need you to also hear me say, as plainly as I know how, that tired is not the same thing as wrong, and exhausted is not the same thing as certain. You're allowed to be tired enough to understand the appeal of an ending. You are not required to choose it just because you understand it. Those are two different things, and I need you to keep believing that, especially on the nights it gets hard to tell them apart."

Dev nodded slowly, some of the tightness in his chest finally easing at having said the thing out loud and had it met with steadiness instead of panic. "I don't want the ending," he said, testing the words fresh, hearing how true they sounded once spoken plainly into the cold night air. "I want the aching, uncertain survival. I want to find out who I am on the other side of this, even if it's not exactly who I was before. I'd rather have that unknown than his guaranteed, permanent nothing."

"Then that's your answer," Priya said. "The real one, not just the one you gave Halloran because you were angry and he was sitting right in front of you. This one, said quietly at two in the morning with nobody watching, no audience to perform certainty for."

"This one," Dev agreed.

They sat a while longer, neither in a hurry to go back inside despite the cold, and eventually Caleb's silhouette appeared in the kitchen window, checking on them the way he'd started doing most nights since the orchard, and Dev found himself glad, for once, that someone was watching, that the watching this time came from people who only ever meant to keep him safe.

"Come inside before you both freeze," Caleb called softly through the screen door. "Whatever you're working through out there, it'll still be there in the morning, and you'll think more clearly about it with feeling in your fingers."

Dev laughed, real and unguarded this time, the first genuinely light sound he'd made since walking out of Halloran's farmhouse. "He's not wrong," he said, pushing himself up off the step, offering Priya a hand to pull her up after him. "Ring plan or completion, doesn't matter which. Frostbite helps nobody's decision-making."

They went inside together, and Caleb had coffee waiting despite the hour, and for a little while the kitchen held nothing heavier than the ordinary tiredness of people who'd been awake too long, before Wren's alarm went off somewhere down the hall, signaling the start of another day pressed close against a countdown that refused to slow down for anyone's need to first make peace with what they'd chosen.

Morning brought the whole group back to the silo, Mara already there ahead of everyone else with a fresh diagram spread across the table, and Dev walked in with something steadier in his posture than he'd carried the night before, the ache of the choice still present but no longer wavering underneath it.

"I want everyone to hear this once, clearly," Dev said, before Mara could even finish her morning greeting. "I met Halloran last night. I heard his argument in full, the honest version, not the porch version. I understand it better than I did a week ago. And I'm choosing the ring anyway, not because I'm afraid of what he's offering, but because I want the uncertain version of surviving this more than I want his certain, permanent ending. I wanted you all to hear that from me directly, once, so nobody has to wonder if I'm still deciding."

Yusuf, who had arrived only minutes earlier, looked at him for a long moment, something like genuine pride settling into his tired face. "Then we build," he said simply. "However many days we have left, we spend every one of them making sure that choice actually holds."

Dev nodded, and for the first time since the blackouts began, the fear in his chest sat alongside something that felt, unmistakably, like resolve rather than dread.
