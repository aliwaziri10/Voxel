CHAPTER THIRTY-ONE

Dev couldn't sleep that night, the outbuilding's warm fire and Halloran's tired, sincere voice replaying behind his eyes every time he closed them, and sometime around two in the morning he gave up trying, pulling on a coat and slipping out onto the back porch where the cold at least gave him something honest to feel instead of the tangled thing sitting in his chest. The stars overhead were sharper than usual, the kind of clear autumn sky that meant a hard frost by morning, and he found himself staring at them longer than he needed to, grateful for anything that didn't require him to think.

He wasn't surprised, twenty minutes later, when the screen door creaked open behind him and Priya settled onto the step at his side without asking permission, the two of them sitting in silence for a while before either spoke, her shoulder pressed lightly against his, a small, wordless anchor against the cold and against whatever else was still unsettled in him.

"You're doing the thing where you go quiet instead of talking," Priya said finally. "I used to think that meant you were fine. I know better now."

Dev huffed out something that wasn't quite a laugh, watching his own breath cloud faintly in the cold air. "I keep hearing him," he admitted. "Halloran. Not what he said exactly, more the way he said it. Like a man who's actually spent his whole life grieving something instead of a man selling me a lie he doesn't believe himself. I went in there braced for a villain and came out with something harder to hold onto than that."

"Does that change your answer," Priya asked carefully, no pressure in the question, just genuine curiosity, the same steady patience she'd shown him since the porch confession weeks earlier.

"No," Dev said, immediate and certain in a way that surprised even him a little, hearing it out loud. "It doesn't change my answer. It just makes the no cost more than it did an hour before I met him. I thought refusing a liar would feel clean. Refusing someone who actually believes he's offering mercy feels like taking something away from him too, not just protecting myself. It feels like I'm the one closing a door he's spent his whole life trying to keep open for people like me."

Priya was quiet for a moment, considering that, her breath visible in the cold same as his. "I think that's probably what it's supposed to feel like," she said eventually. "The real, hard choices. Not clean. I don't think you'd trust your own answer as much if it came easy, and I don't think you'd be the person I know if this cost you nothing at all to refuse."

Dev turned that over, watching the dark line of the orchard he could no longer see clearly in the moonless night, only sense as a deeper shape against the slightly-less-dark sky, familiar even in its invisibility after so many nights of watching it from this same step. "He said the ring might not close the line," he said. "That it might just delay this same terror onto some grandchild of mine two generations from now, the way it delayed onto me from Eleanor, and onto her from whoever came before, all the way back to Ambrose or further. What if he's right about that part. What if I choose the ring, survive it, live a whole life, get old, and fifty years from now some kid who doesn't even exist yet wakes up exactly the way I did, blacking out over ordinary chores, terrified and alone in it the way I was before any of you knew what was happening to me."

"Then that kid gets a valley that already knows how to fight this," Priya said, an edge of fierce conviction entering her voice, sharp enough that it cut cleanly through the cold night air. "That's the part Halloran's doctrine doesn't seem to account for. He's measuring success by whether the debt disappears entirely, forever, in one motion, one grand gesture that either works completely or doesn't count at all. We're not promising that. We're promising the next person who inherits this, if there ever is one, gets Mara's structural map instead of starting from nothing the way we did. Gets Wren's understanding of the doctrine, hard-won and costly. Gets a ring method already tested once instead of invented from scratch under a six-week countdown with no roadmap at all. That's not nothing, Dev. That might even be better than an ending that isn't guaranteed to actually end anything, the way his own doctrine already failed once, by his own admission, sitting right there in his own sitting room."

Dev sat with that for a long moment, something in his chest loosening slightly for the first time since they'd walked back across that dark field hours earlier. "I hadn't thought of it that way," he admitted. "I've been so focused on whether the ring saves me, whether I come out the other side intact, I hadn't really considered what it leaves behind for whoever comes after, if anyone ever does. I was thinking about this like it only mattered whether I survived it. Not what surviving it might actually build."

"You don't have to carry that weight too," Priya said gently, her hand finding his in the dark, warm despite the cold pressing in around them both. "Saving yourself is enough of a task for one person to hold. But if it helps, even a little, I don't think choosing to survive is the selfish option here. I think it might be the more generous one, in a way neither of us expected when this all started, back when it was just four minutes and a stone you didn't remember picking up."

They sat together in the cold for a while longer, the silence comfortable now in a way it hadn't been earlier, something between them settling into a shared quiet rather than a held breath, and eventually Dev spoke again, his voice smaller than it had been all night, almost swallowed by the dark around them.

"Can I tell you something I haven't told anyone," he said. "Not even Caleb."

"Always," Priya said, her grip on his hand tightening slightly, bracing without flinching.

"For a second, sitting in that room, watching the fire, listening to him talk about ending it forever instead of scattering it thin the way we're planning, I felt something that scared me more than the orchard did. Not the pull to say yes, exactly, not like before, not some door in me opening without my permission. Something quieter than that. A tiredness that made his ending sound almost peaceful, compared to two more weeks of this, and then a lifetime after that, of never quite knowing if I'm fully myself or just the version of me that survived something enormous and changed by it. I didn't want to die. I want to be very clear with you about that, Priya, because I know how that sounds and I need you to hear the whole shape of it, not just the frightening piece. I just, for a second, understood why someone exhausted enough might choose an ending over an uncertain, aching survival, the way Denise once must have understood it too, some nights, alone with fifty years still stretching out ahead of her."

Priya's hand tightened further, gripping his so hard it nearly hurt, and when she spoke her voice was steady despite the fear Dev could hear trembling just beneath its surface, held there through sheer effort of will. "Thank you for telling me that," she said. "I need you to also hear me say, as plainly as I know how, that tired is not the same thing as wrong, and exhausted is not the same thing as certain. You're allowed to be tired enough to understand the appeal of an ending. You are not required to choose it just because you understand it. Those are two different things, and I need you to keep believing that, especially on the nights it gets hard to tell them apart, and especially because I am never, ever going to be able to hear you say something like that without my whole chest going cold, so please, keep telling me anyway, every single time it happens."

Dev nodded slowly, some of the tightness in his chest finally easing at having said the thing out loud and had it met with steadiness instead of panic, instead of the frightened overcorrection he'd half feared from her. "I don't want the ending," he said, testing the words fresh, hearing how true they sounded once spoken plainly into the cold night air, no longer just a reaction to Halloran's argument but something chosen deliberately, on its own terms. "I want the aching, uncertain survival. I want to find out who I am on the other side of this, even if it's not exactly who I was before, the way Caleb found out who he was after his own eight years came back to him. I'd rather have that unknown than his guaranteed, permanent nothing."

"Then that's your answer," Priya said. "The real one, not just the one you gave Halloran because you were angry and he was sitting right in front of you making his case. This one, said quietly at two in the morning with nobody watching, no audience to perform certainty for, nothing at stake except telling me the truth."

"This one," Dev agreed, and something in the words felt like a door closing gently rather than being slammed, final without being frightened.

They sat a while longer, neither in a hurry to go back inside despite the cold now properly settling into both of them, and eventually Caleb's silhouette appeared in the kitchen window, checking on them the way he'd started doing most nights since the orchard, and Dev found himself glad, for once, that someone was watching, that the watching this time came from people who only ever meant to keep him safe rather than lead him somewhere he hadn't chosen.

"Come inside before you both freeze," Caleb called softly through the screen door. "Whatever you're working through out there, it'll still be there in the morning, and you'll think more clearly about it with feeling in your fingers instead of numb from sitting on cold concrete half the night."

Dev laughed, real and unguarded this time, the first genuinely light sound he'd made since walking out of Halloran's farmhouse hours earlier. "He's not wrong," he said, pushing himself up off the step, offering Priya a hand to pull her up after him, the small ordinary gesture feeling, in that moment, like its own small act of choosing to keep going. "Ring plan or completion, doesn't matter which. Frostbite helps nobody's decision-making."

They went inside together, and Caleb had coffee waiting despite the hour, warming three mugs on the stove without being asked, and for a little while the kitchen held nothing heavier than the ordinary tiredness of people who'd been awake too long, trading small, easy jokes about the cold and the coffee's strength, before Wren's alarm went off somewhere down the hall, signaling the start of another day pressed close against a countdown that refused to slow down for anyone's need to first make peace with what they'd chosen.

Morning brought the whole group back to the silo, Mara already there ahead of everyone else with a fresh diagram spread across the table, pencil already moving before the others had fully shaken off the night's exhaustion, and Dev walked in with something steadier in his posture than he'd carried the night before, the ache of the choice still present but no longer wavering underneath it, settled now the way a decision settles once it's actually been made rather than merely survived.

"I want everyone to hear this once, clearly," Dev said, before Mara could even finish her morning greeting, his voice carrying further than usual in the silo's echoing space. "I met Halloran last night. I heard his argument in full, the honest version, not the porch version, not the orchard version. I understand it better than I did a week ago, better than I probably want to. And I'm choosing the ring anyway, not because I'm afraid of what he's offering, but because I want the uncertain version of surviving this more than I want his certain, permanent ending. I wanted you all to hear that from me directly, once, plainly, so nobody has to wonder if I'm still deciding, or watch me carefully for signs that I might change my mind under pressure."

Yusuf, who had arrived only minutes earlier, looked at him for a long moment, something like genuine pride settling into his tired face, the same expression he'd worn once watching Denise finally choose honesty over silence. "Then we build," he said simply. "However many days we have left, we spend every one of them making sure that choice actually holds, and making sure this valley never again puts someone in a position where an ending starts to look more merciful than the fight to stay."

Dev nodded, and for the first time since the blackouts began, the fear in his chest sat alongside something that felt, unmistakably, like resolve rather than dread, the two feelings no longer fighting each other but finally, quietly, learning to share the same small space inside him.
