CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE

Priya gave herself three days, and it took her two.

She started with the records office itself, a quiet, unremarkable building she'd walked past a thousand times without ever really looking at it, and began cross-referencing Elias Thorne's official movements against the timeline of every quiet family contact the group had documented so far. The stranger at the orchard gate, the woman on Dev's porch, the visit to her own substation two nights ago. Small things, individually meaningless, that started to align in a pattern she wished, even now, she could talk herself out of seeing.

She worked carefully, methodically, the same discipline she'd built during the Denise stakeout years ago, refusing to let herself skip ahead to a conclusion before the evidence actually supported it. Elias's personnel file was thin and ordinary in the way most long-tenured clerks' files were, decades of unremarkable service, a handful of commendations for thoroughness, no disciplinary notes of any kind. He'd never married, lived alone in a small house at the edge of town, kept to himself outside of work in a manner nobody had ever thought worth commenting on. Ordinary, in every visible way. Exactly the sort of life that left almost nothing behind for anyone to question.

Elias had requested access to the almanac archive the same week Wren first found the eclipse-cycle pattern, citing an unrelated county history project that, on closer inspection, didn't appear to exist anywhere in his actual work record. He'd taken an unscheduled half day the exact afternoon the orchard gate encounter happened, logged as a doctor's appointment Priya confirmed, discreetly, had never actually taken place. And the council briefing's seating chart, the detail that had chilled her most in her own office two nights ago, matched a floor plan Elias himself had drafted and circulated to the room's organizers only hours before the meeting, in his official capacity as records clerk.

None of it, alone, would have held up as proof of anything. Together, laid end to end against the timeline of contacts, it formed a shape Priya couldn't unsee once she'd seen it.

She sat with all of it laid out across her kitchen table late into a second night, Dev asleep upstairs, the house quiet around her, and felt the isolation of being the only person who knew something this heavy before she'd found the courage to say it out loud to anyone else.

"You've been at that table for three hours," Caleb said gently, letting himself in the back door the way he had permission to now, a key Priya had given him months ago during the worst of the Denise investigation and never bothered to take back. "Mara sent me to check on you. She said you sounded strange on the phone earlier."

Priya looked up at him, exhaustion and certainty warring plainly across her face. "I know who it is," she said. "I've spent two days trying to find a way it isn't true, and I can't. It's Elias Thorne."

Caleb sat down slowly across from her, taking in the spread of documents, the timeline she'd built line by careful line. "The records clerk. The one who wanted the oversight subcommittee."

"The one nobody in that room thought twice about," Priya said. "Which is exactly why he's been so effective. He's quiet. He's thorough. He's spent his entire career being the person everyone trusts to file things correctly and never asks a follow-up question about. Nobody watches the man taking notes in the back of the room. That's the whole point of being him."

Caleb was quiet a moment, studying the timeline more carefully, tracing one finger along the dates. "You built this the same way you built the case against Denise," he said. "Every document confirmed twice before you'd let yourself trust it."

"I couldn't do it any other way," Priya admitted. "Not after what almost happened between me and Dev, back before any of us understood what he was carrying. I promised myself I'd never again let a feeling get ahead of the facts, no matter how strongly the feeling was pointing somewhere."

"Are you certain," Caleb asked, and there was no accusation in it, only the careful weight of a man who'd once watched Priya nearly destroy her own relationship with her brother over a rush to judgment neither of them wanted repeated.

"As certain as circumstantial evidence can make me without a confession," Priya said. "The timeline lines up too cleanly to be coincidence. But I know what it looks like to build a case on pattern-matching alone, Caleb. I did that once, with Denise, and it turned out to be true. I don't have the luxury of assuming I'll be right twice in a row just because I was right the first time."

"So what do we do."

"We bring it to the whole group tomorrow," Priya said. "All of it, exactly as I found it, and we let everyone weigh in before anyone confronts him directly. I'm not making this call alone. Not after everything Yusuf learned about moving too slowly, and not after everything I learned about moving too fast."

The next morning, the full group gathered at Mara's kitchen table, the same table that had held Adelaide's letter and the drought file and every other hard truth this valley had forced them to face over the past two years. Priya laid out the timeline in full, watching each face carefully as the pieces landed.

Wren went very still, something clicking into place behind her eyes. "The full read on the coat button," she said slowly. "I got a flash before I had to let go. Someone close to the council, afraid for a long time. I couldn't place a face to it. I think that was him."

"Afraid of what," Dev asked, his voice quieter than usual, still recovering strength between blackouts that had grown longer and closer together with every passing day.

"I don't know yet," Wren admitted. "The read wasn't specific enough to say. Just fear, old and worn smooth the way fear gets when you've carried it a long time without ever putting it down."

Mara had gone quiet, working the problem the way she worked any structural failure, looking for the load-bearing point that had actually given way. "If he's been afraid a long time," she said slowly, "afraid of what specifically, that's the question that matters most right now. Malice and fear point toward very different conversations when we finally sit down across from him."

"The doctrine Wren surfaced in her earlier read matters here," Caleb said. "The quiet family believes every scattered release only delays the debt onto someone else's grandchild eventually. If Elias believes that too, if he's spent years quietly afraid that saving Dev the way we saved Denise just passes this exact nightmare on to some child not born yet, that's not the same as a man selling us out for money or spite."

"It doesn't make it acceptable," Priya said. "But it might make it something we can actually talk to him about, instead of something we simply have to punish."

Yusuf, arriving late and listening to the tail end of the conversation, sat down heavily at the head of the table, his face carrying the same heavy weight Priya had come to recognize from every conversation that touched his own regret over Denise. "I should have caught this sooner," he said. "He's sat in council meetings for years. I should have noticed something."

"You couldn't have," Mara said firmly. "Nobody could have. That's precisely why he's been effective at it for however long this has actually gone on."

"How do we approach him," Dev asked. "Directly. Carefully. Do we let Priya handle it alone, given her position, or does it need to be all of us."

"All of us," Wren said immediately. "If he really is afraid, the way I think he is, showing up with the whole valley's worth of trust in this room might matter more than showing up with a badge. Fear doesn't usually respond well to more authority. It responds to being seen honestly."

"And if he's not afraid," Caleb said carefully. "If I'm wrong, if this is closer to malice than fear, we still need Priya's authority in the room too. We don't know which version of this we're walking into until we're already standing in front of him."

Dev shifted in his chair, something working behind his tired eyes. "I keep thinking about what Denise told me," he said quietly. "About deciding things as yourself, not as whatever role everyone else has already assigned you. If Elias has spent years afraid, believing he was protecting some child not yet born by feeding information to people who want me to die at that eclipse, I wonder if anyone's ever asked him plainly what he actually wants. The way Denise asked me."

"That's a generous read on a man who's been endangering your life for weeks," Priya said, though there was no anger in it, only the same careful weighing she'd been doing since she first laid the timeline out on her own kitchen table.

"Maybe," Dev said. "Or maybe it's the only read that actually gets us somewhere useful instead of just somewhere angry."

The table sat with that for a long moment, the weight of it settling over all of them differently, each person running through their own calculation of risk and trust and what a confrontation with a frightened, sympathetic man who'd nonetheless endangered all of them might actually require.

"Not alone," Priya said finally. "I learned that lesson with Dev too. Whatever this becomes, we do it together, the way we've done everything else that's actually worked."

Wren looked around the table at each of them in turn, something steadier in her expression than the raw uncertainty of a few minutes ago. "Then we go to him today," she said. "Before the quiet family has any more time to use him, and before this valley loses one more day it can't afford to lose."

Priya stood, gathering the timeline documents back into their folder, her hands steadier now than they'd been at any point over the last two sleepless nights. "I'll call his office," she said. "Ask him to meet us somewhere neutral. Not the substation, not the records office. Somewhere he won't feel cornered before we've even said a word."

"The old grange hall," Mara suggested. "Neutral ground for everyone in this valley for as long as I can remember. If we want him to talk honestly instead of just defending himself, that might matter more than we think."

"The grange hall," Priya agreed. "This afternoon. All of us together."

Dev pushed himself up from the table slowly, the effort of it visible in the careful way he moved, but something steadier in his own posture too, the same quiet resolve Priya had watched grow in him across every hard week of this whole ordeal. "I want to be there," he said. "Not hidden away for my own safety this time. If this is about me, I want to hear it happen, not have it reported back to me afterward like I'm something fragile being managed."

Priya looked at him for a long moment, old protective instinct warring with the respect she'd learned to give his own choices across the past difficult weeks. "Okay," she said finally. "You're there. Beside Caleb, the whole time, but there."

Dev nodded, something like gratitude crossing his exhausted face, and the group began gathering their things, the weight of what was coming settling over each of them differently as they moved toward the door.

Outside, the autumn light had gone thin and pale, the eclipse now less than four weeks away, and Priya found herself thinking, not for the first time this week, that the ground beneath all of them kept shifting faster than any of them could build solid footing to stand on.
