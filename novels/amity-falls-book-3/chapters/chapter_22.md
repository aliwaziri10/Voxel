CHAPTER TWENTY-TWO

Priya was alone at the substation two nights after the visit to Denise's kitchen, catching up on paperwork she'd let slide for a week, when the woman appeared in her doorway without the courtesy of a knock.

The building had gone quiet the way it always did after nine, just the hum of the old radiator and the occasional creak of the ceiling settling against the autumn cold outside. Priya had left the front door unlocked out of habit rather than intention, the same habit she'd kept since long before any of this started, back when the worst thing she'd expected to walk through that door on a slow night was a farmer reporting a missing dog. She'd been halfway through a stack of incident reports, her coffee gone cold beside her elbow, when the air in the room shifted in some way she couldn't have named, and she looked up to find she was no longer alone.

She was older than the man who'd approached Dev at the orchard gate, silver-haired, dressed plainly, her hands folded in front of her with the same deliberate stillness Dev had described. Priya's own hand moved instinctively toward her radio before the woman spoke, her voice mild and unhurried in a way that made the reach for the radio feel, suddenly, almost foolish.

"I'm not here to hurt you, Deputy," the woman said. "I'm here to talk, and then I'll leave, and you can call whoever you like the moment I'm gone. I only ask for a few minutes first."

"You're one of them," Priya said, not moving her hand away from the radio entirely. "The quiet family. My brother told us what your man said to him at the orchard."

"Then you already know we prefer conversation to force," the woman said, stepping just inside the doorway, no farther, hands still visible and folded. "I imagine that's small comfort right now. I understand."

"Small comfort is generous," Priya said. "I've spent my whole career learning to read people who'd rather talk their way past a problem than force it. You're better at it than most, I'll give you that much."

"You have exactly as long as it takes me to decide you're worth listening to," Priya said. "Talk."

The woman inclined her head, unhurried, and lowered herself into the chair across from Priya's desk without waiting to be invited, though something in the careful way she did it suggested she'd noted Priya hadn't actually offered the seat and chosen to risk it anyway. "I'll be direct, since you clearly prefer it. I'm here to offer you something valuable in exchange for something simple. Information, in exchange for your group stepping back from what you're planning for your brother."

"We're not stepping back from anything," Priya said flatly.

"Not yet," the woman agreed. "But you might change your mind once you hear what I have to offer. I know things about your own investigation that you haven't shared outside your small circle. I know about the outbuilding six miles past the eastern ridge your Mara identified. I know about the timeline your Wren built from the almanac records. I know, specifically, that your council briefing two nights ago included a headcount of exactly nine people in that room, and I could tell you precisely who sat where."

Priya's blood went cold, the professional part of her mind already racing ahead of the fear, cataloging what the woman had just revealed without seeming to realize she'd revealed it at all. Nobody outside the nine people in that briefing room should have known the headcount, let alone the seating.

"How would you know any of that," Priya asked, keeping her voice level through sheer force of will, "unless somebody in that room told you."

The woman's expression flickered, just slightly, something that might have been the ghost of satisfaction at having made her point without needing to spell it out further. "I didn't say that," she said. "I only told you what I know. You're a sharp woman, Deputy. I trust you to draw your own conclusions."

"You could be lying," Priya said, though even as she said it she heard how thin it sounded. "You could have gotten the headcount from a dozen ordinary sources. Someone talking too loud at the diner. A clerk mentioning a scheduled meeting to a spouse. You don't need a mole to know nine people sat in a room."

"That's true," the woman agreed easily, unbothered by the challenge. "I could have. But I didn't say I knew the headcount, Deputy. I said I could tell you precisely who sat where. That's a different kind of knowing. Ask yourself honestly how many ordinary sources would know that Yusuf sat at the head of the table, or that your Wren chose the seat nearest the door, the way she always does when she wants an easy way out of a room that frightens her."

Priya's stomach dropped further, the specificity of the detail landing exactly the way the woman clearly intended it to. Wren did always sit near the door. Nobody outside that room should have had any reason to know that small habit, unless they'd either watched her do it themselves, which seemed unlikely given the tight security around the meeting, or heard it described by someone who'd actually been sitting inside.

Priya's mind was already moving through the room in memory, seat by seat, face by face. Yusuf. Mara. Caleb. Wren. Dev. Herself. The county records clerk who'd been quietly assisting with the almanac cross-referencing. The two council members who'd been brought in for the formal authorization vote. And Elias Thorne, the longtime records clerk who'd argued for caution and volunteered for the oversight subcommittee, sitting near the back, taking his careful, thorough notes.

She kept her face as still as she could manage, refusing to let the woman see which name had landed hardest. "What's the information you're offering," she asked instead, redirecting before her own expression could betray anything further.

"The identity of the person feeding us details from inside your circle," the woman said simply. "In exchange for your group agreeing to let the completion happen on its own terms, without interference, when the eclipse comes. A clean trade. You get certainty about who's been compromising your safety. We get what we've been waiting generations for."

"And if I say no."

"Then you keep wondering," the woman said, standing smoothly, unhurried even now. "Every meeting, every plan, every safety precaution, you'll wonder if the person sitting across the table from you is the reason we already know about it before you've finished discussing it. That's not a comfortable way to protect your brother, Deputy. I'd think carefully about whether comfort matters less to you than certainty."

Priya stood too, putting the desk fully between them, her hand finally settling firmly on the radio at her hip. "Get out," she said. "I'm not trading anything with you. Not information, not Dev's life, not my own peace of mind. You can walk out that door on your own, or I can make it considerably less pleasant. Your choice."

The woman studied her for a long moment, something almost like respect crossing her weathered face. "Your brother said something very similar to my colleague, at the orchard gate," she said. "I suppose it runs in the family, this stubborn streak. I'll leave you to your evening, Deputy. But the offer remains open. We're patient people. We can wait for you to reconsider, the same way we've waited for your family for generations."

She left as quietly as she'd arrived, and Priya stood alone in the substation for a long moment afterward, her pulse still hammering, her mind circling back relentlessly to the image of Elias Thorne's careful notes, his unusually close attention to the acceleration data, the faint unease she'd felt and dismissed twice already without naming it properly even to herself.

She sat back down heavily, pulled a blank legal pad toward herself, and began writing, the same disciplined, factual language she'd taught Wren to use during the Denise stakeout years ago. Time of contact. Physical description. Exact wording, as close to verbatim as she could reconstruct. And beneath all of it, in smaller letters, a note she almost didn't want to commit to paper because writing it made it feel more real than she was ready for it to be.

*Headcount and seating from council briefing known to outside party. Nine people present. Investigate quietly. Do not tell the group yet. Confirm before accusing.*

She sat with the pen still in her hand for a long time after that, staring at the words, thinking of Elias's steady, unremarkable presence across every meeting since Denise's story had first broken open, a man who'd never once raised his voice or drawn attention to himself, exactly the sort of person nobody thought to watch closely. She thought of Yusuf's own hard-won lesson from hesitating too long with Denise, and wondered whether she was now about to make the opposite mistake, moving too fast on too little, accusing an innocent man on the strength of a stranger's manipulation designed specifically to make her doubt someone she trusted.

But the seating chart. The exact headcount. Someone had given the quiet family details that specific, and Priya had learned, across a hard year of watching patterns resolve into truths nobody wanted, that specific details rarely arrived by accident.

She locked the substation an hour later than usual that night, glancing back once at the dark windows before she drove home, unable to shake the feeling that somewhere out past the eastern ridge, in an outbuilding nobody had mapped until recently, the woman who'd sat across from her tonight was already reporting back that the seed had been planted exactly where she'd intended it to land.

The drive home took longer than it should have, Priya taking the longer route around the north fields instead of the direct road, needing the extra ten minutes to settle her own thoughts before she had to walk through her own front door and decide what, if anything, to say to Dev tonight. He'd been trying to sleep better lately, on Mara's insistence, and she wasn't willing to hand him another sleepless night on the strength of a suspicion she hadn't yet confirmed.

She thought of Elias at the council briefing, the close attentiveness she'd noticed and dismissed, his questions about the acceleration data phrased just carefully enough to sound like ordinary caution rather than anything worth flagging. She thought of every meeting since Denise's story had come to light where he'd sat quietly near the back, contributing little, absorbing everything, a man whose whole professional life had been built around being the person nobody thought twice about in a room. That was, she realized with a cold clarity that settled somewhere deep in her chest, precisely the sort of person who made an effective source for someone patient enough to wait generations for the right opening.

But suspicion wasn't proof, and she'd learned the hard way, watching her own brother nearly destroyed by a rush to judgment once before, that acting on a feeling without confirming it first could cost an innocent person everything. She would investigate quietly. She would confirm before she accused anyone, Elias included, no matter how strongly her instincts were already leaning.

By the time she pulled into her own driveway, the porch light burning warm against the early dark, she had made her decision. She would give herself three days. Three days to watch, to cross-reference, to build the same careful, factual case she'd once built against Denise, before she said Elias Thorne's name out loud to anyone at all.
