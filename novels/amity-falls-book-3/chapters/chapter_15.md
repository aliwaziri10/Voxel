CHAPTER FIFTEEN

Dev didn't sleep. He lay on top of the covers with his boots still on, staring at the ceiling of his old room in Mara's house, the one they'd moved him into the week the blackouts started, close enough to the kitchen that someone would hear if anything happened in the night. Downstairs he could hear the low murmur of voices that hadn't stopped since he'd gone up, Priya's clipped, careful cadence under Caleb's steadier one, Wren interjecting every so often with something sharper. Nobody had told him to go to bed. He'd simply reached the point where he couldn't sit in that kitchen chair another minute without breaking apart in front of all of them, and going up here alone had felt like the only version of holding himself together he had left.

He kept hearing her voice. Not the words themselves, though those sat lodged under his ribs too, heavy and unmoving. The tone. The unhurried, patient warmth of it, the way she'd made an ending sound like a mercy instead of a threat.

That was the part he hadn't told them yet. Not the offer itself, not the shape of what she'd said. He'd given them all of that downstairs, every detail, exactly the way he'd promised himself he would. What he hadn't said, what he wasn't sure he had the nerve to say even now, lying alone in the dark, was that for one full breath on that porch step, some part of him had wanted to believe her.

He rolled onto his side and pressed his forehead against the cool wall, willing the thought to loosen its grip. It didn't. Wanting to believe her wasn't the same as agreeing with her, he told himself, the same argument he'd been running in circles since he'd walked back through that door. But it felt close enough to the same thing that the distinction had stopped comforting him somewhere around the third hour of lying here.

He thought about the years before all this, the ones he still couldn't talk about without his voice going flat and careful, the way it did now whenever the old shame surfaced uninvited. He'd spent a long time learning how to sit with wanting the wrong thing and not acting on it, one day at a time, the same slow discipline that had eventually earned him back a version of Priya's trust he'd once been certain he'd burned for good. He'd thought that kind of wanting was behind him for good. Finding it again tonight, dressed up in a stranger's gentle voice instead of his own old habits, felt like discovering an old wound hadn't healed as fully as he'd let himself believe. It scared him more than the blackouts did, if he was honest. The blackouts took time from him without his consent. This, whatever this was, had asked, and some small, exhausted part of him had almost answered.

A soft knock came at the door a little after midnight. He knew the knock before she said anything, three quiet taps and then quiet again, waiting to be let in rather than assuming she should be.

"I'm awake," he said.

Priya came in and sat on the edge of the bed without turning on the light, leaving them both in the pale grey spill from the hallway. She didn't say anything right away, which was a mercy in itself. He'd spent years learning to read the difference between her silences, back before either of them had trusted the other with much of anything, and this one wasn't the careful, procedural quiet she used on suspects. This one was just her, tired, worried, giving him room to start if he wanted to.

"I didn't tell you everything," he said finally.

"I figured." Her voice stayed even, no judgment in it that he could find, though he was listening hard for it. "You've had that look since we came upstairs. Same one you had the night before you turned yourself in, all those years ago. Like you're carrying something you think is going to change what I think of you."

The comparison landed harder than she probably meant it to, mostly because it was accurate. "For a second," he said, and made himself keep going before he lost the nerve, "for one second out there, I wanted to say yes to her. Not because I want to hurt anyone. Because she made it sound like the only version of this where I actually get to stop being afraid. Like there was a door out, and all I had to do was walk through it, and it would finally be over, one way or another."

Priya was quiet for a long moment. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone rougher than he expected. "That doesn't scare me the way you think it does."

"It should."

"Dev." She turned to face him fully now, the hallway light catching the exhaustion under her eyes, the same exhaustion he'd been feeling settle into his own bones for weeks. "You've been in pain for two weeks straight, blacking out, losing time, watching your own body do things you can't control or predict. Somebody offered you an ending to that. Of course some part of you leaned toward it. That's not betrayal. That's just what happens to a person who's been afraid for long enough. It would worry me more if you told me it never once tempted you."

"You're not angry."

"I'm scared," she said honestly. "There's a difference, and I'm done pretending there isn't, for your sake as much as mine. I was angry at you once, a long time ago, for a choice you made that hurt people, and I let that anger define how I saw you for longer than I should have. This isn't that. This is my brother being offered a way to stop suffering by people who don't actually care whether he survives what they're calling mercy. I'm not going to punish you for being human about that offer. I'm going to make sure you never have to face it alone again."

Something in Dev's chest loosened slightly, not all the way, but enough that he could breathe around it instead of just holding his breath against it. "Mara asked something downstairs. Before I came up. About the next carrier. Whether we've actually thought about what happens two generations from now if we save me the easy way."

"I heard."

"Nobody answered her."

"No," Priya agreed. "Because none of us have a good answer yet. That doesn't mean there isn't one. It means we haven't found it." She reached over and took his hand, the same gesture she'd used a hundred times this past week, steady and unshowy, grounding rather than comforting. "You don't have to solve that tonight. You don't have to solve any of it tonight. You just have to stay, and let the rest of us carry some of this with you instead of trying to hold it alone the way you tried to hide the first blackout."

Dev let out a long breath, some of the tension in his shoulders finally easing under her grip. "I'm scared I'm going to become the thing that makes everyone I love choose between me and the rest of the valley."

"You're not going to become anything," Priya said, firm now, the deputy's certainty bleeding back into her voice. "We're going to find a version of this where nobody has to choose. I don't know what it looks like yet. But I know we're not letting a woman in a grey coat tell us the only options are total completion or an eighty-year delay. There's a third door somewhere. We just haven't found it yet."

They sat in the quiet a while longer, neither of them speaking, until Dev finally felt his eyes start to grow heavy, exhaustion catching up to him now that the worst of the confession was out of him. Priya stayed until his breathing evened out, then eased the door shut behind her and went back downstairs, where the low murmur of voices picked up again almost immediately.

By morning, the whole group had gathered around Mara's kitchen table again, coffee going cold in front of most of them, the same map from the day before still spread across one corner. Dev came down expecting the conversation to have moved on without him overnight. Instead he found all four of them waiting, not for him to explain himself further, but simply for him to be there before they kept going.

"We're not treating last night as a crisis to manage," Caleb said, before Dev had even sat down fully. "We're treating it as information. You told us the truth about something hard. That's the only response we need to have to it."

He said it plainly, without the softness he usually wrapped around hard truths, and Dev understood why the moment he caught the look on Caleb's face. This wasn't sympathy borrowed secondhand. Caleb knew what it was to want an ending that wasn't really his to want, the two years he'd lost still ached in him some mornings like a joint before rain, and he'd told Dev more than once that the worst part of losing that time hadn't been the loss itself. It had been the quiet, guilty relief he'd felt in the first days after getting it back, when some cowardly corner of him had briefly missed not having to carry the weight of remembering. If anyone at this table had earned the right to say Dev's near-agreement wasn't a betrayal, it was Caleb, and everyone around the table seemed to understand that without needing it explained.

"Mara's question," Dev said, sitting heavily in his chair. "About the next carrier. I've been thinking about it since Priya came up last night. I don't want an answer that just gets me through the eclipse. I want one that actually means somebody else's kid doesn't end up on a porch like that one day."

Mara set down her coffee, something in her expression sharpening the way it did whenever a structural problem finally had a shape she could work with. "Then we treat it as its own problem. Not instead of saving you. Alongside it." She pulled the county map toward her, already reaching for a pencil. "I've been tracking the quiet family's one confirmed location and their two confirmed contacts like an isolated incident. I don't think that's right anymore. I think there's a pattern to how they operate, how long they wait, how they choose who to approach and when. If I can map that the way I mapped Denise's well, we might find something that helps both problems at once. Not just where they're staying. How they think."

"That's a bigger job than watching an outbuilding," Priya said.

"It is," Mara agreed. "I'd like to start today."

Wren, who had been unusually quiet through the whole exchange, finally spoke, turning her notebook around so the others could see the page she'd been working on since before dawn. "I want to try something too. A full read, not the light kind I did on the stone. On the coat button I found on the porch step this morning, the one she must have lost when she stood up to leave."

The table went still. Priya's jaw tightened first. "Wren, a full read on something connected to someone we know almost nothing about, that's not a small risk. You know what a full read cost you before. Twice, with Denise's well and with Kell's letters."

"I know exactly what it cost me," Wren said, steady in a way that made it clear she'd already argued this out with herself before bringing it to the table. "I'm not asking permission carelessly. I'm asking because we don't actually know why they want this, not really, only that they say they do. A read on an object she touched might tell us the real reason, not just the version she chose to say out loud on that porch. I think that's worth the risk. I don't think it's worth doing alone, or without every safeguard we used the last two times."

Dev looked between them, something steadying in his own chest at the sight of the four of them already arguing out loud over how best to fight for him, instead of quietly deciding it without him the way people so often had before. "Do it," he said. "Whatever you need to make it safe. Do it."

Priya held his gaze a long moment, weighing something behind her eyes that Dev couldn't fully read, before she finally nodded, slow and reluctant. "Full precautions. Caleb and I both present. If anything goes wrong, we pull you out immediately, no negotiation."

"Understood," Wren said.

Outside, the frost had thickened overnight, the fields beyond Mara's kitchen window gone a hard, glittering white in the early light, four weeks and six days now standing between all of them and whatever waited at the eclipse. Dev looked out at it and found, for the first time since the porch step, that the fear sitting in his chest had company now instead of solitude, four other people bracing themselves against the same cold morning, refusing to let him face any part of it alone.
