CHAPTER THIRTEEN

Priya spent the better part of two days working the stranger's description through every channel she had access to, official and otherwise, starting with the plate on the car Wren had spotted parked half a mile down the county road the same afternoon, gone by the time anyone thought to walk out and check it more closely.

She'd started, as she always did, with the boring work first, the part that rarely made for a good story but usually cracked a case faster than any dramatic leap of intuition ever did. A physical description written up in the same flat, factual language she'd taught Wren to use during the Denise stakeout, no adjectives, no speculation, only what could be verified. Mid-forties, she guessed, though something about his stillness had made him hard to place exactly. Ordinary height, ordinary build, dark coat, nothing about his clothing memorable enough to distinguish him from a hundred other men who might pass through any small town on any given day. The only truly unusual thing about him, in the end, was how thoroughly unremarkable he'd made himself, a deliberate kind of invisibility that Priya had learned, across years of this work, usually meant someone who'd had a great deal of practice avoiding exactly this kind of scrutiny.

The plate came back registered to a rental agency three counties over, the car itself returned two days after the encounter, paid for in cash by someone using an identification number that didn't match any state database Priya had access to. Not expired. Not fraudulent in any way she could immediately prove. Simply absent, as though the number had been generated rather than issued, existing only long enough to complete one transaction before dissolving back into nothing.

"That shouldn't be possible," she said, sitting at Mara's kitchen table with her laptop open, the rental agency's confirmation email glowing on the screen in front of her. "Every ID number in this system traces back to an actual issuing office eventually. Even fake ones usually borrow a real structure, a real sequence, something that at least looks plausible if you don't dig deep enough. This doesn't look plausible. It looks manufactured from scratch by someone who understood the format well enough to fake it convincingly at a glance, but didn't care whether it held up under real scrutiny, because they never expected anyone to look this closely."

"Or because they knew you would, and didn't care," Caleb said, leaning over her shoulder to study the screen himself. "If they're as patient as Odette's grandmother said, maybe getting caught looking suspicious doesn't actually threaten them. Maybe they wanted us to see exactly how untraceable they are. A demonstration, not a mistake."

Priya's jaw tightened at that, the same flicker of unease crossing her face that had been building steadily since the encounter itself. "I don't like that theory. But I can't rule it out either."

Dev had been listening from his chair by the stove, quiet through most of the technical discussion, but something in Priya's frustration seemed to reach him now in a way the earlier details hadn't. "So even someone as good as you can't find them," he said, his voice low. "That's supposed to reassure me somehow, that the people watching my family are better at hiding than the deputy who's been protecting me this whole week."

"It's not supposed to reassure you," Priya said honestly, closing the laptop halfway before opening it again, restless with the same energy that had been building in her for two days. "It's supposed to tell you the truth, which is that we're dealing with something more sophisticated than we first assumed. That doesn't mean we lose. It means we adjust how we're playing this."

She'd tried three other angles over the two days, each one dissolving the same way the identification number had. A physical description circulated quietly among a few trusted contacts in neighboring counties, no matches, no sightings before or since. A search through property records for any recent land transactions near Whitlock-adjacent sites going back decades, nothing that stood out as obviously connected. Even a careful, deniable inquiry to an old contact of hers in state records, someone who owed her a favor from years back, had come back with nothing but a slightly nervous phone call asking why she was interested in an identification number that "didn't look like it should exist."

She'd sat with that phone call longer than she wanted to admit to anyone at this table, turning the contact's unease over in her mind late into the previous night. He wasn't a man who spooked easily, not after fifteen years of favors traded back and forth between them, and something about the flatness in his voice when he'd asked the question had told her he already suspected an answer he didn't want confirmed. She hadn't pushed him further. Some doors, she'd learned long ago, were better left only cracked rather than forced fully open before you knew what waited on the other side.

"Maybe that's the actual answer," Wren said, looking up from her own notebook where she'd been cross-referencing every detail of Dev's transcript against the almanac records one more time. "Maybe it doesn't exist. Maybe whoever these people are, they've found a way to move through the world without leaving any trail our systems are built to catch. Old enough, patient enough, maybe they figured out generations ago how to stay invisible to whatever counts as official record-keeping in any given era."

"That's not comforting," Dev said quietly from where he sat near the stove, wrapped in a blanket despite the fire, still not fully recovered from the encounter two days earlier even though nothing physical had happened to him during it.

"It's not meant to be comforting," Priya said, closing the laptop with more force than strictly necessary. "It's meant to be honest. I've spent two days trying every legitimate tool available to me, and every single one of them came back empty. That tells me something important, even if it's not the something I wanted to find. Whoever's tracking your family, Dev, they're either extraordinarily good at covering their movements, or they're operating by rules our systems were never built to catch in the first place."

Mara had been quiet through most of this, working a different angle entirely, spreading out the county survey maps she'd used to track Denise's pattern the previous year, now cross-referenced against every property within a day's travel of the valley that had ever shown up in old Whitlock-adjacent records. She'd spent the better part of a full day on it herself, methodical the way she was with any structural problem, ruling out property after property that turned out to have clean, boring histories, nothing more sinister than ordinary farm families who'd owned their land for generations without a single gap worth noting.

"I found one thing," she said finally, tapping a small parcel marked on the map's edge, well outside the valley proper. "An outbuilding on land that changed hands informally sometime in the nineteen forties, never fully registered under any name I can pin down cleanly. It's a gap in the record that usually means either sloppy paperwork or deliberate obscurity. Given everything else we've learned this week, I'm inclined to guess the second."

"How far out," Priya asked, already reaching for her own notes.

"Six miles past the eastern ridge. Far enough that nobody from town would have a reason to pass it regularly, close enough that a patient watcher could still reach the valley inside twenty minutes if they needed to." Mara traced the route with one finger, the same careful precision she'd once used mapping Denise's well and root cellar. "It's not proof of anything. It's an anomaly, the kind a careful person notices precisely because everything around it is so ordinary by comparison."

"Could that be where he's staying," Caleb asked, leaning over to study the map himself.

"I don't know," Mara admitted. "It's a guess built on the absence of information rather than the presence of it, exactly the sort of guess Priya's spent two days teaching me to be suspicious of. But it's the only thread we have that isn't a dead end, and I think it's worth watching, carefully, the same way we watched Denise's well before we knew for certain what we were looking at."

Priya considered the map for a long moment, some of the old procedural caution settling back over her features, the same discipline that had carried them through the entire Denise investigation without a single misstep costing them the case. "Watching only," she said finally. "Nobody approaches. Nobody goes onto that land alone, or at all, until we understand a great deal more than we do right now. If this is where they're staying, and if they're as patient and careful as everything we've learned suggests, then walking up to that door without a plan is a mistake that gets someone taken instead of getting answers."

"Agreed," Mara said. "I only want eyes on it. See who comes and goes, if anyone does. Build the same kind of pattern we built with Denise before we ever considered doing anything more direct."

Dev shifted under his blanket, some of the old restlessness surfacing despite his exhaustion. "How long do we have before watching isn't enough anymore. Before we actually have to do something more than log what we see."

"That depends on what the outbuilding tells us," Priya said. "If it's active, if people are actually coming and going from it, that changes the timeline. If it's empty, or if it turns out to be nothing at all, we've spent days chasing a dead end while the clock keeps running regardless. I don't have a clean answer for you, Dev. I wish I did."

Nobody answered immediately, and the silence stretched long enough that the fire had time to pop twice before Priya finally spoke, her voice quieter than usual, carrying the weight of a deadline none of them could ignore any longer.

"Five weeks," she said. "That's how long until the eclipse now. That's how long we have to turn watching into something that actually saves you, Dev, instead of just documenting how it happened after the fact."

Caleb reached over and set his hand on Dev's shoulder again, the same steady weight he'd offered every day since the first blackout. "We've turned less into more before," he said quietly. "A drought file and an old letter turned into enough to save Denise. We'll make five weeks count the same way."

The number sat heavy in the room, heavier than it had felt even a week ago, now that a stranger's face and a car with a number that shouldn't exist had made the whole abstract danger suddenly, uncomfortably concrete.

Wren closed her notebook slowly, looking around the table at each of them in turn, her usual bluntness softened by something closer to determination than fear. "Five weeks is longer than Ruth Calloway ever got to understand what was happening to her husband's memory. Longer than Adelaide got, in the end, to understand her own bargain before it consumed the rest of her life. We've got more time than most of the people this practice has ever touched, and more people willing to fight for one of us than any of them ever had waiting on their side."

"That's a generous way to frame a countdown," Caleb said, but there was warmth under the dry note in his voice, the same steady humor Dev had come to rely on more than he'd admitted to anyone.

"I'm not trying to be generous," Wren said. "I'm trying to be accurate. We keep talking about how much time we don't have. I think it's worth remembering, every so often, how much more we actually have than anyone who came before us."

Dev looked up at that, something in his exhausted face shifting slightly toward a version of hope he hadn't let himself fully feel since the well ritual first started this whole unraveling. "Five weeks," he repeated, testing the shape of the number the way he'd tested every other hard fact this past week. "Okay. Then that's what we use. All of it."

Outside, the early frost had returned to the grass again, thin and silver in the last of the daylight, counting down the same way everything else in this valley now seemed to be counting down, patient and unhurried and entirely indifferent to how ready any of them actually felt.
