CHAPTER TWENTY-SIX

The blackout took Dev on a Tuesday afternoon, three weeks and two days out from the eclipse, while he was doing nothing more dangerous than carrying a crate of apples from the orchard shed to Mara's truck, a task the group had approved specifically because it kept him within sight of the house and required no more than four minutes outside the fence line.

Priya was watching from the kitchen window when it happened. One moment he was setting the crate down on the truck bed, steady on his feet, and the next his hands had simply stopped moving, the crate balanced at an angle that should have tipped and didn't, his whole body gone still in a way that had nothing restful in it at all. She was out the back door before she'd consciously decided to move, years of procedural training collapsing into pure animal urgency the second she saw him standing there empty behind his own eyes.

By the time she reached him, he was already walking. Not toward the house. Toward the tree line at the edge of the orchard, slow and deliberate, the crate abandoned in the truck bed, his gait wrong in a way she'd learned to recognize across these past weeks, present in his body but absent from whatever was steering it.

"Dev," she said, keeping her voice level the way Caleb had coached her to, no panic in it, panic only ever seemed to make the blackouts worse. "Dev, can you hear me."

He didn't answer, didn't slow, and that was when she saw the man standing just inside the tree line, unhurried and unremarkable in exactly the way Odette had warned them the quiet family would always be, watching Dev's approach with the patient stillness of someone who had done this before and knew better than to rush it.

"Caleb," Priya called, not shouting, because shouting felt like it might snap something in Dev she couldn't afford to have snapped, but pitched to carry through the open kitchen window all the same. "Caleb, now."

The man in the trees didn't retreat at the sound of another name being called. If anything he seemed to relax slightly, as though a witness changed nothing about what he'd come to do. Dev kept walking, six feet from the tree line now, five, his face slack and unguided, and Priya broke into a run despite every instinct telling her that running might be exactly the wrong instinct here.

"Dev," the man said, and his voice was gentle, almost kind, the same practiced warmth Odette had described from the porch confrontation weeks earlier. "You don't have to fight this part. You've been fighting so hard for so long. This is the part where you get to stop."

"Don't," Priya said, closing the distance, close enough now to see that Dev's eyes were open but unfocused, tracking nothing, his feet still carrying him forward one slow step at a time toward a voice that had found the one door in him nobody had thought to guard, the door that only opened when he wasn't there to stand behind it himself.

"I'm not taking anything from him that isn't already his," the man said, still to Dev and not to her, as though she weren't quite real enough in this moment to warrant a direct answer. "I'm offering him the chance to stop carrying something that was never supposed to be his burden alone. You've seen what the group is planning, haven't you, Dev. Nine strangers standing around you like a fence, each one paying a piece of your price so you don't have to pay it yourself. Doesn't that feel like a mercy that only delays the real cost onto someone else's grandchild. Doesn't part of you already know that."

Dev's steps slowed, just slightly, and for one terrible second Priya thought it was hesitation working in the man's favor rather than against him, some blank, undefended part of Dev actually turning the words over and finding them persuasive in a way the waking, guarded version of him never would have allowed.

"Dev," Priya said again, closer now, close enough to reach for his arm, and she did, gripping it hard enough that it would leave a mark, not to pull him but simply to be there, present and solid and undeniable, a fact his body might register even where his mind couldn't currently follow. "I need you to hear my voice. Not his. Mine. You know my voice."

For a moment nothing changed. Dev's arm was rigid under her hand, his eyes still fixed on some middle distance past the tree line that had nothing to do with either of them, and the man watched this small contest with an expression of patient, almost pitying calm, as though he'd seen this exact resistance fail before and was simply waiting out the last of it.

Then Caleb was there, moving faster than Priya had ever seen him move, placing himself bodily between Dev and the tree line without touching Dev at all, his whole frame a wall the man would have had to go through rather than around.

"You need to leave," Caleb said, and there was nothing gentle in his voice at all now, the plainspoken steadiness Priya knew from a hundred quieter moments sharpened into something with real edge in it. "Whatever you think you're offering him, he doesn't get to actually choose it right now, not like this, not with no more say in it than a man talking in his sleep. You know that. That's exactly why you waited for a moment like this instead of asking him standing up, wide awake, with people around him who love him. That's not persuasion. That's theft with better manners."

Something shifted in the man's face then, not anger, something closer to genuine, weary disappointment, as though he'd hoped for a cleaner outcome and was disappointed rather than defeated by its absence.

"I'm not your enemy," he said. "I know that's hard to believe, given how this looks. But every day you wait is a day closer to the same failure Denise almost caused, scattered across a smaller circle instead of one careless well. We're offering something that actually ends, not something that just moves the debt to whoever's unlucky enough to be born into it two generations from now."

"Then make that argument to him standing up," Caleb said. "Make it to his face, in daylight, with his sister holding his hand and knowing exactly what you're saying to him. If it's really as kind as you claim, it'll survive him being awake to hear it."

The man looked past Caleb's shoulder at Dev one more time, at the way Priya's grip on his arm hadn't loosened, at the stillness of him standing there caught between two pulls he had no current say in at all, and something in his patience finally seemed to run out, not into anger but into a kind of tired retreat.

"He'll remember wanting to say yes," the man said quietly, already stepping back into the tree line's deeper shadow. "Even if he doesn't remember this exact conversation clearly. A stubborn part of him heard it, and a quieter part of him agreed, if only for a moment. That part doesn't go away just because you got here in time today."

Then he was gone, moving back through the trees with the same unhurried pace he'd arrived with, and a moment later Dev's knees buckled entirely, Priya and Caleb both catching him before he hit the ground, his body suddenly, fully present again, blinking up at them with the raw confusion that always followed a blackout, made sharper this time by something underneath it that looked almost like shame.

"What happened," Dev said, his voice thin. "I was carrying the crate. Then I was on the ground."

Priya knelt beside him in the grass, not letting go of his arm even now that the danger standing behind it had retreated back into the trees. "You were walking toward the orchard fence," she said carefully. "There was a man. From the quiet family. He was talking to you the whole time you weren't there to hear it properly."

Dev's face went through several things in quick succession, confusion sliding into horror sliding into something quieter and worse, a kind of exhausted, frightened grief settling in underneath it all. "What did I do," he asked. "Was I walking toward him. Was I going with him."

"You were walking," Caleb said, crouching down on Dev's other side, his voice steady even now, refusing to soften the truth the way he'd told Dev weeks ago he wouldn't. "Slowly. Priya got to you before it went any further than that. You didn't get close enough to actually leave with him, if that's what you're asking."

"But part of me was going," Dev said, and it wasn't a question, his voice cracking slightly on the last word. "He said something like that, didn't he. That part of me agreed. I can feel that he's not lying about that part. There's something in me right now that feels like it already said yes to something, even though the rest of me is horrified by it."

Priya's hand tightened on his arm. "That's not you," she said. "That's the blackout finding the one door in you that isn't guarded, because it can't be guarded, because you're not there to stand at it. That's not a real yes. A yes needs someone standing behind it who actually chose it."

"Does it matter," Dev said, staring up at the orchard fence like it had personally betrayed him. "If part of me can be made to want it, even for a few seconds, without me there to stop it. Doesn't that mean the fear I've been carrying this whole time was right. That whoever survives this isn't actually going to be entirely me."

Nobody answered that right away, because nobody had an honest answer that wasn't also a hard one, and by now this household had learned the difference between comfort and truth well enough to know which one Dev actually needed lying in the grass with his knees still too unsteady to stand.

"It means we tell Yusuf today," Priya said finally, helping him sit up slowly, keeping one arm braced firmly around his shoulders. "Not tomorrow. Today. Whatever information got that man here at exactly the four minutes you were outside the fence, whatever's still leaking even after Elias, we find it now, before three weeks becomes two and this happens again somewhere we can't get to you in time."

Dev nodded slowly, some of the horror in his face settling into something harder and more determined, the same edge Priya had heard in his voice at the kitchen table days earlier when he'd insisted on helping choose the ring himself.

"Today," he agreed. "And Priya. Whatever that door in me is, the one that can be talked to without me standing behind it. I want it found and shut before the eclipse. I don't care what it costs to find it."

Caleb helped him to his feet, one hand steady under his elbow, and the walk back to the house took longer than it should have, Dev's legs still unsteady enough that Caleb kept his grip firm the whole way. None of them spoke again until they'd reached the porch steps, the same steps where the quiet family's earlier visitor had once cornered him with her own version of this offer. Dev stopped there without being asked, looking back once at the tree line, empty now, the man long gone back into whatever patient waiting he did between attempts.

"He knew exactly which four minutes to be there," Dev said quietly. "Not roughly. Exactly. That's not luck, and it isn't something Elias could still be feeding them from wherever he is now, watched the way Yusuf's watching him. That's newer information. Somebody still inside knows my schedule down to the minute."

"Or something inside the schedule itself gave it away," Priya said, thinking out loud now, the procedural part of her mind already working even through the fear still sitting heavy in her chest. "We approved this exact task, this exact stretch of time, in a meeting three days ago. Five people in that room besides you and me. I don't want to think it's one of them. But I don't get to not think about it either."

Dev's jaw tightened at that, a fresh weight settling onto a day that had already asked more of him than any single afternoon should have to hold. "Another Elias," he said. "That's what you're saying. Not the same person, but the same shape of problem, hiding somewhere inside the same five people we trust with everything."

"I'm saying we don't know yet," Priya said. "And I'm saying we tell Yusuf everything, today, and let Mara's structural instincts loose on the timing the same way she mapped the quiet family the first time. If there's a pattern, she'll find it faster than either of us sitting here guessing."

They found Mara and Wren still at the kitchen table, the abort-signal sketch spread out between them, and the moment Priya's face came through the door both women were on their feet before a single word had been said, some shared, hard-won instinct for reading each other's fear replacing the need for explanation.

"Tell us," Mara said, already reaching for a fresh sheet of paper, and Priya did, the whole thing in order, the crate and the tree line and the exact four minutes the group's own schedule had handed the quiet family without anyone meaning to.

Wren went very still partway through, her jaw tightening in a way Priya recognized from the porch conversation days earlier, the look of someone recalculating a risk she'd thought she'd already accounted for. "That changes the timeline for the ring," she said quietly, once Priya had finished. "If they can reach him this close, this precisely, we can't wait until we're fully ready to start briefing candidates. We need at least the first few standing by before he's alone again, even behind the fence."

"Then we move faster," Mara said, already sketching, the pencil moving with the same steady, unhurried certainty it always did even when everything around her felt like it was accelerating past anyone's control. "I'll cross-check the meeting attendance against every incident since Elias was caught. If the leak is still coming from inside that room, I'll find the shape of it."

Dev sank into the nearest chair, the last of the day's adrenaline finally draining out of him all at once, leaving something hollow and exhausted in its place. "Three weeks," he said, half to himself, the number sounding heavier now than it had that morning. "Three weeks, and I still don't know how many of me are going to be standing at the end of it."

Caleb sat down beside him without a word, the same steady, wordless presence he'd offered on the porch nights before, and this time nobody reached for comfort or explanation, because some afternoons only asked to be sat through, together, until the shaking finally stopped.
