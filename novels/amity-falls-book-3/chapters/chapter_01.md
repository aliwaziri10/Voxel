CHAPTER ONE

The first blackout lasted four minutes, though Dev didn't know that until later, and when it happened he didn't even notice anything had gone wrong until he looked down and found the wheelbarrow already half full of gravel he had no memory of shoveling.

He was working the substation's back lot that morning, laying the new drainage bed Yusuf had approved two weeks earlier, and the last thing he remembered clearly was setting the wheelbarrow down at the edge of the trench, checking the slope with a level the way Priya had taught him to check everything twice before trusting it once. Then nothing. Then gravel, half a load of it, and his own hands raw and dusty in a way that told him he'd been working steadily the whole time, just without him anywhere behind his own eyes to witness it.

"You alright?" Marcus, the other hand on the crew, was watching him from a few feet off, brow furrowed. "You've been standing there a minute. Staring at the wheelbarrow like it bit you."

"Fine," Dev said, and made himself smile, the automatic, practiced smile he'd gotten good at over the past year, the one that told people nothing was wrong even when he wasn't sure himself. "Just thinking about the slope. Want to make sure we don't have to redo it."

Marcus shrugged and went back to his own section, and Dev stood there a moment longer, running his tongue over dry lips, trying to piece together four minutes that simply weren't there. Not fuzzy. Not dreamlike. Gone, the way a page torn cleanly from a book leaves no ragged edge behind, just a gap between two pages that otherwise fit together perfectly.

He told himself it was nothing. Heat, maybe, though the morning had barely climbed out of the cool end of autumn. Low blood sugar, skipped breakfast, too many hours logged this week trying to prove to himself as much as anyone else that he could be trusted with a full workload again. He finished the drainage bed by noon, checked the slope three times instead of twice, and did not tell anyone what had happened.

He almost made it through the whole day that way.

The second one hit while he was walking home, just past the old grange hall, and this time there was no wheelbarrow to fill the gap with evidence, only a stretch of road he had apparently walked the length of without remembering a single step of it. He came back to himself standing at his own front gate, keys already in his hand though he had no memory of pulling them from his pocket, the last of the daylight gone from gold to the deep blue that came right before full dark.

He'd lost, by his best guess, close to twenty minutes.

Dev stood at the gate for a long time after that, not moving, listening to his own heartbeat climb the way it hadn't climbed since the worst of his first year back, since the nights he used to lie awake convinced every knock at the door was someone finally coming to tell him he'd been wrong to think he'd earned a second chance at all.

This felt different from that fear, though. That fear had always come with a clear shape, a specific dread of specific consequences. This felt like something moving underneath him that had no shape at all yet, only weight.

He didn't sleep well that night, and when Priya called the next morning to ask if he wanted to come by for dinner, he almost told her everything right then, the words sitting right at the back of his throat, ready. Then he thought about how it would sound. Two blackouts, no injury, no obvious cause, coming from the one person in this family with an actual history of people needing to watch him carefully.

"I'm good," he said instead. "Little tired. Long week at the substation."

"You sure?" Something in her voice sharpened slightly, the deputy underneath the sister never fully off duty even on an ordinary phone call. "You sound off."

"I'm sure." He made himself sound steadier than he felt. "I'll come by Thursday like we planned. Promise."

He hung up before she could press further, and stood in his kitchen with the phone still warm in his hand, telling himself the same thing he'd told himself all day. It was nothing. It would pass. He'd earned exactly enough trust this past year to finally start believing good things about his own future, and he wasn't ready to hand any of that back over a couple of strange, empty minutes that probably meant nothing at all.

The third blackout came two days later, in the middle of the afternoon, with no warning and no obvious trigger, and this time it didn't just take minutes.

He came back to himself sitting on the ground, his back against the old stone wall that ran along the edge of the Voss property, though he had absolutely no memory of walking anywhere near it, let alone sitting down there. The light had shifted dramatically since the last thing he actually remembered, which had been standing in his own kitchen making coffee sometime around ten in the morning. Now the sun hung low and orange behind the western ridge, throwing long shadows across ground he didn't recognize walking, only recognized now, from where he sat, dazed and cold and deeply, thoroughly frightened.

Hours. He'd lost hours.

His hands were shaking when he finally managed to stand, and there was a strange, unfamiliar taste at the back of his mouth, metallic and faintly sweet, like biting into something that wasn't quite food. His head throbbed with a headache unlike any he'd had before, less like ordinary pain and more like pressure, something pushing outward from somewhere behind his own eyes that had no business being there.

He looked down at his own hands and found, tucked into his palm, fingers curled tight around it without his knowledge or consent, a small, smooth river stone, worn perfectly round by decades of water that hadn't run this close to the Voss well in years.

He had no memory of picking it up. He had no memory of walking here at all. But some part of him, some part that had apparently been driving his body around for the better part of six hours without him, had chosen this exact spot, this exact stone, out of an entire valley full of ground he could have wandered instead.

Dev stood there a long moment, the stone warm now from his own closed fist, staring out at the darkening fields toward home, and felt, for the first time since he'd come back to this valley and started the long, careful work of becoming someone worth trusting again, a fear that had nothing at all to do with his own past mistakes.

This fear was new. This fear felt like it belonged to something else entirely, something old, something that had been waiting, patiently, for a door inside him to finally come open.

He put the stone in his pocket rather than leaving it behind, though he couldn't have said why even if someone had asked him, and started the long walk home in the last of the fading light, already rehearsing, without fully admitting to himself that he was doing it, exactly how he might finally explain this to his sister.
