CHAPTER ONE

The first blackout lasted four minutes, though Dev didn't know that until later, and when it happened he didn't even notice anything had gone wrong until he looked down and found the wheelbarrow already half full of gravel he had no memory of shoveling.

He was working the substation's back lot that morning, laying the new drainage bed Yusuf had approved two weeks earlier, one of the first jobs the council had trusted him with that carried his name alone on the paperwork rather than a supervisor's signature backing his own. It had taken most of a year to get here. A year of showing up early, leaving late, taking the jobs nobody else wanted, letting Yusuf watch him work from a careful distance for months before finally handing him something that mattered without someone standing over his shoulder the whole time. The drainage bed was small, unglamorous, a job most people in this valley wouldn't think twice about, but Dev had treated every foot of it like a test he couldn't afford to fail, because in a town this size a man's whole reputation could still turn on whether a drainage bed held through the first hard rain.

He hadn't expected the work itself to be the hard part. The hard part, all year, had been the small daily arithmetic of rebuilding trust one unremarkable task at a time, showing up to jobs that carried no glory and no forgiveness either if he got them wrong, proving something over and over to people who had every right to still be watching him closely. He'd stopped keeping count of the days somewhere around month four, not because he'd stopped caring how long it had been, but because counting had started to feel like doubting himself out loud, and he'd promised Priya, in the early weeks, that he'd try to stop doing that where anyone could see.

The last thing he remembered clearly was setting the wheelbarrow down at the edge of the trench, checking the slope with a level the way Priya had taught him to check everything twice before trusting it once. Then nothing. Then gravel, half a load of it, and his own hands raw and dusty in a way that told him he'd been working steadily the whole time, just without him anywhere behind his own eyes to witness it. The level was still sitting exactly where he'd set it, bubble centered, the slope apparently checked and approved by whatever version of him had been standing here without his knowledge.

"You alright?" Marcus, the other hand on the crew, was watching him from a few feet off, brow furrowed, a bag of quick-set mix balanced on one shoulder. "You've been standing there a minute. Staring at the wheelbarrow like it bit you."

"Fine," Dev said, and made himself smile, the automatic, practiced smile he'd gotten good at over the past year, the one that told people nothing was wrong even when he wasn't sure himself. "Just thinking about the slope. Want to make sure we don't have to redo it."

Marcus shrugged and went back to his own section, and Dev stood there a moment longer, running his tongue over dry lips, trying to piece together four minutes that simply weren't there. Not fuzzy. Not dreamlike. Gone, the way a page torn cleanly from a book leaves no ragged edge behind, just a gap between two pages that otherwise fit together perfectly. He crouched down and ran his fingers along the freshly laid gravel bed, checking it the way he'd been taught, and found it not just adequate but genuinely well done, evenly graded, packed at exactly the angle the drainage plan called for. Whatever had been steering him for those four minutes had known what it was doing, which somehow frightened him more than if he'd found the work sloppy or half-finished.

He told himself it was nothing. Heat, maybe, though the morning had barely climbed out of the cool end of autumn. Low blood sugar, skipped breakfast, too many hours logged this week trying to prove to himself as much as anyone else that he could be trusted with a full workload again. He finished the drainage bed by noon, checked the slope three times instead of twice, and did not tell anyone what had happened, not even during the lunch break when Marcus asked, casually, if he'd been sleeping alright lately.

"Fine," he said again, the word starting to feel worn smooth from overuse, like a coin rubbed thin by too many hands.

He almost made it through the whole day that way. Yusuf stopped by the site in the early afternoon, walking the trench line with the slow, deliberate attention of a man who'd learned the hard way not to take anyone's competence on faith, and crouched to run his own thumb along the gravel bed the same way Dev had.

"Good work," Yusuf said, straightening up. "Better than good, actually. I might start giving you the jobs the county inspector actually bothers to look at twice."

"I'd like that," Dev said, and meant it, though the words came out quieter than he intended, some part of him still bracing for the sentence to end differently than it had, the way it so often had a year ago, with a qualifier attached, a reminder of what he still had to prove.

Yusuf just nodded once, like the compliment had cost him nothing to give and required nothing further from either of them, and moved on to the next section of the crew, and Dev stood there a moment in the fading warmth of that small, ordinary kindness, trying not to let it get tangled up with the four minutes he still couldn't account for.

The second one hit while he was walking home, just past the old grange hall, and this time there was no wheelbarrow to fill the gap with evidence, only a stretch of road he had apparently walked the length of without remembering a single step of it. He came back to himself standing at his own front gate, keys already in his hand though he had no memory of pulling them from his pocket, the last of the daylight gone from gold to the deep blue that came right before full dark. A neighbor's dog was barking somewhere down the lane, and the sound of it took a long, disorienting moment to resolve into something ordinary and explainable rather than one more piece of a world that had briefly stopped making sense around him.

He'd lost, by his best guess, close to twenty minutes.

Dev stood at the gate for a long time after that, not moving, listening to his own heartbeat climb the way it hadn't climbed since the worst of his first year back, since the nights he used to lie awake convinced every knock at the door was someone finally coming to tell him he'd been wrong to think he'd earned a second chance at all. He made himself walk the last few steps to his own front door slowly, deliberately, testing each motion like a man checking whether a bridge would hold his weight.

This felt different from that fear, though. That fear had always come with a clear shape, a defined dread of consequences he could name and brace against. This felt like a slow pressure moving underneath him with no shape at all yet, patient and unhurried in a way that made it worse than a threat he could actually see coming.

He didn't sleep well that night, lying awake replaying the walk home from memory that wasn't actually memory, just guesswork stitched together from where he'd started and where he'd ended up, and when Priya called the next morning to ask if he wanted to come by for dinner, he almost told her everything right then, the words sitting right at the back of his throat, ready. His thumb hovered over her name in his contacts, some instinct in his hand wanting to reach for her the way he might once have reached for a drink, badly and without a plan for what came after. Then he thought about how it would sound. Two blackouts, no injury, no obvious cause, coming from the one person in this family with an actual history of people needing to watch him carefully, back when watching him carefully had meant something very different than it might mean now.

"I'm good," he said instead. "Little tired. Long week at the substation."

"You sure?" Something in her voice sharpened slightly, the deputy underneath the sister never fully off duty even on an ordinary phone call. "You sound off."

"I'm sure." He made himself sound steadier than he felt, pressing the phone a little tighter against his ear as if that might help the lie land better. "I'll come by Thursday like we planned. Promise."

He hung up before she could press further, and stood in his kitchen with the phone still warm in his hand, telling himself the same thing he'd told himself all day. It was nothing. It would pass. He'd earned exactly enough trust this past year to finally start believing good things about his own future, and he wasn't ready to hand any of that back over a couple of strange, empty minutes that probably meant nothing at all. He made himself a plain dinner he barely tasted and went to bed early, hoping sleep might reset whatever small, fragile thing had come loose inside him.

The third blackout came two days later, in the middle of the afternoon, with no warning and no obvious trigger, and this time it didn't just take minutes.

He came back to himself sitting on the ground, his back against the old stone wall that ran along the edge of the Voss property, though he had absolutely no memory of walking anywhere near it, let alone sitting down there. The light had shifted dramatically since the last thing he actually remembered, which had been standing in his own kitchen making coffee sometime around ten in the morning. Now the sun hung low and orange behind the western ridge, throwing long shadows across ground he didn't recognize walking, only recognized now, from where he sat, dazed and cold and deeply, thoroughly frightened.

Hours. He'd lost hours.

His hands were shaking when he finally managed to stand, legs unsteady beneath him like he'd been sitting far longer than made physical sense, and there was a strange, unfamiliar taste at the back of his mouth, metallic and faintly sweet, like biting into something that wasn't quite food. His head throbbed with a headache unlike any he'd had before, less like ordinary pain and more like pressure, something pushing outward from somewhere behind his own eyes that had no business being there, a sensation he had no frame of reference for at all.

He looked down at his own hands and found, tucked into his palm, fingers curled tight around it without his knowledge or consent, a small, smooth river stone, worn perfectly round by decades of water that hadn't run this close to the Voss well in years, the streambed long since shifted somewhere further downslope.

He had no memory of picking it up. He had no memory of walking here at all. But whatever had been driving his body around for the better part of six hours without him had chosen this exact spot, this exact stone, out of an entire valley full of ground he could have wandered instead, as if the choosing itself meant something he wasn't yet equipped to understand.

Dev pulled his phone from his pocket with hands that still wouldn't quite steady, thumb hovering over Priya's name in his contacts for a long, suspended moment. He got as far as opening the call screen before he put the phone away again, telling himself he'd wait until Thursday, until he had more than four minutes and twenty minutes and now this to explain, until he could walk into whatever conversation came next with something more useful to offer than his own fear.

Dev stood there a long moment, the stone warm now from his own closed fist, staring out at the darkening fields toward home, and felt, for the first time since he'd come back to this valley and started the long, careful work of becoming someone worth trusting again, a fear that had nothing at all to do with his own past mistakes, nothing to do with the version of himself he'd spent a year trying to leave behind.

This fear was new. This fear felt like it belonged to something else entirely, something old, that had been waiting, patiently, for a door inside him to finally come open, a door he hadn't even known existed until whatever stood on the other side of it had already started testing the hinges.

He put the stone in his pocket rather than leaving it behind, though he couldn't have said why even if someone had asked him, some instinct insisting it mattered, that leaving it here in the grass would be a kind of carelessness he couldn't afford. He started the long walk home in the last of the fading light, already rehearsing, without fully admitting to himself that he was doing it, exactly how he might finally explain this to his sister, and wondering, with a dread that had nothing to do with her reaction and everything to do with his own, what she would see in his face the moment he finally tried.

He didn't go straight home. He found himself walking the long way instead, past the substation where the day had started so ordinarily, past the grange hall where the second blackout had taken twenty minutes he still couldn't account for, tracing the shape of his own lost time the way a man might walk the perimeter of a house fire looking for the point where it started. None of it told him anything useful. The substation looked the same as it always did, windows dark, Priya's cruiser gone for the night. The grange hall sat quiet and unlit, waiting for whatever the next town meeting would ask of it. Ordinary buildings, holding no answers, indifferent to the fact that something inside him had apparently used them as landmarks for a journey he hadn't chosen to take.

By the time he reached his own porch, full dark had settled over the valley, and he sat down on the top step rather than going inside right away, turning the river stone over in his palm in the porch light, studying it as though it might eventually explain itself if he looked at it long enough. It didn't. It just sat there, smooth and ordinary and entirely unremarkable, a stone a hundred children had probably skipped across the old streambed a hundred years ago, giving up nothing about why some part of him had needed to hold it, or what it might mean that his hands had chosen it without asking his permission first.

He thought about calling Priya again, right then, porch light buzzing faintly overhead, the stone still warm from his own grip. He thought about how she'd sound if he told her everything at once, all three blackouts stacked together instead of doled out one manageable piece at a time, and decided, for tonight, that he wasn't ready to watch her face do whatever it would do when she heard it. Thursday. He'd tell her Thursday, in person, where she could see for herself that he was still standing, still himself, still the man who'd spent a year proving he could be trusted with small things before anyone handed him anything larger.

He went inside eventually, set the stone on his kitchen windowsill where he could see it from the sink, and lay awake long after that, listening to his own house settle around him, waiting for a fourth blackout that didn't come that night, though some part of him lay there anyway, bracing for it, the way a man braces for a storm he can already smell on the wind long before the first drop actually falls.
