CHAPTER NINE

Yusuf's office still smelled faintly of the same cedar polish it had the day Priya first walked in to file a formal inquiry against a woman he'd known his whole life, and he thought about that smell now, sitting across from a different family in crisis, understanding with a clarity that hadn't been available to him two years ago exactly how badly hesitation could cost people who trusted him to move faster than his own fear. The office itself hadn't changed much since then, the same worn map of the valley pinned to the wall behind his desk, the same low bookshelf crowded with council records going back generations, but Yusuf himself felt like a different man sitting behind that desk now, one who'd learned the hard way what his own caution had nearly cost.

"Walk me through the timeline again," he said. "All of it. From the first blackout to this afternoon. I want it in order, and I want every detail, even the ones that seem too small to matter."

Priya laid it out with the same procedural steadiness she brought to everything, four minutes, then twenty, then several hours, and now an entire afternoon, each gap wider than the last, no rhythm to the spacing at all except a trend that pointed in only one direction, a pattern that refused to offer the comfort of predictability.

"Four days," Yusuf said quietly, once she'd finished, doing the arithmetic slowly, deliberately, as if saying the numbers aloud might somehow make them less alarming. "Between the third blackout and this one. Four days, and the gap nearly doubled from hours to a full afternoon."

"Denise's cycle ran six to eight years between renewals," Mara said, leaning forward slightly in her chair, the engineer in her already working the problem the way she worked any structural failure, looking for the load path that explained the collapse. "Even accounting for the fact that hers was chosen and controlled, this isn't a smaller, faster version of the same pattern. This is something that's accelerating on its own, independent of anything anyone is doing to manage it, growing worse with a momentum that has nothing to do with training or restraint."

Yusuf looked at Dev, sitting quiet and pale in the chair beside Priya, his hands folded tightly in his lap, and felt something tighten in his chest that had nothing to do with council politics or procedure, something closer to the plain, uncomplicated fear of watching a young man he'd known his whole life slowly running out of time. "How are you feeling right now. Honestly. Not the answer you think I want to hear."

"Tired," Dev said, his voice flat with exhaustion. "Tired in a way sleep doesn't fix, like the tiredness lives somewhere sleep can't reach anymore. And scared that the next gap is going to be longer than four hours, and scared that whatever I see when I come back is going to be worse than a stranger's hands holding something dark in a stone room. I keep waiting to wake up from one of these and not recognize my own name."

Yusuf nodded slowly, absorbing that, letting the weight of it settle fully before responding, and Mara watched him make a decision in real time, watched the careful, cautious man who had once needed weeks of formal process before authorizing an inquiry into Denise finally choose speed over procedure without a moment's visible hesitation, something in his posture straightening as the decision crystallized.

"I'm authorizing full council resources, effective immediately," Yusuf said. "Not a formal inquiry. Not a committee. Not a review process that takes weeks to produce a decision everyone already knows is coming. Direct support. Whatever Mara needs to build her structural map of this, whatever Wren needs to do her reading safely, whatever medical resources exist that might help stabilize Dev physically while the rest of this gets worked out, all of it, starting today, no waiting for anyone's approval but mine, and I'll answer for that decision to anyone on the council who wants to question it."

"Last time," Priya said carefully, choosing her words with the precision of someone who remembered exactly how much that delay had cost, "it took months to get this kind of authorization. Denise's case sat in review for weeks before anyone with real power was willing to move, weeks that nearly cost Caleb everything, weeks I still think about sometimes."

"I know exactly how long it took," Yusuf said, something hard and honest in his voice, no defensiveness in it at all, only plain acknowledgment. "I was the one slowing it down, out of fear of being wrong about someone I respected, out of fear of what moving too fast might cost if I was mistaken about Denise, about all of it. I nearly cost this valley everything with that hesitation, and I've spent two years since telling myself I'd never make that mistake again if I got a second chance to prove it, though I never expected the proof to come this soon, or to matter this much to someone I've watched grow up in this valley." He looked directly at Dev, holding his gaze with a seriousness that filled the small office. "This is that chance. I'm not going to sit on this the way I sat on Denise's case. Four hours is too long a gap to still be treating this like something that can wait for careful process, and I refuse to let procedure be the reason we lose more time than we already have."

Wren, who had been quiet through most of the meeting, sitting cross-legged in the corner chair with a stack of old ledgers balanced on her knee, leaned forward. "I found something in the almanac records last night. Old entries, going back generations, nothing that explains itself outright, the kind of thing you'd miss entirely if you weren't already looking for a pattern, but a pattern if you know what you're looking for. Every uncontrolled awakening the valley's ever recorded, and there are a handful, scattered and vague, some of them barely more than a sentence in a margin somewhere, clusters around an autumn eclipse."

The room went very still, the ordinary sounds of the building outside the door suddenly loud in the silence.

"There's one coming," Wren continued. "Six weeks from now. I checked twice because I didn't want to believe it the first time, and then I checked a third time because two confirmations somehow didn't feel like enough."

"You think that's the deadline," Caleb said, his voice carefully even despite the tension visibly gathering in his shoulders. "Not just a pattern. An actual timeline this is building toward, something with an end point already fixed."

"I think it's more than a pattern," Wren said. "I think whatever's happening to Dev is tied to something bigger than just his own body deciding to accelerate on its own schedule. I think the eclipse might be when it's meant to finish, one way or another, and every gap getting wider between now and then isn't random. It's counting down, like a clock nobody could see until now, ticking toward something that's been fixed in motion for longer than any of us have been alive."

Dev's hands had gone white-knuckled on the arms of his chair, his knuckles pale against the worn wood. "Meant to finish how."

"I don't know yet," Wren admitted, and for once there was no bravado in the admission, only plain honesty. "But I think we need to find out fast, because six weeks sounded like plenty of time until about thirty seconds ago, and now it sounds like nowhere near enough, not with gaps doubling every four days."

Caleb had said little through most of the meeting, standing near the window with his arms crossed, but now he stepped forward, his voice steady despite the tightness in his jaw. "I want to be the one with him tonight. Not because I don't trust Priya to handle it, I trust her more than almost anyone. But I know what it's like to lose time you can't get back, and I think Dev needs someone in the room who understands that from the inside, not just from watching it happen to someone else."

Dev looked up at that, something grateful and worn moving across his exhausted face. "I'd like that. If it's not too much to ask."

"It's not," Caleb said simply. "You sat with me plenty, after the well, when I still couldn't tell which memories were mine and which weren't. I owe you a night or two of the same."

Yusuf stood, something decisive settling into his posture that Mara hadn't seen from him since the night of the well itself, the same quality of a man who'd finally stopped weighing options and started acting on the one he trusted. "Then we don't waste a single day of it. Mara, I want your structural map built as fast as you can build it, every pattern, every gap, every connection to the Whitlock line we can trace, no detail too small to include. Wren, keep pulling every record the almanac has on prior eclipses and prior awakenings, no matter how old or fragmentary, and bring anything you find straight to me the moment you find it. Priya, Caleb, I want the two of you focused directly on Dev, on his safety, day to day, hour to hour if it comes to that, someone with him at all times starting tonight. And Dev." He turned to face him fully, crouching slightly so they were nearly eye level. "I want you to understand that this council failed someone before, badly, through hesitation and fear of getting it wrong. I am not going to fail you the same way. You have every resource this valley has, starting this moment, and I intend to prove that means something, not just to you, but to myself."

Dev's composure finally broke, just slightly, a single unsteady breath escaping him that was half relief and half a fear too large to fully release even now, his eyes glassy with unshed tears he seemed determined not to let fall. "Thank you," he managed. "I know that's not enough, but thank you, for actually listening, for not making me convince you the way it sounds like everyone had to convince you about Denise."

"Don't thank me yet," Yusuf said, not unkindly, straightening back up. "Thank me once we've actually gotten you through the other side of this. Gratitude's easy to offer at the start of a fight. I'd rather earn it properly, by the end, when there's actually something real to be grateful for."

The meeting broke soon after, each of them carrying their piece of the new urgency out into the fading afternoon light, papers and plans already forming in their heads before they'd even reached the door, and Mara lingered a moment longer in the doorway, watching Yusuf sit back down heavily at his desk, the weight of the decision he'd just made settling visibly onto his shoulders now that the room had emptied and there was no one left to perform certainty for.

"You did the right thing," she said quietly.

"I know I did," Yusuf said, rubbing a hand over his tired eyes. "That's almost the strange part. Last time, I couldn't tell right from cautious until it was nearly too late, until the difference between them had already cost people something irreplaceable. This time it felt obvious the moment Wren mentioned the eclipse, obvious in a way that frightened me almost as much as it clarified things. I just wish obvious hadn't come with a six-week countdown attached to it, and I wish I trusted myself to have moved this fast the first time too, before I had to learn the cost of not moving at all."

Outside, the sky had begun its slow slide toward evening, the same gray-gold Mara had watched settle over Denise's orchard days earlier, except now it carried a different weight entirely, no longer the color of an old story finally being resolved, but the color of a clock that had just started, audibly, to run, every passing minute now something to be accounted for rather than simply lived through.
