CHAPTER NINE

Yusuf's office still smelled faintly of the same cedar polish it had the day Priya first walked in to file a formal inquiry against a woman he'd known his whole life, and he thought about that smell now, sitting across from a different family in crisis, understanding with a clarity that hadn't been available to him two years ago exactly how badly hesitation could cost people who trusted him to move faster than his own fear.

"Walk me through the timeline again," he said. "All of it. From the first blackout to this afternoon."

Priya laid it out with the same procedural steadiness she brought to everything, four minutes, then twenty, then several hours, and now an entire afternoon, each gap wider than the last, no rhythm to the spacing at all except a trend that pointed in only one direction.

"Four days," Yusuf said quietly, once she'd finished. "Between the third blackout and this one. Four days, and the gap nearly doubled."

"Denise's cycle ran six to eight years between renewals," Mara said. "Even accounting for the fact that hers was chosen and controlled, this isn't a smaller, faster version of the same pattern. This is something that's accelerating on its own, independent of anything anyone is doing to manage it."

Yusuf looked at Dev, sitting quiet and pale in the chair beside Priya, and felt something tighten in his chest that had nothing to do with council politics or procedure. "How are you feeling right now. Honestly. Not the answer you think I want."

"Tired," Dev said. "Tired in a way sleep doesn't fix. And scared that the next gap is going to be longer than four hours, and scared that whatever I see when I come back is going to be worse than a stranger's hands holding something dark in a stone room."

Yusuf nodded slowly, absorbing that, and Mara watched him make a decision in real time, watched the careful, cautious man who had once needed weeks of formal process before authorizing an inquiry into Denise finally choose speed over procedure without a moment's visible hesitation.

"I'm authorizing full council resources, effective immediately," Yusuf said. "Not a formal inquiry. Not a committee. Direct support. Whatever Mara needs to build her structural map of this, whatever Wren needs to do her reading safely, whatever medical resources exist that might help stabilize Dev physically while the rest of this gets worked out, all of it, starting today, no waiting for anyone's approval but mine."

"Last time," Priya said carefully, "it took months to get this kind of authorization. Denise's case sat in review for weeks before anyone with real power was willing to move."

"I know exactly how long it took," Yusuf said, something hard and honest in his voice. "I was the one slowing it down, out of fear of being wrong about someone I respected, out of fear of what moving too fast might cost if I was mistaken. I nearly cost this valley everything with that hesitation, and I've spent two years since telling myself I'd never make that mistake again if I got a second chance to prove it." He looked directly at Dev. "This is that chance. I'm not going to sit on this the way I sat on Denise's case. Four hours is too long a gap to still be treating this like something that can wait for careful process."

Wren, who had been quiet through most of the meeting, leaned forward. "I found something in the almanac records last night. Old entries, going back generations, nothing that explains itself outright, but a pattern if you know what you're looking for. Every uncontrolled awakening the valley's ever recorded, and there are a handful, scattered and vague, clusters around an autumn eclipse."

The room went very still.

"There's one coming," Wren continued. "Six weeks from now. I checked twice because I didn't want to believe it the first time."

"You think that's the deadline," Caleb said. "Not just a pattern. An actual timeline this is building toward."

"I think it's more than a pattern," Wren said. "I think whatever's happening to Dev is tied to something bigger than just his own body deciding to accelerate on its own schedule. I think the eclipse might be when it's meant to finish, one way or another, and every gap getting wider between now and then isn't random. It's counting down."

Dev's hands had gone white-knuckled on the arms of his chair. "Meant to finish how."

"I don't know yet," Wren admitted. "But I think we need to find out fast, because six weeks sounded like plenty of time until about thirty seconds ago, and now it sounds like nowhere near enough."

Yusuf stood, something decisive settling into his posture that Mara hadn't seen from him since the night of the well itself. "Then we don't waste a single day of it. Mara, I want your structural map built as fast as you can build it, every pattern, every gap, every connection to the Whitlock line we can trace. Wren, keep pulling every record the almanac has on prior eclipses and prior awakenings, no matter how old or fragmentary. Priya, Caleb, I want the two of you focused directly on Dev, on his safety, day to day, hour to hour if it comes to that. And Dev." He turned to face him fully. "I want you to understand that this council failed someone before, badly, through hesitation and fear of getting it wrong. I am not going to fail you the same way. You have every resource this valley has, starting this moment, and I intend to prove that means something."

Dev's composure finally broke, just slightly, a single unsteady breath escaping him that was half relief and half a fear too large to fully release even now. "Thank you," he managed. "I know that's not enough, but thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Yusuf said, not unkindly. "Thank me once we've actually gotten you through the other side of this. Gratitude's easy to offer at the start of a fight. I'd rather earn it properly, by the end."

The meeting broke soon after, each of them carrying their piece of the new urgency out into the fading afternoon light, and Mara lingered a moment longer in the doorway, watching Yusuf sit back down heavily at his desk, the weight of the decision he'd just made settling visibly onto his shoulders.

"You did the right thing," she said quietly.

"I know I did," Yusuf said. "That's almost the strange part. Last time, I couldn't tell right from cautious until it was nearly too late. This time it felt obvious the moment Wren mentioned the eclipse. I just wish obvious hadn't come with a six-week countdown attached to it."

Outside, the sky had begun its slow slide toward evening, the same gray-gold Mara had watched settle over Denise's orchard days earlier, except now it carried a different weight entirely, no longer the color of an old story finally being resolved, but the color of a clock that had just started, audibly, to run.
