CHAPTER SEVENTEEN

They did the read in Mara's kitchen rather than out at the well, the same table where Wren had done the light read on the stone weeks earlier, though nothing about tonight felt light. Priya had cleared the room of everything but the essentials, a chair for Wren, a chair for herself close enough to reach her in an instant, and Caleb standing by the door with a glass of water and a folded towel, the same two things he'd kept ready during both of Wren's full reads in the Denise investigation.

The button sat in the center of the table on a square of white cloth, small and grey and entirely unremarkable, something that could have come from any coat in any closet in the valley. Wren had found it on the porch step the morning after the woman's visit, half-buried in frost, easy to miss unless a person already knew to look for it. It had sat in an evidence bag in Priya's kitchen drawer for two days while the group argued out whether the risk of a full read was worth taking, and now that the argument had finally settled in favor of trying, the button looked almost absurdly small for the weight everyone in the room was putting on it.

Wren had spent the afternoon preparing the way Priya had taught her after the Denise investigation, eating a full meal first, drinking more water than she wanted, resting an hour with her eyes closed even though she insisted she hadn't slept. Caleb had checked on her twice, quiet and unobtrusive, the same steady presence he'd offered Dev through every blackout this past month. By the time evening came and the kitchen had been cleared for the read itself, Wren looked as ready as she was ever going to look, which Priya had learned, across two prior full reads, still wasn't the same thing as actually being ready.

Wren looked at the button a long moment before she looked at Dev, who stood near the window with his arms crossed, having refused outright to wait upstairs the way Priya had first suggested.

"You don't have to watch this," Wren told him.

"I do," Dev said. "You're doing this because of me. I'm not going to hide from what it costs you."

Wren didn't argue the point, though something in her expression suggested she understood exactly why he'd said it and respected him more for saying it than she was willing to admit out loud. She flexed her fingers once, twice, a habit Priya recognized from every full read she'd ever watched Wren attempt, and then reached out and closed her hand around the button.

For a moment nothing happened. Then her breath caught, sharp and sudden, and her eyes went distant the way they always did when she'd left herself entirely, somewhere none of the rest of them could follow.

"She's not the first," Wren said, her voice gone strange and layered, carrying traces of something older underneath her own. "There have been so many hands. Grandmothers. Daughters. A boy, once, barely older than Dev, given the button before he was ready for what it meant." Her fingers tightened around the small grey shape. "They don't call themselves anything grand. Just the family. The ones who remember what the bargain was always supposed to be, before people started being afraid of the size of it."

"What was it supposed to be," Priya asked, careful and steady, the same voice she used coaxing testimony out of frightened witnesses.

"A closing," Wren said. "Not a wound that keeps reopening every few decades in a different unlucky family. A single, total payment, once, everywhere, forever. They believe the bargain was never meant to be managed the way Denise managed it, doled out carefully across fifty years to keep the cost small enough to bear. They believe that mercy was actually the mistake. Every small, careful taking just delays the real ending and passes the debt forward to someone else's grandchild, the way it passed to Dev." Her breath hitched again, deeper this time. "They've watched it happen for generations. Someone tries to be kind, tries to make the cost bearable, and all they actually do is guarantee it happens again to somebody else, decades later, no closer to actually being finished."

Caleb's jaw had gone tight, standing rigid by the door. "That's not a philosophy. That's a body count they've decided to be patient about."

"They don't see it that way," Wren said, still caught in whatever the button was showing her. "They see themselves as the only ones honest enough to look at the actual cost of kindness. Every carrier they've ever approached, they've offered the same choice. Most refused. A few said yes." Something in her face shifted, pained now, genuinely pained. "I can feel one of them. A woman, decades ago, who said yes, who let it complete the way they wanted. It didn't end everywhere the way they promised. It only ended for her valley. The debt still exists somewhere else, still waiting for the next unlucky bloodline to surface it. They know that. They've always known that. And still they keep offering the same promise to the next carrier, and the next, generation after generation, because believing the promise might finally be true this time is easier than admitting they've been wrong for as long as anyone in the family can remember."

The room had gone very still. Dev's arms had uncrossed at some point without him noticing, his hands pressed flat against the windowsill behind him as though he needed something solid to hold onto.

"They know it doesn't work," he said quietly. "They know, and they're still asking me to do it."

Wren's eyes cleared slightly, focus returning in fractured pieces. "I don't think they're lying to you, Dev. I think they've lied to themselves so many times, for so many generations, that they can't tell the difference anymore between the truth and the story they need to keep believing in order to keep doing this." She was breathing harder now, sweat beading at her hairline, the strain of holding the connection open starting to show plainly on her face. "There's more. Something about the mole. I can feel it, just at the edge, but it's slipping."

"Let it go," Priya said immediately, half-rising from her chair. "Wren, that's enough. Let it go now."

"Almost," Wren said, teeth gritted, her grip tightening around the button until her knuckles went white. "Someone close. Someone who's been afraid for a long time, longer than any of this, someone who thinks they're protecting the valley by feeding information out instead of standing against it directly."

Her voice broke off in a gasp, cut short before she could say anything more, and her hand sprang open, the button clattering onto the table as she slumped forward. Caleb was across the room before it finished rolling, catching her before she could fall fully out of the chair, pressing the folded towel into her hands the way he had the first two times, murmuring something low and steady that Dev couldn't quite hear from across the room.

"I'm okay," Wren managed, voice thin and shaking. "I'm okay. Just give me a second."

Priya knelt beside her, checking her pulse with the same clinical care she'd used the last two times this had happened, relief and worry fighting for space in her expression in equal measure. "You didn't have to push that far."

"I wanted the mole," Wren said, some of her old bluntness returning even through the exhaustion. "I almost had it. A face, a feeling, something close to the council. It slipped before I could hold it."

"You got enough," Dev said, crossing the kitchen finally to crouch on her other side. "You got more than enough. You told me why they're doing this. That's not nothing, Wren. That's the first honest answer any of us has had since this started."

Wren managed a small, tired smile at that, though it cost her visibly. "They actually believe it, Dev. That's the part that scares me most, more than any lie would have. A liar can be caught out eventually. Someone who genuinely believes they're saving the world by asking you to die for it, that's a different kind of dangerous. You can't out-argue faith. You can only refuse it."

Dev sat with that for a long moment, something hardening in his expression that hadn't been there a week ago, the last soft edge of his earlier temptation finally, fully closing over. "Then I refuse it," he said. "Not because I don't understand why they believe it. Because I don't have to agree with someone to know they're wrong about what my life is worth spending on."

He said it steadily, no waver in his voice at all, and something in the certainty of it seemed to settle the whole room slightly, as though everyone present had been waiting, without quite admitting it, for him to say those words out loud and mean them. Priya's hand found his shoulder, brief and grounding, the same gesture she'd used the night he'd confessed his own near-agreement, except this time there was nothing to forgive, only something to witness.

Caleb helped Wren up slowly, steadying her as she found her feet again, still pale but steadier than she'd been a minute earlier. "Water. Then rest. You've earned both."

"I want to write this down first," Wren said. "While it's fresh. Before I lose the shape of it."

Priya didn't argue, though she stayed close, watching Wren with the same careful attention she'd given every step of tonight, unwilling to fully relax until she was certain the worst of it had passed.

"You keep doing this," Priya said quietly, once Wren had steadied enough to hold a pencil without her hand shaking. "Pushing past what's safe. I need you to tell me honestly, not the brave version, whether you can keep doing this and still be standing when the eclipse actually comes."

Wren considered the question longer than Dev expected her to, some of the exhaustion in her face giving way to something more thoughtful. "I don't know," she admitted. "I felt something tonight I haven't felt in the other reads. Not just information. Weight. Like the button wasn't just showing me a memory, it was asking me to carry a little of it forward with me. I don't fully understand what that means yet. I'll tell you the moment I do."

"That's all I'm asking," Priya said. "Honesty. Not heroics."

"I know the difference," Wren said. "I promise you, I know the difference."

Caleb brought her a second glass of water, crouching beside her chair with the same patient stillness he'd shown Dev through every hard night this past month. "For what it's worth," he said, "what you just did might be the most useful thing any of us has managed since this started. We finally know what we're arguing against, not just who."

"Knowing what we're arguing against doesn't make it easier to argue against," Wren said, but some warmth crept back into her voice as she said it, the exhaustion loosening its grip slightly under Caleb's steady attention.

"It makes it possible, though," Priya said, gathering the loose pages Wren had already filled and setting them in order. "We spent two days chasing a ghost we couldn't even name. Tonight you gave the ghost a shape, a history, a reason. That's not a small thing, whatever it cost you to get it."

"It's not nothing," Wren agreed, managing something closer to her usual dry humor now that the worst of the shaking had passed. "Ask me again in the morning whether it was worth it. I'll have a more honest answer once I've slept."

Dev stayed a while longer after that, watching Wren write out everything she could remember in her careful, cramped handwriting before the details could fade the way she'd warned they sometimes did by morning. He thought about the woman she'd described, decades gone now, who'd said yes and believed she was ending something forever, only to leave the same debt waiting somewhere else for someone she'd never meet. He thought about how easy it must have been for her to believe the promise, exhausted and frightened the same way he'd been on that porch step, wanting so badly for an ending to actually mean ending.

Outside the kitchen window, the frost had thickened again overnight, the eclipse four weeks and four days away now and counting, and somewhere out past the ridge, a family that believed itself merciful was still waiting, patient as ever, for an answer none of them intended to give.
