CHAPTER SEVEN

Caleb found Dev sitting alone on the porch steps of the Voss house well after the others had gone home, a mug of coffee gone cold in his hands, staring out at the dark line of the orchard like it might explain itself if he watched it long enough.

"Mara sent you," Dev said, without turning around. "Didn't she."

"Mara didn't send anybody." Caleb lowered himself onto the step beside him, joints protesting the cold the way they always did lately, a small honest ache that belonged entirely to him now instead of to whatever had been borrowed from him for eight years. "I came because I wanted to, and because I remember what it felt like to sit on a porch step at night convinced everyone around me understood something about my own life that I didn't."

Dev's jaw tightened. "You don't have to do the whole speech. I've heard it. Priya's given it, Mara's given it, even Yusuf tried a version of it last week, God help us both."

"I'm not giving a speech." Caleb looked out at the orchard too, matching Dev's stillness rather than pushing against it. "I'm telling you I know exactly what two years of missing time feels like from the inside, because I lived it for the better part of a decade without knowing it was even gone. And I'm telling you that's not the same as what's happening to you, not exactly, and I don't want to pretend it is just to make you feel less alone."

That got Dev's attention. He turned, finally, something wary and tired in his eyes. "Then why bring it up at all."

"Because there's one piece of it that overlaps, and I think it's the piece that actually matters." Caleb turned the mug over in his own hands, an old habit from the months right after his memory came back, when he'd needed something physical to hold onto during the parts of a conversation that got too close to the wound. "When my two years came back, in pieces, over that spring, I kept waiting to feel like myself again. Whole. Fixed. And it never quite worked that way. What came back instead was just more of me. More weight to carry, more grief I hadn't grieved yet because I never knew there was anything to grieve. My father's death, sitting there in my own memory for eight years like it happened to a stranger, and then all at once it wasn't a stranger's grief anymore. It was mine, and it hurt exactly as much as if it had happened yesterday."

Dev was quiet for a long moment. "That sounds worse than forgetting."

"Some days it was," Caleb admitted. "But I got to choose how to carry it, once it came back. Mara helped. Time helped. The valley helped, in its slow, clumsy, well-meaning way. I got the version where the weight came back all at once and then I had the rest of my life to figure out how to hold it." He set the mug down on the step between them. "What you're describing isn't that. You're not getting weight handed back to you slowly enough to learn how to hold it. It sounds like it's building inside you faster than anyone can teach you to carry it, and I think that's the actual danger here, more than the blackouts themselves. Not that you're forgetting. That you're being asked to hold something enormous with no time at all to learn how."

Dev's hands tightened around the cold mug. "Denise had years. Decades. She said so herself."

"She did." Caleb nodded slowly. "And I keep thinking about that, because it's the part that scares me most on your behalf. She got a slow apprenticeship into something terrible. You're getting thrown into the deep end of it with nobody who's ever survived the deep end to show you how it's done."

"So what do I do." Dev's voice cracked slightly, the fear underneath the exhaustion finally breaking through. "Everyone keeps talking about plans and rituals and timelines, and none of it feels like it's actually about me. It feels like it's about the valley. About stopping something bad from happening to everyone else. Nobody's asked me what I want."

Caleb turned fully to face him then, something sharpening in his expression. "What do you want, Dev?"

The question seemed to catch Dev off guard, like he hadn't expected anyone to actually ask it plainly.

"I want to still be me when this is over," Dev said finally, quiet and raw. "Whatever happens. Whatever they decide to do. I don't want to wake up in six weeks and find out I traded myself for the valley's safety the way it sounds like Denise traded fifty years of hers. I don't want to become a story people tell carefully, the way they tell hers now. I want to still be someone Priya recognizes."

"Then that's what we're fighting for," Caleb said simply. "Not the valley first. You first. If the valley benefits along the way, good, but you're not a sacrifice being managed here, Dev. You're a person we're trying to save, and I need you to hear that from someone who's been on the other end of almost losing themselves to this thing, because I think it matters more coming from me than from anyone who's only ever seen it from the outside."

Something in Dev's shoulders loosened, just slightly, the tightly held posture of a man bracing for a blow that hadn't come.

"Did it ever feel like it was going to take everything," Dev asked quietly. "Back when it was you. Did it ever feel like there wasn't going to be anything left of you once it was done."

Caleb considered the question honestly before answering, the way he'd learned to answer Dev's questions since this all started, no easy comfort, only what was actually true.

"Every day," he said. "For most of those eight years I didn't even know I was missing anything, so I couldn't be afraid of the ending, only confused by the gaps. But once I knew, once Denise's confession made the shape of what had happened to me visible for the first time, there were nights I genuinely didn't know if I'd get all of it back, or if some piece of who I used to be had simply stopped existing the moment it was taken. I lived with that uncertainty for months before the well fixed it, and I won't pretend that uncertainty didn't nearly break something in me." He held Dev's gaze steadily. "But I'm sitting here now. Whole, as far as anyone can tell. Married to the woman who never once stopped looking for the truth even when everyone told her to give up. If that's possible for me, after eight years of not even knowing what I'd lost, I have to believe it's possible for you too, especially with all of us already looking, already fighting, from the very first blackout instead of eight years too late."

Dev was quiet for a long moment, staring out at the dark orchard, and when he finally spoke his voice had steadied, just slightly, into something closer to resolve than fear.

"I don't want to be brave about this," he said. "Everyone keeps telling me I'm being brave. I don't feel brave. I feel like I'm barely holding on some days."

"Barely holding on and showing up anyway is the only definition of brave that's ever actually meant anything," Caleb said. "I spent eight years not even knowing there was anything to hold onto. You know exactly what's coming for you, and you're still here, still fighting, still asking the right questions instead of running from them. That's not nothing, Dev. That's most of the whole fight already."

Dev huffed out something that wasn't quite a laugh, but came close, the first almost-light sound Caleb had heard from him in days.

"You're better at this than Priya gives you credit for," Dev said.

"Priya gives me plenty of credit," Caleb said, allowing himself a small, dry smile. "She just also reminds me constantly that I talk in metaphors too much, which, given present company, might be a family trait worth watching for."

That earned a real, if small, laugh, and Caleb let the silence settle comfortably between them afterward, the two of them sitting side by side on the cold porch steps while the orchard held its dark, patient shape in front of them, neither of them pretending the fear was gone, both of them a little steadier for having said its true name out loud.
