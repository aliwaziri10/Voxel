CHAPTER SEVEN

Caleb found Dev sitting alone on the porch steps of the Voss house well after the others had gone home, a mug of coffee gone cold in his hands, staring out at the dark line of the orchard like it might explain itself if he watched it long enough. The night had turned properly cool, the first real edge of autumn finally cutting through what had been a stubbornly warm September, and Caleb could see his own breath faintly in the porch light as he crossed the yard.

"Mara sent you," Dev said, without turning around. "Didn't she."

"Mara didn't send anybody." Caleb lowered himself onto the step beside him, joints protesting the cold the way they always did lately, a small honest ache that belonged entirely to him now instead of to whatever had been borrowed from him for eight years. He'd noticed, in the months since the well, that he'd started almost welcoming the small aches of ordinary aging, proof that his body was finally moving through time the way it was supposed to instead of holding two years of it frozen somewhere he couldn't reach. "I came because I wanted to, and because I remember what it felt like to sit on a porch step at night convinced everyone around me understood something about my own life that I didn't."

Dev's jaw tightened. "You don't have to do the whole speech. I've heard it. Priya's given it, Mara's given it, even Yusuf tried a version of it last week, God help us both, and honestly it's starting to feel less like comfort and more like a script everyone's been handed."

"I'm not giving a speech." Caleb looked out at the orchard too, matching Dev's stillness rather than pushing against it, letting the silence stretch long enough that it didn't feel like pressure. "I'm telling you I know exactly what two years of missing time feels like from the inside, because I lived it for the better part of a decade without knowing it was even gone. And I'm telling you that's not the same as what's happening to you, not exactly, and I don't want to pretend it is just to make you feel less alone. That kind of comfort doesn't actually help. It just teaches you not to trust the people offering it."

That got Dev's attention. He turned, finally, something wary and tired in his eyes, the look of a man who'd been bracing for one more well-meaning lecture and hadn't gotten it. "Then why bring it up at all."

"Because there's one piece of it that overlaps, and I think it's the piece that actually matters." Caleb turned the mug over in his own hands, an old habit from the months right after his memory came back, when he'd needed something physical to hold onto during the parts of a conversation that got too close to the wound. "When my two years came back, in pieces, over that spring, I kept waiting to feel like myself again. Whole. Fixed. Like a light switching back on after a long outage. And it never quite worked that way. What came back instead was just more of me. More weight to carry, more grief I hadn't grieved yet because I never knew there was anything to grieve. My father's death, sitting there in my own memory for eight years like it happened to a stranger, and then all at once it wasn't a stranger's grief anymore. It was mine, and it hurt exactly as much as if it had happened yesterday, maybe more, because I'd lost the chance to grieve it slowly the way most people get to."

Dev was quiet for a long moment, turning that over. "That sounds worse than forgetting."

"Some days it was," Caleb admitted. "But I got to choose how to carry it, once it came back. Mara helped. Time helped. The valley helped, in its slow, clumsy, well-meaning way, showing up with casseroles and awkward silences and small kindnesses that added up to something real even when none of it felt like enough in the moment." He set the mug down on the step between them, the ceramic clicking softly against old wood. "I got the version where the weight came back all at once and then I had the rest of my life to figure out how to hold it. What you're describing isn't that. You're not getting weight handed back to you slowly enough to learn how to hold it. It sounds like it's building inside you faster than anyone can teach you to carry it, and I think that's the actual danger here, more than the blackouts themselves. Not that you're forgetting. That you're being asked to hold something enormous with no time at all to learn how."

Dev's hands tightened around the cold mug, knuckles going pale. "Denise had years. Decades. She said so herself, this afternoon, sitting right there at her own kitchen table like it was the most natural thing in the world to talk about fifty years of carrying something like this."

"She did." Caleb nodded slowly. "And I keep thinking about that, because it's the part that scares me most on your behalf. She got a slow apprenticeship into something terrible, years to grow calluses over the parts that would otherwise have broken her. You're getting thrown into the deep end of it with nobody who's ever survived the deep end to show you how it's done, no roadmap, no mentor who's walked your exact version of this path."

"So what do I do." Dev's voice cracked slightly, the fear underneath the exhaustion finally breaking through the careful composure he'd been holding all evening. "Everyone keeps talking about plans and rituals and timelines, and none of it feels like it's actually about me. It feels like it's about the valley. About stopping something bad from happening to everyone else, some abstract disaster to be prevented. Nobody's asked me what I want. Not really. Not in a way that expected an actual answer."

Caleb turned fully to face him then, something sharpening in his expression, the porch light catching the seriousness in it. "What do you want, Dev?"

The question seemed to catch Dev off guard, like he hadn't expected anyone to actually ask it plainly, without wrapping it in reassurance first.

"I want to still be me when this is over," Dev said finally, quiet and raw, the words coming slower than usual like he was excavating them from somewhere he hadn't visited before. "Whatever happens. Whatever they decide to do. I don't want to wake up in six weeks and find out I traded myself for the valley's safety the way it sounds like Denise traded fifty years of hers. I don't want to become a story people tell carefully, the way they tell hers now, always careful, always a little distant, like she's more legend than person. I want to still be someone Priya recognizes. Someone she doesn't have to explain to people, the way she never had to explain me before any of this started."

"Then that's what we're fighting for," Caleb said simply. "Not the valley first. You first. If the valley benefits along the way, good, and it probably will, but you're not a sacrifice being managed here, Dev. You're a person we're trying to save, full stop, and I need you to hear that from someone who's been on the other end of almost losing themselves to this thing, because I think it matters more coming from me than from anyone who's only ever seen it from the outside looking in."

Something in Dev's shoulders loosened, just slightly, the tightly held posture of a man bracing for a blow that hadn't come, muscles that had been locked tight for hours finally allowing themselves a fraction of relief.

"Did it ever feel like it was going to take everything," Dev asked quietly, his voice smaller now than it had been a moment ago. "Back when it was you. Did it ever feel like there wasn't going to be anything left of you once it was done, like whoever came out the other side might not really be you anymore."

Caleb considered the question honestly before answering, the way he'd learned to answer Dev's questions since this all started, no easy comfort, only what was actually true, even when the truth wasn't especially reassuring.

"Every day," he said. "For most of those eight years I didn't even know I was missing anything, so I couldn't be afraid of the ending, only confused by the gaps, small strange holes in conversations I didn't understand until Mara started asking questions no one else had thought to ask. But once I knew, once Denise's confession made the shape of what had happened to me visible for the first time, there were nights I genuinely didn't know if I'd get all of it back, or if some piece of who I used to be had simply stopped existing the moment it was taken, gone the way a photograph fades until there's nothing left to develop." He held Dev's gaze steadily, unflinching. "I lived with that uncertainty for months before the well fixed it, and I won't pretend that uncertainty didn't nearly break something in me, some nights worse than others. But I'm sitting here now. Whole, as far as anyone can tell, myself in every way that matters. Married to the woman who never once stopped looking for the truth even when everyone told her to give up, including me, more than once, when the search felt too painful to keep going. If that's possible for me, after eight years of not even knowing what I'd lost, I have to believe it's possible for you too, especially with all of us already looking, already fighting, from the very first blackout instead of eight years too late."

Dev was quiet for a long moment, staring out at the dark orchard, the shapes of the trees barely visible now against the deepening night, and when he finally spoke his voice had steadied, just slightly, into something closer to resolve than fear.

"I don't want to be brave about this," he said. "Everyone keeps telling me I'm being brave. I don't feel brave. I feel like I'm barely holding on some days, like the only thing keeping me upright is momentum and everyone else's expectations."

"Barely holding on and showing up anyway is the only definition of brave that's ever actually meant anything," Caleb said, firm and certain. "I spent eight years not even knowing there was anything to hold onto, walking around with a hole in my own history and calling it normal because I had nothing to compare it to. You know exactly what's coming for you, and you're still here, still fighting, still asking the right questions instead of running from them or pretending they don't exist. That's not nothing, Dev. That's most of the whole fight already, more than most people manage when they're facing something this frightening."

Dev huffed out something that wasn't quite a laugh, but came close, the first almost-light sound Caleb had heard from him in days, a small crack in the heaviness that had settled over the porch.

"You're better at this than Priya gives you credit for," Dev said.

"Priya gives me plenty of credit," Caleb said, allowing himself a small, dry smile that eased some of the tension from his own face. "She just also reminds me constantly that I talk in metaphors too much, which, given present company and how much of this conversation has apparently qualified, might be a family trait worth watching for going forward."

That earned a real, if small, laugh, genuine enough that it seemed to surprise Dev as much as anyone, and Caleb let the silence settle comfortably between them afterward, the two of them sitting side by side on the cold porch steps while the orchard held its dark, patient shape in front of them.

They sat that way for a while longer, neither in any hurry to go back inside, the cold settling more fully into the evening around them. Somewhere down the road a screen door banged shut, an ordinary sound from an ordinary house, and Dev turned his head slightly toward it, some part of him still cataloguing the valley's small, unremarkable noises like proof that a normal world still existed alongside whatever was happening inside him.

"Thank you," Dev said eventually, quiet, not looking at Caleb directly. "For coming out here without being sent. For not pretending you know exactly how this ends."

"I don't know how it ends," Caleb said honestly. "Nobody does yet, and I won't insult you by acting otherwise. But I know what it looks like to fight for someone who thinks he isn't worth fighting for, because I watched Mara do exactly that for me, every single day, for longer than either of us would have chosen if we'd had a say in it. I intend to do the same for you, for as long as it takes, whether that's weeks or months or however long this turns out to run."

Dev nodded slowly, and for the first time all evening the silence between them felt less like something being endured and more like something being shared, two men sitting together in the cold, neither of them pretending the fear was gone, both of them a little steadier for having said its true name out loud, for having sat with it honestly instead of dressing it up as something smaller than it actually was.
