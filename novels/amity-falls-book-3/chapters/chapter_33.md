CHAPTER THIRTY-THREE

By the fourth day after Halloran's death, the silo no longer felt like a war room. It felt like a fortress, and Priya had started to hate how quickly she'd gotten used to the difference.

Yusuf had made good on his promise within hours, pulling in every deputy the county could spare and a handful of trusted valley men besides, rotating pairs stationed not just around the silo itself but at each of the ten candidates' homes individually, a schedule Priya had helped build and now barely recognized as her own life anymore, every waking hour divided between Dev's safety and the ring's readiness with almost nothing left over.

"Farrow's place worries me most," she said, spreading the coverage map across the folding table for what felt like the twentieth time that week. "Too many tree lines close to the house, too few sightlines from the road. If I were planning to reach one of the ten quietly, I'd start there."

"Then we don't let it be quiet," Mara said, marking a second watch point on the map with the same steady hand she'd used to mark Denise's well two years ago, though the tightness around her eyes told Priya the steadiness was costing her more than it used to. "Two pairs instead of one, staggered shifts so there's never a clean gap between watches. I'd rather over-cover Farrow's house for eleven days than under-cover it for the one night that matters."

Castellan's apartment above the feed store got a similar treatment, along with the elderly Petrov sisters who'd volunteered together on the same afternoon and refused, gently but immovably, to be separated even for security's sake. By the end of the fourth day, Priya had walked the full loop of all ten homes twice herself, checking each posted watch in person rather than trusting the schedule alone, a habit Yusuf hadn't asked for and hadn't tried to talk her out of either.

She found Dev exactly where she expected to, at the silo's back corner, sitting on an overturned crate with his knees drawn up, watching Wren and Mara argue quietly over a diagram neither of them seemed willing to concede.

"You're supposed to be resting," Priya said, settling onto the crate beside him.

"I rested this morning. Four whole hours, if you can believe it." Dev's mouth twisted into something that wasn't quite a smile. "Caleb made me promise before he'd let me out of the house."

"Good. He's better at making you keep promises than I am." Priya studied her brother's profile in the low silo light, the sharper lines that had settled into his face over the past weeks, exhaustion and resolve wearing the same groove deeper each day. "How are you actually doing. Not the answer you give Yusuf. The real one."

Dev was quiet for a moment, turning something over before he answered, the same deliberate care he'd brought to every honest thing he'd said since the porch confrontation weeks ago. "Scared," he said finally. "More than I've let anyone see, maybe more than I've let myself see, most days. But it's a different kind of scared than it used to be."

"Different how."

"Before, I was scared of what was happening to me. Losing time, losing pieces of myself I couldn't account for, becoming something none of us understood." He looked over at her, and Priya saw, not for the first time, how much steadier her brother's eyes had become since the first blackout, how much of the boy who'd flinched from her doubt two years ago had been replaced by someone who'd learned, the hard way, to stand still inside his own fear instead of running from it. "Now I'm scared of losing people who chose to stand next to me anyway. Odette. Farrow. That kid Castellan, who's not that much younger than I am and talks about his grandmother like she's the reason he gets out of bed some mornings. I used to only have myself to be afraid for. That was almost easier."

Priya reached over and took his hand, the way she used to when they were kids and their mother worked nights and the house felt too big and too quiet for the two of them alone. "You didn't ask any of them to carry this," she said. "They chose it, with their eyes open, the same way Mara made everyone promise back with Denise. That's not a burden you put on them, Dev. That's a gift they're choosing to give you. There's a difference, even if it doesn't always feel like one from where you're sitting."

"I know that. I do." He squeezed her hand once, gratefully, before letting go. "I just don't know how to hold that gift without feeling the weight of every single thing it might cost them, especially now that we know somebody's already died for taking the gentler side of this fight."

"You don't have to hold it alone," Priya said. "That's the whole point of the circle, isn't it. Ten people standing with you specifically so you're not carrying any of this by yourself anymore."

Dev nodded slowly, some of the tightness in his shoulders easing fractionally, and for a moment they sat together in comfortable silence, the same silence they'd shared countless nights growing up, before any of this, before his conviction, before the long careful work of learning to trust each other again piece by piece.

"Can I tell you something," Dev said eventually. "Something I haven't told Caleb or Mara or anyone else yet."

"Always."

"I keep thinking about our grandmother. Eleanor." He said the name carefully, like it still didn't quite fit comfortably in his mouth even weeks after learning it. "She buried this her whole life. Never told our mother, never told either of us, carried the fear of it silently until it died with her, and then it turned out it hadn't died at all, it had just been waiting. I keep wondering if she'd have made a different choice, if she'd had ten people willing to stand in a circle for her the way I have now. If maybe the reason this got as far as it did with me is that she never had what I have."

Priya felt something catch in her throat, the shape of a grief that belonged to a woman neither of them had ever really known, only the silence she'd left behind. "Maybe," she said softly. "Or maybe she made the only choice that felt survivable to her, alone, scared, with nobody who understood enough to help her the way we understand now. I don't think that makes her wrong, Dev. I think it makes her exactly as afraid as you'd expect someone to be, facing this completely by herself."

"I'm not facing it by myself," Dev said, and something in his voice had steadied further, settling into the same cold, clear resolve Priya had watched harden in him since the schedule board revealed Halloran's tactics. "That's the whole difference. That's what I keep reminding myself, every time I'm scared for the ten of them instead of for me. She didn't have this. I do."

Across the silo, Yusuf's truck pulled up outside again, and this time Priya recognized the particular urgency in the engine's cut, the same abrupt stop that had announced Halloran's death four days earlier, and she felt her whole body tense before he'd even reached the door.

He came in carrying a thin folder, his expression grim but controlled, the look of a man delivering hard news he'd at least had time to prepare himself for.

"We have a name," he said, without preamble, setting the folder down on the table where Mara and Wren had been working. "Or close enough to one. My contact in the state investigator's office traced a partial print from the Halloran scene against an old, half-forgotten file from thirty years back. A man named Corwin Drake, tied to a string of unsolved disappearances across three counties in the seventies and eighties, cases that went cold because nobody could ever place a consistent motive behind them."

"Whitlock-adjacent disappearances," Mara said slowly, already reaching for the folder.

"That's the theory my contact and I both landed on independently," Yusuf said. "Each case involved someone with a buried family connection similar to Dev's, someone who'd have been a candidate for exactly this kind of awakening, if the bloodline theory holds the way we believe it does. If Drake's still alive, and the timeline suggests he could be, he'd be in his seventies now. Which means he's not likely operating alone anymore. He's leading people younger and more capable than he is."

"How many people," Priya asked, the deputy in her instinctively reaching for the operational question first.

"Unknown," Yusuf admitted. "Halloran's own circle numbered somewhere around a dozen, by Wren's earlier read on the stranger. If Drake's splinter faction pulled even half of them away from Halloran's restraint, we could be looking at six to eight people willing to use force where Halloran only ever used persuasion."

The silo went quiet, the weight of the number settling over the room the same way Halloran's death had settled four days earlier, another piece of abstract danger sharpening suddenly into something with a face, a history, a body count stretching back decades.

"Seven days," Wren said quietly, checking the almanac calculation she'd pinned to the wall weeks ago, the number shrinking with each passing day the way it always seemed to, patient and indifferent to how ready anyone felt. "Seven days until the eclipse, and now we know at least part of what's standing between us and getting there safely."

"Then we use every one of them," Dev said, rising from the crate at the silo's back corner, some of his earlier vulnerability folding back into the same hardened resolve Priya had watched grow in him for weeks now. "Whatever Drake's planning, we don't wait to find out on his timeline. We find him first, if we can, or we make damn sure this silo and every home on that map is ready for whatever he tries before we ever have to."

Yusuf studied him for a long moment, something like real respect moving across his tired face. "That's the plan," he said. "Full security review tonight, every home, every watch schedule doubled again where we can manage it. And I want Priya running point on anything involving Drake specifically, given her background. If this man's spent thirty years staying invisible to law enforcement, I want someone who actually understands how that's done working the problem from our side."

Priya nodded, already feeling the shape of the coming days settle over her, security sweeps and coverage maps and a name that hadn't existed in her world twelve hours ago now sitting at the center of everything she'd be doing until the eclipse either saved her brother or didn't.

Mara had already pulled Drake's thin file toward her, cross-referencing something against her own structural maps with the same relentless focus she'd once brought to Denise's well and root cellar, searching for the pattern underneath the danger the way she always did, looking for the load path that would tell them where the real weight of this threat was actually going to land.

"Seven days," Caleb said, arriving in time to catch the tail end of the briefing, his hand finding Dev's shoulder in the same steady gesture he'd offered every day since the first blackout. "We've turned less into more before. We'll make seven days count the same way we made five weeks count, and eleven days before that."

Dev looked around the silo at the people who'd gathered there for him, at the chalk circle waiting patient in the dim light, at the ten stakes marking places ten people had chosen to stand for his sake despite a dead man's warning and a name that had just attached itself to real, documented danger.

"Seven days," he repeated, testing the number the way he tested every hard fact now, feeling its true weight settle into place. "Then that's what we use. All of it, starting tonight."

Outside, the frost had deepened further since the previous evening, and somewhere beyond the ridge, in whatever place Corwin Drake had chosen to wait out his own patient countdown, the same indifferent cold was settling in, unhurried, entirely unconcerned with how much the valley had already survived to reach this point, or how much more it was about to be asked to survive before the eclipse finally came.
