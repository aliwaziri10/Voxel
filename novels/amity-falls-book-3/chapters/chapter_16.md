CHAPTER SIXTEEN

Mara claimed the whole of the back porch by mid-morning, the way she'd once claimed the shed behind her house for Denise's well diagrams, spreading county maps and old newspaper clippings across a folding table until the wood grain disappeared entirely under paper. Caleb helped her carry out a second table when the first ran out of room, and then left her to it, the way everyone had learned to do once Mara's work took on that quiet intensity of hers that meant she'd rather be interrupted for coffee than for conversation.

She started with what they actually knew, which was less than she wanted and more than she'd expected once she laid it all out in front of her. Three confirmed contacts. The man at the orchard gate, careful and deliberately unremarkable, offering the first version of the argument. The woman on the porch step, more direct, willing to sit where she could be seen and studied in a way the first man never had. And Odette's grandmother's old saying, folk memory rather than fact, but consistent enough with everything else that Mara had started treating it as a data point instead of a story.

She wrote three columns on a fresh sheet of paper. Approach. Timing. Information known.

It was the same method she'd used mapping Denise's well the year before, back when nobody had understood yet what they were looking at, only that something in the valley didn't add up and the only way through the confusion had been to lay every known fact out flat and stop trusting her own assumptions about which ones mattered. She'd learned, slowly and at real cost, that the shape of a problem usually hid in the gaps rather than the facts themselves, the places where a pattern should have continued and didn't, or where two pieces of information sat too close together to be coincidence. She approached the quiet family the same way now, distrusting her own instinct to see menace everywhere and instead forcing herself to notice only what the record actually supported.

The work was slower than she wanted it to be. Twice she caught herself reaching for a conclusion the evidence hadn't earned yet, and twice she made herself cross it back out, the same discipline Priya had spent a year teaching all of them by example. By midmorning she had less certainty than she'd started with and more genuine understanding, which she'd come to recognize, over the course of this whole ordeal, as the actual sign that the work was going well rather than badly.

The orchard gate contact had approached Dev alone, mid-afternoon, roughly a week after his fourth blackout, back when only the five of them and Yusuf's inner circle knew anything at all about what was happening. He'd known Dev's walking route well enough to intercept him precisely, and he'd known about the recent discovery of the Whitlock name specifically enough that Priya's working theory, an inside source, had held up under two weeks of scrutiny without a single contradiction. The porch step contact had come later, after the council had been read in fully, and she'd known even more, timing her arrival for the ten minutes of supervised air Priya had only instituted days earlier.

"That's the part that bothers me most," Mara said, when Priya came out to check on her progress an hour in, two mugs of coffee in hand, one already going cold from how long Mara had been ignoring it. "Not that they know things. That they know things fast. The porch step contact came within days of a rule we hadn't even had time to explain to anyone outside this house yet."

"You think the leak's inside this house."

"I don't know what to think yet," Mara admitted, setting down her pencil to take the coffee gratefully. "I think it's either someone very close to us, or someone with a method of watching we haven't considered at all. Both possibilities scare me for different reasons."

Priya settled into the chair across from her, studying the spread of paper with the same flat, careful attention she brought to any scene. "Walk me through what you actually have. Not theories. Facts."

Mara appreciated the discipline of the question more than she said out loud. It was the same demand Priya had drilled into Wren during the Denise investigation, the same one that had kept all of them from chasing ghosts instead of evidence for most of the Denise investigation. She tapped the first column. "Both contacts described themselves, directly or indirectly, as part of something old. Generations old, according to Odette's grandmother's saying. If that's accurate, and I have no reason to doubt Odette on family folklore specifically, then whatever we're dealing with has been watching Whitlock bloodlines long enough to have refined a whole approach to it. Not improvised. Practiced."

"Practiced how."

"Look at the contrast in tactics." Mara pulled the two contact summaries side by side. "The man at the gate led with gentleness, reframing, soft language. Inheritance instead of illness. When that didn't land the way they wanted, they didn't escalate to threats. They sent someone different, someone willing to sit closer, be more direct, offer a fuller version of the truth than the first contact gave. That's not desperation. That's strategy. Someone is choosing which approach fits which moment, which means there's a structure behind this, a hierarchy, maybe, or at least a shared plan they're executing in sequence rather than reacting on instinct."

Priya turned that over for a moment, some of the old investigator's caution settling into her expression. "So we're not dealing with two isolated people. We're dealing with an organization."

"I think so," Mara said. "I don't know how large. I don't know if it's five people or fifty. But I don't think it's two."

Yusuf found them out on the porch not long after, driving out himself rather than sending word, a habit he'd picked up since the end of the Denise affair that everyone had quietly come to appreciate without ever saying so directly. He studied Mara's spread of papers for a long moment before speaking, the same careful weighing of information Priya recognized from every council session she'd sat through since he'd taken the chair.

"The council's asking questions I can't fully answer yet," he said finally. "How many people are we actually dealing with. Whether this stays contained to your household or becomes a valley-wide concern the way Denise's situation eventually did. I told them I'd have something firmer for them by the end of the week. I'd rather bring them truth slowly than comfort quickly."

"That's the right instinct," Mara said. "I wish I had more to give you today."

"You have more than you think," Yusuf said, studying the loose diagram over her shoulder. "An organized pattern is more than we had yesterday. I'll take that back to them as progress, not as an answer, and ask for patience a little longer." He hesitated at the porch steps on his way out, something older and more tired crossing his face. "I waited too long once already, with Denise. I don't intend to make that mistake twice, whatever it costs me with the council this time. Send word the moment you have something firmer."

"We will," Priya said, and meant it.

They worked through the rest of the afternoon together, Priya bringing her own procedural instincts to bear on the timeline while Mara kept building out the structural questions she was better suited to answering. By the time Wren wandered out to find them, notebook in hand, the folding tables had accumulated a rough sketch of something neither of them wanted to say out loud yet, a pattern that looked less like a small group of fanatics and more like an institution that had outlasted generations of the very people it watched.

"You've been out here all day," Wren said, settling onto an overturned crate beside the table. "Find anything?"

"A shape," Mara said. "Not a name yet. But a shape." She turned her sketch around so Wren could see it, a loose diagram of contacts, timing, and information flow, question marks scattered through it like the gaps in an incomplete map. "If I'm right, whoever these people are, they've been doing this long enough to have real institutional memory. Not just tracking Whitlock descendants. Refining how they approach each one, learning from whatever happened the last time this bloodline surfaced, however long ago that was."

"That's not exactly comforting."

"It's not meant to be," Mara said, echoing Priya's words from two days earlier without quite meaning to. "But it does tell us something useful. An organization this patient, this practiced, probably has rules. Limits. A pattern they follow because it's worked before. If we can figure out what those rules are, we might be able to predict what they do next instead of just reacting every time they show up somewhere new."

Wren considered that, turning her pencil over in her fingers the way she did when working through something half-formed. "The read on the coat button might help with that. Not just the why. The who, maybe. If it's really been passed down that carefully, whoever's wearing it now probably carries some trace of everyone who wore it before her."

"That's a lot of weight to put on one small object," Priya said.

"It's the only object we have," Wren said simply. "I'd rather try and learn something than sit here guessing forever."

Mara looked between them, something settling in her chest that felt almost like the early stages of a plan instead of just a collection of fears. "I want one more day on this before we do the read. If I'm right about the structure, there might be a pattern in how far apart their contacts space themselves. If the third approach is coming, I'd like to know roughly when, so we're not caught flat-footed by it the way Dev was caught on that porch."

"You think there's a third approach coming," Priya said.

"I think an organization this patient doesn't stop at two," Mara said. "I think they escalate until they get an answer, or until they run out of time. And we know exactly how much time all of us have left before the eclipse makes the question moot one way or another."

"What would escalation even look like, past two people showing up separately to talk to him," Wren asked.

"I don't know," Mara admitted, and something in the flatness of the admission was more unsettling than a confident guess would have been. "That's the part I keep circling back to without an answer. The first two contacts were both patient, both willing to walk away without pushing further when Dev didn't say yes on the spot. If that pattern holds, the next one might simply be a third attempt at persuasion, someone new, some different angle we haven't heard yet. If it doesn't hold, if they've decided patience has a limit after all, I don't have a good guess for what comes next, and I don't like sitting with that gap in the map."

"Then we watch harder," Priya said. "No more unsupervised air, not even the porch. Not until we understand the spacing better."

"Dev's going to hate that," Wren said.

"Dev's going to survive hating it," Priya said, with the weariness of someone who had already had this exact argument in her head several times that week. "I'd rather he resent me for a week than end up trusting the wrong stranger because we let our guard down out of politeness."

The afternoon light had started to slant low and gold across the porch by the time they finally gathered the papers back into some kind of order, the day's work leaving Mara with more questions charted than answers found, but a shape now where there had only been fear before. Inside, she could hear Dev's voice, steadier than it had sounded in days, talking with Caleb about something ordinary, a conversation that had nothing to do with blackouts or bargains or ancient watching families, just two people passing an afternoon together the way they might have before any of this started.

Mara let herself sit with that sound a moment before gathering the last of her charts, some small, stubborn part of her insisting on believing that a valley capable of holding this much fear could still hold this much ordinary warmth alongside it, right up until the night everything finally came due.

She thought, gathering the last of the papers into some rough order, of how differently this year had started, back when the well had been the only shape she'd needed to understand and the worst danger any of them had faced wore a name she already knew. This felt larger in a way she hadn't fully let herself measure until today, an old, patient organization instead of one frightened woman guarding a fifty-year secret, and she found herself hoping, more than she usually allowed herself to hope about anything, that the shape she was building on this porch would turn out to be enough.
