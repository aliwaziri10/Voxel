CHAPTER TWENTY-NINE

Mara found the anomaly at two in the morning, three days after the orchard, sitting alone at her kitchen table with the same cross-referenced timeline she'd built to catch Elias, except this time she was hunting for something that wasn't a person at all.

She'd started from the assumption everyone else had jumped to first, the assumption Priya had voiced out loud in the yard the day of the blackout: that someone inside the restricted five-person circle was still talking, another Elias hiding in plain sight among the people they trusted most. Mara had spent two full days testing that theory and found nothing. No unexplained absences. No unusual archive requests. No fabricated appointments. Whatever the leak was, it wasn't a second version of the same failure.

So she'd started over, working backward from the one hard fact she actually had: the quiet family had known Dev's exact four-minute supervised window down to the minute, a window the group had approved in a meeting three days before it happened. She pulled the meeting notes. She pulled the household's landline records, its cell records, the schedule board Priya kept pinned to the kitchen wall with the week's approved outdoor tasks written in dry-erase marker.

The schedule board. She stared at it for a long time before the shape of it finally clicked into place, cold and simple and entirely her own fault for not seeing it sooner.

The board sat directly in front of the kitchen window, the same window that faced the orchard, the same window with a clear, unobstructed line of sight to anyone standing at the tree line a hundred yards out with a decent pair of field glasses. Nobody had ever needed to get inside the house, inside the five-person circle, inside anything Yusuf had so carefully restricted. Somebody had simply been watching the board itself, reading the week's schedule straight off dry-erase marker through a kitchen window nobody had thought twice about leaving uncovered.

She called Priya at six, unable to wait for a decent hour once she'd confirmed it with a second look at the sightline from the exact spot in the orchard where Dev's blackout had ended.

"It's not a person," Mara said, before Priya had finished saying hello. "It's the board. Your schedule board, in the kitchen window. Anyone with field glasses standing at the tree line can read it clean. We've been looking for another Elias, and there isn't one. We've just been leaving the whole plan sitting in the window like a menu."

The silence on the other end lasted long enough that Mara wondered if the call had dropped.

"That's almost worse," Priya said finally, her voice flat with the particular exhaustion of someone who'd spent three sleepless nights bracing for betrayal and instead found simple carelessness. "I'd rather it were Elias again, if I'm honest. At least that has a name I can be angry at."

"I know," Mara said. "But it also means something important. It means the second leak isn't an ongoing person we still have to root out of this household. It's a fixable mistake. We move the board. We close the blinds. We stop treating our own kitchen like a place where nobody's ever watching."

By the time the whole group reconvened that morning, Mara had already sketched a full accounting of every place in the house where sensitive planning happened within sight of a window, and the list was longer and more embarrassing than any of them wanted to admit out loud. The schedule board. The whiteboard in the hallway where Wren had been tracking ring candidates by first name only, but still visible from the side yard if someone stood at the right angle near the woodpile. Even the kitchen table itself, glimpsed clearly enough through the gap in the curtains most evenings that anyone patient enough to sit in the tree line with glasses could have read body language and rough shapes of conversation, if not exact words.

"We've been so careful about who we trust," Yusuf said, reading through Mara's list with a grim, tired expression, "that we never stopped to ask whether the walls themselves were careful enough to hold what we were trusting them with."

"It changes the tactical picture," Wren said. "If they've been watching the house itself rather than turning someone inside it, that means they've had eyes on us for longer than we thought, patient enough to sit at that tree line for hours at a stretch without ever once needing to risk direct contact. That's not amateur work. That's the same kind of institutional patience Denise once described in Ambrose, if this really is the same lineage of watchers going back that far."

Dev had been quiet through most of the morning, sitting at the far end of the table with his hands wrapped around a mug of tea he hadn't touched, and when he finally spoke his voice had an edge Caleb recognized now as something closer to cold clarity than fear.

"If they've been watching the house," he said, "then they know roughly where I sleep, what time the lights go off, who comes and goes and when. That's not just my supervised windows. That's everything."

"It's a fixable problem," Mara said again, firmer this time, meeting his eyes directly. "We black out every window that faces the orchard side. We move sensitive planning into the back room with no sightline at all. We stop assuming the danger only comes from inside a locked door and start treating the glass itself as part of the perimeter."

"How long have they had eyes on us," Priya asked. "Realistically. Weeks? Since before Elias was even caught?"

"Possibly since before the orchard gate contact," Mara said. "Which would mean they've had a working picture of this household's rhythms for well over a month, layered on top of whatever Elias was separately feeding them until his own leak got cut off. Two channels running in parallel for a while, one human and deliberate, one just patient observation nobody thought to guard against."

Caleb had gone quiet, turning something over that had clearly been sitting uneasily in him since Mara's phone call that morning. "There's another angle to this," he said slowly. "If they've been watching this long, watching this closely, they've probably also seen everything we've been building toward. The ring. The candidates coming and going. Odette's visits."

The table went still at that.

"You think they know about the ring," Yusuf said.

"I think we have to assume they might," Caleb said. "Not the exact number, maybe, not every name, but the shape of it. Nine or ten strangers cycling through this house over the past week, staying longer than a casual visit, some of them clearly upset or shaken when they leave. That's not subtle, even from a hundred yards out with field glasses, even without hearing a single word."

Dev set his mug down hard enough that tea sloshed over the rim, unnoticed. "So the ring isn't a secret anymore either," he said. "The one thing we actually had, the one plan that was supposed to be ours instead of something they could counter, and it might already be sitting in their hands the same way my schedule was."

"We don't know that for certain," Priya said, though her voice carried the same fraying edge Dev's had.

"We don't know it isn't, either," Dev said. "And I'm tired of building plans on the hope that the worst version of things isn't already true. Let's assume it is. Let's assume they know about the ring, roughly, and plan around that instead of hoping we still have the advantage of surprise."

Yusuf looked at him for a long moment, something like grim respect settling into his tired face. "That's the harder, more useful question," he agreed. "If they know the ring exists, what does that change about how we finish building it, and how we protect it once it's assembled."

"It means we stop doing this here," Mara said, already thinking several steps ahead, pencil moving across a fresh page. "The candidates, the rehearsals, all of it. Somewhere they haven't had a month to map. Somewhere with no windows facing anything they can watch from a comfortable distance."

"Where," Wren asked.

Mara was quiet for a moment, and when she spoke again there was something careful in her voice, the tone of someone about to suggest a solution that came with its own uncomfortable weight attached. "There's an old grain silo on the Voss property, past the far fence line, closer to the river than the orchard. Structurally sound, I checked it myself last spring for an unrelated reason. No windows at all above ground level except a few high vents, nothing anyone could read a schedule board through even with the best glasses money can buy. It's isolated enough that anyone approaching would have to cross open ground first, which means we'd see them coming long before they saw us."

"You've been thinking about this for longer than this morning," Priya said, something almost like a question in it.

"I've been thinking about defensible spaces since the day Wren first said the word ring out loud," Mara admitted. "I just didn't want to move us somewhere new until I was sure the current setup had actually failed us, rather than moving out of paranoia we didn't need yet. Now I'm sure."

Yusuf nodded slowly. "Then we move. Today, if the silo's ready. I'll have Mara's crew go over every inch of it before anyone else sets foot inside, and I want a watch rotation on the approach starting immediately, someone with eyes on that open ground around the clock until the eclipse."

"That's a lot of resources for one building," a councilwoman might have said, if any of them were in the room, but they weren't, and Yusuf didn't wait for that objection before continuing.

"It's the only building we're planning to save Dev's life in," he said. "It gets whatever it needs."

Dev had gone very still through this whole exchange, something working behind his eyes that Caleb recognized as the shape of a question building before Dev was quite ready to ask it out loud.

"There's something else," Dev said finally. "If they've been watching us this closely, this long, and they knew enough to be at the orchard exactly when I was vulnerable, doesn't that mean they know things about me that I don't even know about myself yet. Patterns in my own blackouts I haven't noticed. Something in how I move or where I go during them that might tell them more than it tells us."

The question landed heavy in the quiet kitchen. Wren was the one who finally answered it, her voice careful in a way that told Caleb she'd already been circling this same fear on her own.

"It's possible," she said. "I don't want to lie to you and say it isn't. If they've had eyes on this house for a month, they may understand the shape of your blackouts better than we do, purely from patient observation, the same way a hunter learns an animal's trail without ever needing to be told where it sleeps."

Dev's jaw tightened, but he didn't flinch from it, and Caleb watched something in him settle into a harder resolve rather than crumble under the weight of it.

"Then we use the silo," Dev said. "And we assume from now on that every advantage we think we have might already be known to them, and we build the plan to survive that assumption instead of hoping it's wrong."

"That's a colder way to plan," Yusuf said, something like approval threaded through the observation.

"I'm done planning warm," Dev said. "Warm planning is what almost got me walked into those trees. I'd rather be cold and alive than comfortable and caught off guard again."

The meeting broke soon after into a flurry of practical motion, Mara heading out to inspect the silo properly with a crew Yusuf authorized within the hour, Priya beginning the delicate work of quietly relocating the ring candidates' next rehearsal without alarming anyone further than they'd already been alarmed, Wren pulling every scrap of her research together to rebuild the candidate whiteboard somewhere no window could ever betray it again.

Caleb found Dev alone on the back porch an hour later, staring out at the orchard with an expression that had none of the old fear in it anymore, something colder and more deliberate having taken its place.

"You did the right thing in there," Caleb said, easing down onto the step beside him. "Making them assume the worst instead of hoping for the best."

"I didn't do it because it felt brave," Dev said. "I did it because I'm tired of finding out the hard way what they already knew. If assuming the worst keeps one more person from having to catch me before I walk into those trees again, then I don't care how cold it makes the planning feel."

"For what it's worth," Caleb said, "cold planning kept people alive plenty of times, in ways warm hope never managed on its own. I'd rather stand next to someone thinking clearly through fear than someone pretending the fear away."

Dev was quiet for a while, watching the tree line the way he'd watched it every day since the blackout, no longer with dread exactly, something closer to a wary, patient vigilance settling into its place instead.

"Two weeks and two days," he said finally, testing the number the way he tested all of them now, turning it over to see what shape it held today. "A silo instead of a kitchen table. A ring I still don't know if they've already mapped. I keep thinking there should be a point where this stops getting more complicated instead of less."

"Maybe there is," Caleb said. "Maybe it's just further out than either of us can see clearly from here."

Dev huffed out something that wasn't quite a laugh, tired but real. "That's either very wise or very unhelpful. I can't decide which."

"Probably both," Caleb said, and let the silence settle comfortably between them again, the orchard holding its ordinary, watchful shape in front of them, no longer entirely trustworthy, but no longer entirely unknown either, mapped now on both sides in ways neither of them had wanted but both of them had finally learned to use.
