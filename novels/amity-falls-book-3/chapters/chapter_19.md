CHAPTER NINETEEN

The clinic released Dev on the third day with a folder of test results that explained almost nothing and a single instruction the doctor had repeated twice, slowly, as though she didn't quite trust any of them to remember it under stress: no exertion, no unsupervised hours, and call the moment anything felt wrong, not after it had already gone wrong.

He came home to a kitchen that had been rearranged without his input into something closer to a command post than a house. Mara had claimed the far wall, where a fresh sheet of butcher paper now ran from the window to the pantry door, covered in the same tight, confident handwriting she used for load calculations and joist spans, except now it mapped blackout intervals instead of stress points. Wren had colonized the table itself, books and photocopied almanac pages fanned out in a pattern only she seemed able to read. Caleb had pulled the good armchair in from the front room and set it by the stove, close enough to the door that he could reach Dev in three steps from anywhere in the kitchen, a habit none of them had discussed out loud but all of them had apparently agreed to independently. Priya stood at the counter with her service radio clipped to her belt even though she was off shift, the posture of someone who had stopped fully believing in off shift two weeks ago.

"You rearranged my whole kitchen," Dev said, easing himself into the chair Caleb had angled for him.

"I rearranged a workspace," Mara said, not looking up from the wall. "The kitchen was incidental."

"She timed how long it took each of us to reach you from every point in the house," Wren said, not lifting her eyes from the almanac page either. "I got benched to slowest. I'm bitter about it."

"You were reading while walking," Mara said. "That's not a design flaw in the room."

Dev found himself smiling despite everything, the small, worn comfort of watching people who loved him bicker over logistics instead of standing around him with the careful, frightened faces he'd been dreading since the clinic. Priya caught his eye across the kitchen and something in her expression eased slightly at the sound of it, even as she stayed where she was, radio at her hip, watching the door the way she'd watched every door for two weeks now.

Dev let his eyes travel over the butcher paper wall while the argument ran its course, tracing Mara's neat brackets from the four-minute blackout through the twenty-minute one, through the lost afternoon, through today's collapse in the square, each interval shorter than the last, each one drawn in the same steady hand as though steadiness alone might keep the numbers from meaning what they meant. He thought of Caleb's truck idling outside the feed store that morning, of Mara's diagram for a chicken-feed run, and understood, with a fresh, cold clarity, exactly how much of his family's daily life had already reorganized itself around a body he no longer fully trusted.

"Okay," Caleb said, once Dev was settled and the room had quieted enough to hold a real conversation instead of four half ones. "We said we'd do this properly this time. Not wait for the next crisis to force the plan on us. So let's actually do it."

"Do what, exactly," Dev asked.

"Split it," Mara said. "The way we should have split Denise's problem from the start, before we let it stay one enormous, undifferentiated fear for months longer than it needed to be. Four different problems wearing one face. We treat them as four."

She turned from the wall and laid it out the way she laid out everything, in clean, load-bearing pieces that made the size of it briefly manageable instead of crushing. "I take the structure. Timeline, acceleration rate, what today at the feed store actually tells us about how much runway we have left, separate from the eclipse date entirely. I want a second number by the end of this week, not just five weeks to the eclipse. A body clock, if we can even estimate one."

"I take the read," Wren said, tapping the almanac page in front of her. "Not on Dev again, not unless it's absolutely necessary and we've exhausted every other option first. On the objects. The stone. Anything the quiet family's left behind that we can get our hands on without walking into another ambush like the orchard gate. If there's a controlled way to do what they want done uncontrolled, I want to find it before they do."

"I take Dev," Caleb said simply, and something in the plainness of it landed harder than a longer speech would have. "Not the timeline, not the read. Him. What he's scared of, what he needs to say out loud that he hasn't said yet, whether he's actually sleeping or just telling Priya he is so she stops asking. Somebody in this family has to hold the part of this that isn't a problem to solve. That's mine."

Dev's throat tightened unexpectedly at that, the plain, unfussed weight of it landing somewhere he hadn't braced for. He didn't trust his voice enough to answer, so he only nodded, and Caleb nodded back, the two of them understanding each other in the same wordless shorthand they'd built over months of exactly this kind of care.

"And I take the threat," Priya said, from the counter, arms crossed, the last piece falling into place. "The quiet family. Whoever's feeding them information, if anyone is. What they'll actually try next, not what they say they'll try. I already flagged the rental car dead end and the property past the ridge to the county records office under a different pretext. I'm not waiting for them to come to us again."

Priya's radio crackled once against her hip, a routine dispatch call for someone else's night, and she silenced it with a practiced thumb without breaking her focus on the room. "One more thing on my end," she said. "Yusuf wants a full council briefing. Not the five of us in this kitchen anymore. He called this afternoon while you were still under observation, Dev, and he wasn't asking."

"When," Mara said, already reaching for a blank corner of her wall.

"Two days. He wants Denise's testimony included, if she's willing, and he wants Wren's read findings whether they're finished or not." Priya's mouth tightened. "He said, and I'm quoting him exactly, that he's not going to be the reason this valley loses two weeks to caution again, but he's also not going to let five people carry a decision this size without the council's eyes on it. I think part of him is still afraid of his own hesitation with Denise, and part of him is afraid of the opposite mistake now. Moving so fast that we miss something the way careful people usually catch."

"That's fair," Caleb said. "Uncomfortable. But fair."

"It's fine," Dev said, surprising himself with how steady it came out. "Let them look at it properly. I'd rather have too many careful eyes on this than too few, after today."

"That's a good split," Dev said quietly, once all four pieces sat on the table between them. "It actually is. But where does that leave me?"

The question hung there a beat too long, and Dev watched something pass between the other three, unspoken, before Mara answered it directly, the way she answered everything.

"You're not a fifth problem to assign, Dev. You're the reason the other four matter at all. But I won't pretend that isn't a strange, uncomfortable place to sit, being the center of a plan without being handed a piece of it to hold yourself."

"So give me a piece," Dev said, sitting up straighter than the doctor's instructions strictly allowed. "I'm not asking to run the timeline math or pull county records. I know I can't do the things you can each do. But I've spent two weeks being managed. I want to actually help decide something, not just be kept safe while the rest of you decide it for me."

Priya's jaw tightened, the old protective instinct visibly warring with something else in her face, something that looked almost like recognition. "What did you have in mind."

"Denise," Dev said. "Everyone keeps saying we should ask her more, use what she remembers of Adelaide's teaching. But nobody's actually sat with her since the first time, back before any of this got as bad as it is now. I want to go. Not alone, obviously," he added quickly, before Priya could say it for him. "But I want to be the one who asks her, not have someone report back to me secondhand what she said. If this is happening to my body, I want to hear the parts that scare me directly from the person who's lived through the closest version of it, not filtered through someone trying to protect me from how bad it sounds."

The kitchen went quiet for a long moment, the fire popping once in the stove, and Dev watched Priya's face move through fear, reluctance, and finally something that looked almost like pride, before she spoke.

"Okay," she said. "You and me. Caleb comes too, since Denise trusts him more than any of us after everything last year. But you ask the questions, Dev. Not us, filtering it for you. I think you're right that you've earned that much."

"I'll set it up for tomorrow," Caleb said. "She's been asking about him anyway, since the feed store. Word travels fast in a valley this size."

Wren looked up from her almanac pages at last, something rare and unguarded crossing her usually blunt face. "For what it's worth, Dev, I think that's the first genuinely brave thing I've watched you choose since this started. Not the blackouts. Not surviving what happened at the porch. This. Actually asking for the hard truth instead of letting us decide how much of it you can handle."

"Don't get sentimental on me," Dev said, but his voice cracked slightly around the joke, and nobody in the room pretended not to notice.

Mara turned back to her wall of numbers, already sketching a fifth column beside the four she'd drawn, and when Dev asked what it was for, she answered without turning around, the dry, engineering calm of her voice not quite hiding what sat underneath it.

"A margin," she said. "Every structure needs one. We've been building this whole plan assuming five weeks and hoping the number holds. I'd rather build in room for it not to, starting now, than discover the gap the way we discovered it today, in the middle of the town square, with no warning at all."

Nobody argued the point. Outside, the early dark of late autumn had already dropped over the valley, the kitchen's four lamps throwing long shadows across a wall covered in numbers none of them fully understood yet, and for the first time in two weeks, the fear in the room felt less like something happening to them and more like something they had, finally, begun to actually work.

Later, once Wren had gone home to her own small apartment above the feed store, trading one set of almanac pages for another, and Mara had retreated upstairs with her margin column still unfinished, Priya found Dev alone in the front room, sitting in the dark rather than turning on a lamp, staring out through the window glass at nothing at all.

She didn't switch the light on either. She just sat down beside him on the arm of the old couch, close enough that their shoulders touched, the radio finally clipped off her belt and set on the side table for the first time in two weeks.

"You scared me today," she said quietly, not the deputy's voice, just her own. "More than the porch. More than any of the blackouts. I keep seeing you go down in that square."

"I know," Dev said. "I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize for that. Mara already told you not to, and she was right." Priya was quiet for a moment, her hand finding his in the dark the way it had at the clinic. "I just need you to know I heard what you asked for tonight. The Denise trip. Wanting a real piece of this instead of being managed through it. I heard it, and I'm proud of you for asking, even though every instinct I have wants to lock you in this house until the eclipse comes and goes."

"I know that too," Dev said, his voice rough. "I'm not trying to make this harder for you."

"You're not," Priya said. "You're trying to still be yourself while it happens to you instead of just letting it happen. That's exactly what you told Caleb you wanted, back at the start of all this. I should have expected you'd actually mean it."

They sat together in the dark a while longer, the house settling around them, the valley outside quiet and cold and entirely unaware of the countdown running inside its own borders, and for a few minutes neither of them said anything else at all, because for once, nothing else needed saying.
