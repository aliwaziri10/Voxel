CHAPTER FOURTEEN

The second one didn't bother hiding at all.

Dev noticed her the moment he stepped out onto the porch for what Priya still insisted on calling "supervised air," a phrase that had started as a joke between them and had, over the last week, hardened into something closer to policy. Ten minutes, always in sight of the kitchen window, never past the fence line. He'd agreed to the terms without complaint, mostly because he understood exactly why they existed, and partly because arguing with Priya about his own safety these days felt like arguing with the weather.

The woman was sitting on the porch step itself, as though she'd been invited, as though the last two weeks of locked doors and rotating watches had simply never happened around her.

"You're new," Dev said, staying in the doorway, one hand still on the frame, close enough to retreat inside in three steps if he needed to.

"I am," she agreed, and didn't turn around, her voice carrying the same unhurried warmth Odette used when she was about to tell a story she'd told a hundred times before and still loved telling. "The man who spoke with you at the orchard gate wasn't the right person to finish the conversation. I am."

"There's a lot of you, then."

"There have always been a lot of us." She turned now, and Dev found himself studying a face that gave away almost nothing, pleasant in the way that made it hard to look away from and harder still to describe afterward, the same unremarkable quality Priya had spent two fruitless days trying to pin a name to. Even the color of her coat seemed designed to be forgotten, a gray so ordinary it barely registered as a color at all. "That's rather the point, Dev. We're patient because we've had generations of practice being patient. You've had two weeks to be afraid. I understand that isn't a fair fight."

He should go back inside. He knew that with the same flat certainty he knew his own name, the rule Priya had drilled into him laid out plainly: anyone unfamiliar, anyone at all, you come inside immediately and you tell someone. He didn't move. Some part of him, the same part that had once needed Priya to stop seeing him only as her mistake, wanted to hear what came next, wanted proof that he could stand in front of something dangerous now without simply flinching away from it the way he had for years after his conviction.

"Say what you came to say," he said instead. "I'm not walking off with you. I'm not doing whatever the last one wanted either."

"I'm not asking you to walk anywhere." She patted the step beside her, an invitation he didn't take, and something in her expression suggested she hadn't expected him to. "I only came to correct a misunderstanding. The man at the gate spoke to you about inheritance and completion. Gentle words, chosen carefully, and not wrong, exactly, but perhaps not entirely honest either, in the way gentle words so rarely are."

"So what's the honest version."

She considered him for a long moment, something almost like respect moving behind her eyes. "The honest version is that we don't want to help you carry this quietly, the way your grandmother tried to carry the silence of it, or the way Denise carried her own version for fifty years believing restraint was mercy. We want it to finish. Completely. All at once, the way it was always meant to, before people like Adelaide started interrupting it out of fear and calling that interruption kindness."

Dev's chest tightened. "Finish how."

"You already know how, I think, somewhere underneath the part of you that's been taught to be afraid of it." Her voice stayed gentle, terribly gentle, the kind of gentleness that made the words underneath it land harder rather than softer. "What's accumulating in you isn't a slow fifty-year debt the way it was for Denise. It's generations of unspent weight, skipped and buried and denied, all of it gathering now because your body is the first one in a very long time that couldn't be taught to bury it further. If it releases the way your friends are planning, carefully, in pieces, shared out among willing hands the way they freed Denise, it survives. You survive. But the debt itself survives too, diminished, scattered, waiting to resurface again in someone else's grandchild two generations from now, the way it waited in you."

"And if it releases your way instead."

"Then it's finished," she said simply. "Not scattered. Not postponed. Spent completely, in one true, total payment, the way Ambrose himself always believed it should have been paid from the very beginning, before people started flinching from the size of the cost and doing it in careful, cowardly pieces instead."

Something cold moved through Dev's stomach, sharper than fear, closer to a kind of vertigo, the ground shifting slightly under a version of the truth he hadn't let himself consider directly until she said it plainly, sitting on his own porch step in the fading afternoon light.

"You're talking about the whole valley," he said slowly. "Not just me."

"I'm talking about an ending," she said. "Every place this practice has ever touched carries some version of what Amity Falls carries now, quietly, most of them never even knowing it. What you're carrying, Dev, if it's allowed to complete the way it wants to, doesn't only end your own debt. It could end all of it, everywhere, permanently, the way generations of careful, well-meaning people have failed to end it precisely because they kept choosing the smaller, safer, more selfish mercy instead."

"Selfish." The word came out sharper than he meant it to, anger finally cutting through the vertigo. "You think it's selfish that they want me alive."

"I think it's understandable," she said, and for the first time something like genuine sorrow crossed her face, quick and real and gone almost before Dev could be certain he'd seen it at all. "I would want the people who love me to choose me too, Dev, over an ending too large and too costly for any of us to fully comprehend. I'm not asking you to hate them for it. I'm asking you to understand that their love, however real, is also the reason this has never once, in all the generations we've watched it, actually ended."

Dev's hand tightened against the doorframe. "And what happens to me, in your version. If it finishes completely instead of scattering out safely the way they're planning."

The woman didn't answer immediately, and the silence told him more than any answer could have.

"I don't know," she admitted finally, and something about the honesty of it was almost worse than a lie would have been. "No one's carried a completion that large in living memory. We believe it's survivable, based on the old accounts, the ones written down before people stopped writing such things down at all. We believe the cost would be significant. I won't stand here and tell you it's free, because I don't actually know that it is, and you deserve better than a comfortable lie dressed up as comfort."

"That's supposed to convince me."

"It's supposed to be honest with you," she said. "Which is more than most people in your life have managed to be, in my experience, when the truth is this uncomfortable to hold." She rose slowly from the step, unhurried, brushing off her coat with the same unremarkable calm she'd arrived with. "I'm not here to force anything, Dev. I told the man who came before me that force rarely works on people worth choosing, and I meant it. I'm only here to make sure you have the whole choice in front of you, not just the half your friends are comfortable offering."

"They're not hiding anything from me."

"Aren't they?" Her head tilted slightly, something almost pitying in the gesture. "Ask them, when you go back inside, whether they've considered what happens to the next carrier, two generations from now, if your ending is the safe, scattered kind instead of the true, complete one. Ask them whether saving you and ending this forever were ever actually the same goal in their minds, or whether they simply assumed you'd prefer the version that costs you less without asking whether you'd prefer the version that costs the world more, or less, in your place."

She was three steps down the path before Dev found his voice again. "Wait."

She turned back, patient, entirely unsurprised.

"If I say no," Dev said. "If I choose their version instead of yours. What happens then."

"Then the debt scatters the way it's scattered before," she said. "Diminished, survivable, buried again for another generation or two before it finds its way to someone else's child, the way it found its way to you. And we wait, Dev, the way we have always waited, patient enough to see whether the next carrier, or the one after that, finally understands what you're being asked to understand tonight." Something in her expression softened, briefly, into something almost like genuine regret. "I hope, for your sake, that you get the easier ending. I hope, for everyone else's sake who might otherwise inherit this after you, that you don't take it."

She walked on into the deepening dusk without waiting for a response, unhurried, unremarkable, gone around the bend of the road before Dev's legs finally remembered how to move, carrying him back inside through the door he should have retreated through the moment he first saw her sitting there.

Priya was on him the instant he crossed the threshold, reading something in his face that made her abandon whatever she'd been doing at the counter and cross the kitchen in three quick strides. "Dev. What happened. Who was that."

He opened his mouth to answer, and found, for one terrible, honest moment, that he genuinely didn't know which version of the truth he wanted to tell her first: the frightening one, about a woman offering him an ending too large to fully understand, or the harder one, the one that had lodged itself somewhere under his ribs and wouldn't stop asking its question no matter how firmly he tried to set it down.

"Dev." Priya's hands found his shoulders, steadying him the way she used to steady suspects on the worst nights of her shift, before either of them had learned to be gentle with each other again. "Look at me. You're safe. Whoever that was, they're gone now. Tell me what she said."

He told her. All of it, the way he'd promised himself two weeks ago he'd tell her everything from now on, no matter how it sounded, no matter how much it might frighten her. Mara and Caleb came in from the other room partway through, drawn by the tension in Priya's voice, and Dev found himself repeating pieces of it a second time, the total ending, the diminished scattering, the honest uncertainty about what survival would even mean if the larger version happened instead.

Wren arrived last, breathless from having run the last stretch of road when Caleb texted her, and by the time Dev finished, the kitchen had gone very quiet, the fire in the stove the only sound besides his own unsteady breathing.

"She's lying," Priya said first, fierce and immediate, the words coming before she'd fully thought them through. "She has to be lying. There's no version of this where letting it consume the whole valley is somehow the kinder choice."

"I don't think she was lying," Dev said quietly, and hated how much it cost him to say it out loud. "I think that's what scares me most. She didn't sound like someone selling me something. She sounded like someone who actually believed it."

Mara had gone very still, the way she did when a structural problem revealed a flaw she hadn't accounted for. "She asked you whether we'd considered the next carrier. Two generations from now."

"Yeah."

"Have we?" Mara looked around the kitchen, at Caleb, at Priya, at Wren, none of them quite meeting her eyes. "Have any of us actually asked that question, or have we all just assumed that saving Dev and ending this were automatically the same thing?"

Nobody answered right away, and the silence itself was an answer none of them wanted to sit with.

"It doesn't matter," Priya said finally, sharper than she meant to, fear sharpening into something closer to anger. "Even if she's right, even if there's some version of this that ends it forever, I am not gambling my brother's life on an uncertain account written down by people who've spent generations deciding other people's costs were acceptable to pay. We save Dev. We save him the way we know how to, the way that's actually survivable. Whatever comes after that, comes after."

"I agree," Caleb said quietly. "But I think we need to actually say it out loud, decide it deliberately instead of just assuming it, because she's not wrong that we haven't. Not yet."

Dev sat down heavily at the table, the adrenaline of the porch finally draining out of him, leaving something colder and more exhausted behind. "I don't want to be the reason this keeps happening to someone else," he said. "I don't want some kid two generations from now standing on a porch listening to the same offer I just heard."

"Then we make sure they don't have to," Wren said, sliding into the chair beside him, her voice steadier than her wind-reddened face suggested she felt. "Not by letting you pay a price nobody can even promise the size of. By actually finishing the job properly this time. Finding a way to end it that doesn't just scatter the debt and hope it stays quiet for another eighty years."

"Is that even possible," Dev asked. "Or are we just telling ourselves a kinder version of the same lie she told me."

Nobody had an answer to that either, not yet, and the fire in the stove popped once, twice, marking the silence the way it always seemed to in this kitchen when the truth outran anyone's ability to comfort it.
