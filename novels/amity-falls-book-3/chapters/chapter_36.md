CHAPTER THIRTY-SIX

The moment Dev's hands closed around the center stake, Priya felt something shift in the air itself, a pressure change she couldn't name, the same way the valley sometimes felt heavier just before a storm broke.

Wren gave the count, her voice pitched to carry across the circle without shouting, steady despite everything watching from the tree line. "Contact in three. Two. One."

Ten hands settled against ten stakes in near-unison, and Priya felt her own palm meet the frost-slicked wood with a jolt that traveled up her arm and settled somewhere behind her ribs, not pain exactly, more like the moment a held breath finally releases, except the breath belonged to someone else entirely.

Above them, the eclipse slid deeper into totality, the last curved sliver of moonlight vanishing behind the earth's shadow, and the orchard field plunged into a darkness that felt, to Priya, less like nightfall than like something ancient finally being permitted to surface.

Dev's whole body went rigid at the center stake, his head tipping back, a low sound escaping him that wasn't quite pain and wasn't quite relief, and Priya watched, helpless and terrified, as something she couldn't see but could absolutely feel began moving outward from him in ten directions at once, finding the ten waiting channels the group had spent six weeks building toward this exact moment.

At the northern point, Odette's whole body went taut, her eyes squeezed shut, her weathered hands gripping the stake with a strength that belied her age, and Priya heard her murmur something under her breath, words in no language Priya recognized, syllables that felt older than the orchard itself, older than the valley, carried down through whatever fragmentary teaching had passed to her from the folklore she'd spent her whole life half-believing.

At the eastern point, Farrow made a sound like a man being struck, staggering slightly against his stake before catching himself, his breath coming in short, controlled bursts that Priya recognized from somewhere, some technique the retired teacher had clearly practiced in the privacy of his own home in the days since volunteering, bracing himself deliberately for exactly this moment.

"It's working," Mara said from somewhere behind Priya, her voice tight with something between relief and disbelief. "Look at the distribution. It's actually working, evenly, the way we designed it."

Priya couldn't look anywhere but at Dev, at her brother's face contorted with an effort that had nothing to do with physical strength, watching decades of buried weight finally begin, however painfully, to move.

Then, from the tree line, a single gunshot cracked across the still night air.

The sound tore through the ritual's careful rhythm like a blade through fabric, and Priya felt the whole circle lurch, ten hands flinching against ten stakes, the delicate balance Mara had engineered wavering violently under the sudden, sharp intrusion of violence into a space that had, until that instant, belonged entirely to the ring.

"That wasn't us," Yusuf's voice barked over the radio, sharp with fury. "That came from Drake's line. Hold positions, hold positions, do not break the circle."

At the southwest point, Castellan cried out, and Priya's head snapped toward him in time to see him stumble, not shot, she realized with a rush of relief, but staggered by the sudden disruption in whatever current was passing through him, his young face white with a pain that had clearly sharpened rather than eased.

"Steady," Odette called out, her own voice strained but carrying across the circle with surprising force. "Whatever they're trying to do, we don't give them the reaction they want. Hold your stake, Castellan. Hold it."

Castellan's jaw clenched, and Priya watched him wrestle his own body back under control, planting his feet more firmly, gripping the stake with white-knuckled determination even as sweat began beading on his forehead despite the freezing air.

A second shot rang out, closer this time, and Priya's training screamed at her to break position, to draw her weapon, to do something other than stand rooted at her own point in the circle with her hand locked against a stake that seemed, impossibly, to be humming now beneath her palm.

"They're trying to spook the ring into breaking early," Caleb called from his position near Dev, his voice tight with barely controlled fury. "Don't give it to them. Whatever's happening out there, our job is right here."

Priya forced herself to hold, every instinct fighting against six weeks of rehearsal, and felt, for the first time, the actual weight beginning to move through her own channel, a fragment of grief that wasn't hers, sharp and sudden and aching, someone's lost memory of a child's laughter settling into a space inside her chest that had been empty a moment before.

"I feel it," she gasped, the words escaping before she could stop them. "Dev, I feel it, it's real, it's actually happening."

"Stay with it," Dev's voice came back, strained but clear, cutting through his own visible agony with the same hardened resolve he'd carried since Halloran's death. "All of you, stay with it, whatever they're trying to do out there."

Mara's voice cut across the chaos, sharp and controlled, the engineer in her refusing to let panic override calculation even as gunfire continued to punctuate the tree line's darkness. "The distribution's holding. Whatever they're shooting at, it isn't disrupting the actual mechanism, only trying to disrupt us. Hold. Everyone hold."

A third shot came, and this time Priya heard a cry from the tree line itself, one of the deputies, she realized with cold horror, someone on their own perimeter taking fire rather than simply hearing it.

"Medic," Yusuf's voice barked over the radio, urgent now in a way that made Priya's stomach drop. "Deputy Hollis is hit, I need eyes on him now, but nobody breaks that circle, do you hear me, nobody."

Priya's whole body screamed to go to her fallen colleague, every year of training and instinct demanding she abandon her post and render aid, and she felt the same terrible tension mirrored in Caleb's face across the circle, in Farrow's white-knuckled grip, in every member of the ring caught between the ritual's unbreakable demand and the very real violence unfolding just beyond the tree line's edge.

"Hold," Dev said again, and something in his voice this time carried a weight Priya had never heard from her brother before, an authority earned through six weeks of fear finally crystallizing into absolute, unshakeable command. "Whatever's happening out there, Hollis needs us to finish this more than he needs us to break formation. If this fails now, if it scatters wrong because we panicked, everything he's bleeding for tonight was for nothing."

Priya held her stake, tears she didn't have time to acknowledge streaking cold down her face, and felt the weight continue moving through her, fragment after fragment of grief that belonged to strangers, to Ruth Calloway's husband's voice, to a mother's memory of her daughter's first steps, decades of careful, surgical harvesting finally beginning to scatter the way it was always meant to, evenly, deliberately, into ten willing hands instead of one broken woman's endless burden.

Above them, the eclipse held its full, dark totality, indifferent to gunfire or grief or the desperate, unbreakable circle of people fighting to finish what six weeks of preparation had built toward, and somewhere beyond the tree line, Corwin Drake watched his own carefully planned disruption fail to break the one thing he'd traveled decades and killed a man to prevent.
