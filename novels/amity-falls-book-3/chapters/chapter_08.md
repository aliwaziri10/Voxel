CHAPTER EIGHT

The gap this time wasn't four minutes, or twenty, or even the several hours of the third blackout. It was an entire afternoon, swallowed whole, and Dev came back to himself standing in the middle of a wheat field three miles outside town with no memory of leaving his own kitchen.

The sun told him it was late afternoon. His phone told him it was four hours since he'd last checked it, sitting at his own table with a cup of coffee, waiting for Priya to finish a shift. His shoes told him he'd walked the whole distance, mud dried into the treads, grass seed caught in his socks. None of it told him where he'd actually been in his own mind for those four hours, or what he'd been doing while his body carried itself three miles from home without him.

He called Priya first, his hands shaking too badly to manage more than a few words before she cut him off and told him to stay exactly where he was, that she was coming, that Mara and Caleb were already being called.

He sat down in the wheat, because standing suddenly felt like more effort than his legs could manage, and that was when the memory surfaced.

It didn't feel like his own. That was the first and worst thing about it, the immediate, visceral wrongness of a memory arriving in his mind with none of the texture his own memories carried, no sense of having lived it himself. He was standing somewhere cold, a stone room, candlelight instead of electric light, and a woman's voice was speaking words he didn't recognize but somehow understood anyway, a kind of understanding that bypassed language entirely and landed directly as meaning. *This is the shape of the debt. This is what it costs to keep it small.* He saw hands, not his own hands, older, weathered, holding something small and dark that he couldn't quite make out before the image dissolved as suddenly as it had arrived, leaving him gasping in the middle of a wheat field with tears on his face he hadn't noticed starting.

By the time Priya's car came tearing up the access road, Dev had curled forward with his arms wrapped around his knees, shaking hard enough that his teeth had started to chatter despite the mild afternoon warmth.

"Dev." Priya was out of the car before it had fully stopped, crossing the field at a run, dropping to her knees in the wheat beside him. "Dev, look at me. Are you hurt. Can you tell me what happened."

"I don't know what happened to me for four hours," Dev said, his voice raw and unsteady. "But something happened after. Right before you got here. I saw something, Priya. Someone else's memory. It wasn't mine. It felt like it landed inside me from somewhere else, and I don't know whose it was, or why it chose me, or if this is just the start of a lot more of it."

Mara and Caleb arrived minutes later, Wren close behind them, and the five of them stood together in the field while Dev, still shaking, tried to describe the fragment as precisely as he could manage. The stone room. The candlelight. The voice speaking words that meant something even though he'd never heard the language. The old hands holding something dark and small.

Wren went very still as he described it. "That sounds like a ritual space," she said slowly. "Not a home. Somewhere built specifically for doing this kind of work. I've never seen anything like it in the valley itself, not in any of the places I've read."

"It's not the valley," Mara said quietly, working through it with the same careful precision she'd once applied to structural drawings, mapping load paths and stress points onto something far less solid than steel and concrete. "Think about it. If this is hereditary, if it's coming from the Whitlock side rather than anything local to Amity Falls, then whatever Dev is picking up might not be a memory of anything that happened here at all. It might be older. From wherever the Whitlock line actually comes from, before any of it touched this valley."

"That's not possible," Priya said, though her voice carried more fear than conviction. "How would he have access to a memory from before he was even born, from a family line he didn't even know he belonged to a week ago."

"I don't know that it's impossible," Wren said carefully. "If the bargain moves through blood the way Denise thinks it does, maybe it doesn't just pass down a hunger. Maybe it passes down fragments of what came before too. Echoes of every hand that's carried it, going back as far as the blood itself goes."

Dev made a small, broken sound at that, pressing the heels of his hands against his eyes. "So it's not just going to take things from me. It's going to show me things that aren't mine to see, from people I never knew existed, and I have no idea how many more of those are waiting inside whatever this is."

Caleb crouched down in front of him, steady and grounding the way he'd been since the porch step conversation days earlier. "One thing at a time," he said. "Right now you're safe, you're here, and we're not letting you sit alone with this. Whatever comes next, comes next together."

"That's not an answer," Dev said, an edge of real anger cutting through the fear for the first time since this had all started. "None of you have answers. Denise doesn't have answers. Wren doesn't have answers. Everyone keeps saying together like it's a solution instead of just a nicer word for waiting to see how bad this gets before anyone actually knows what to do."

The field went quiet, the anger landing harder than anyone had expected, and Priya reached for her brother's hand only to have him pull it back, immediately regretting it from the flash of hurt on her face but too raw himself in the moment to soften it.

"I'm sorry," Dev said after a long, ragged breath. "I didn't mean that at you. I meant it at all of it. The whole situation. I'm scared, Priya, and being scared makes me want to snap at the people trying hardest to help me, which isn't fair, and I know it isn't fair, and I can't seem to stop myself anyway."

"You don't have to apologize for being scared," Priya said quietly, reaching for his hand again, and this time he let her take it. "You get to be angry about this. You get to be exactly as scared as you actually are. I just need you to keep telling us, even when it's ugly, even when it's unfair, because the alternative is you carrying it alone the way Eleanor did, and we already know how that story ends."

Dev let out a long, shaking breath, some of the fight draining out of him, replaced by something closer to exhausted resignation. "What do we do about the memory. The one that isn't mine. Do I just wait for more of them."

"We start documenting them," Mara said, already thinking several steps ahead the way she always did when a problem finally had enough shape to work with. "Every detail, every fragment, no matter how small or strange. If these are echoes from the Whitlock line, they might eventually tell us something none of the records can. A face. A place. Something that helps us understand what we're actually dealing with, beyond guesses and folklore."

"And if it's not helpful," Dev said, quiet and tired. "If it's just more terrifying things landing in my head with no purpose except to scare me."

"Then we still write it down," Wren said, gentler than her usual bluntness. "Because even the parts that don't make sense yet sometimes make sense later. That's how the well worked. Half of what mattered didn't mean anything until we already knew the whole shape of the thing."

The sun had started sinking toward the tree line by the time they finally got Dev back into the car, wheat seed still caught in his socks, the memory of a stone room and a stranger's careful hands sitting heavy and unfamiliar in a mind that was, piece by piece, no longer entirely his own to navigate alone.
