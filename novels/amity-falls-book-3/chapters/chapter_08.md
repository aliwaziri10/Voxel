CHAPTER EIGHT

The gap this time wasn't four minutes, or twenty, or even the several hours of the third blackout. It was an entire afternoon, swallowed whole, and Dev came back to himself standing in the middle of a wheat field three miles outside town with no memory of leaving his own kitchen, no sense of transition at all between one moment and the next, as if someone had simply cut the film and spliced a different scene into the gap.

The sun told him it was late afternoon, hanging low and orange over the tree line in a way that made his shadow stretch long and strange across the stubbled wheat. His phone told him it was four hours since he'd last checked it, sitting at his own table with a cup of coffee, waiting for Priya to finish a shift. His shoes told him he'd walked the whole distance, mud dried into the treads, grass seed caught in his socks, small burrs snagged in the laces like evidence gathered by a body that had gone somewhere without asking permission from the mind meant to be steering it. None of it told him where he'd actually been in his own head for those four hours, or what he'd been doing while his body carried itself three miles from home without him, alone and vulnerable in ways that made his stomach turn to consider once the shock had faded enough to let real fear back in.

He called Priya first, his hands shaking too badly to manage more than a few words before she cut him off and told him to stay exactly where he was, that she was coming, that Mara and Caleb were already being called, her voice carrying the steady calm she'd perfected over years of emergency calls that demanded composure rather than panic.

He sat down in the wheat, because standing suddenly felt like more effort than his legs could manage, his whole body trembling with an exhaustion that felt older than four hours of missing time should account for, and that was when the memory surfaced.

It didn't feel like his own. That was the first and worst thing about it, the immediate, visceral wrongness of a memory arriving in his mind with none of the texture his own memories carried, no sense of having lived it himself, no anchor to any moment he could place in his own history. He was standing somewhere cold, a stone room, candlelight instead of electric light casting shifting shadows against rough-hewn walls, and a woman's voice was speaking words he didn't recognize but somehow understood anyway, a kind of understanding that bypassed language entirely and landed directly as meaning, whole and complete, the way a smell can carry an entire memory before conscious thought catches up to it. *This is the shape of the debt. This is what it costs to keep it small.* He saw hands, not his own hands, older, weathered, spotted with age and hard work, holding something small and dark that he couldn't quite make out before the image dissolved as suddenly as it had arrived, leaving him gasping in the middle of a wheat field with tears on his face he hadn't noticed starting, his chest heaving with breaths that felt too shallow to be enough.

By the time Priya's car came tearing up the access road, dust rising behind it in a long pale trail, Dev had curled forward with his arms wrapped around his knees, shaking hard enough that his teeth had started to chatter despite the mild afternoon warmth still lingering in the air.

"Dev." Priya was out of the car before it had fully stopped, crossing the field at a run, dropping to her knees in the wheat beside him, her hands finding his shoulders, his face, checking him over with the frantic thoroughness of someone who'd spent the entire drive imagining the worst. "Dev, look at me. Are you hurt. Can you tell me what happened."

"I don't know what happened to me for four hours," Dev said, his voice raw and unsteady, the words coming out in fragments between shaking breaths. "But something happened after. Right before you got here. I saw something, Priya. Someone else's memory. It wasn't mine. It felt like it landed inside me from somewhere else, like it was dropped into me rather than remembered, and I don't know whose it was, or why it chose me, or if this is just the start of a lot more of it, more strangers' lives crowding into whatever's left of my own head."

Mara and Caleb arrived minutes later, headlights sweeping across the darkening field, Wren close behind them on a bicycle she'd apparently ridden the whole distance without stopping, and the five of them stood together in the field while Dev, still shaking, tried to describe the fragment as precisely as he could manage, each detail costing him visible effort to retrieve and hold steady long enough to speak aloud.

The stone room. The candlelight. The voice speaking words that meant something even though he'd never heard the language, not once, not anywhere in his life that he could recall. The old hands holding something dark and small, cupped like they were protecting it or perhaps grieving it.

Wren went very still as he described it, her usual restless energy going suddenly, uncharacteristically quiet. "That sounds like a ritual space," she said slowly. "Not a home. Somewhere built specifically for doing this kind of work, deliberately, with intention. I've never seen anything like it in the valley itself, not in any of the places I've read or heard described, not even in Odette's oldest stories."

"It's not the valley," Mara said quietly, working through it with the same careful precision she'd once applied to structural drawings, mapping load paths and stress points onto something far less solid than steel and concrete, her mind visibly assembling the pieces into a shape the rest of them hadn't reached yet. "Think about it. If this is hereditary, if it's coming from the Whitlock side rather than anything local to Amity Falls, then whatever Dev is picking up might not be a memory of anything that happened here at all. It might be older. From wherever the Whitlock line actually comes from, before any of it touched this valley, before Ambrose or whoever came before him ever set foot anywhere near here."

"That's not possible," Priya said, though her voice carried more fear than conviction, the protest of someone who wanted very badly for the world to still make the kind of sense it had made a week ago. "How would he have access to a memory from before he was even born, from a family line he didn't even know he belonged to a week ago, from people who might have died a century before he was born."

"I don't know that it's impossible," Wren said carefully, choosing her words with the caution she'd learned the hard way in Book Two's costliest moments. "If the bargain moves through blood the way Denise thinks it does, maybe it doesn't just pass down a hunger. Maybe it passes down fragments of what came before too. Echoes of every hand that's carried it, going back as far as the blood itself goes, memory riding alongside whatever else gets inherited, folded into the same inheritance as the hunger itself."

Dev made a small, broken sound at that, pressing the heels of his hands against his eyes like he could physically hold back whatever else might be waiting to surface. "So it's not just going to take things from me. It's going to show me things that aren't mine to see, from people I never knew existed, dead a hundred years or more, and I have no idea how many more of those are waiting inside whatever this is, how many strangers I'm going to have to carry pieces of before this is over."

Caleb crouched down in front of him, steady and grounding the way he'd been since the porch step conversation days earlier, his presence a deliberate anchor in the middle of the chaos. "One thing at a time," he said. "Right now you're safe, you're here, and we're not letting you sit alone with this. Whatever comes next, comes next together, every piece of it, no matter how strange or frightening."

"That's not an answer," Dev said, an edge of real anger cutting through the fear for the first time since this had all started, sharper than any of them had heard from him before. "None of you have answers. Denise doesn't have answers. Wren doesn't have answers. Everyone keeps saying together like it's a solution instead of just a nicer word for waiting to see how bad this gets before anyone actually knows what to do about it, before anyone can actually fix anything."

The field went quiet, the anger landing harder than anyone had expected, the wind seeming to still along with the group's collective breath, and Priya reached for her brother's hand only to have him pull it back, immediately regretting it from the flash of hurt on her face but too raw himself in the moment to soften it.

"I'm sorry," Dev said after a long, ragged breath, his shoulders slumping under the weight of his own outburst. "I didn't mean that at you. I meant it at all of it. The whole situation. I'm scared, Priya, and being scared makes me want to snap at the people trying hardest to help me, which isn't fair, and I know it isn't fair, and I can't seem to stop myself anyway, like the fear needs somewhere to go and you're just the nearest target."

"You don't have to apologize for being scared," Priya said quietly, reaching for his hand again, and this time he let her take it, his fingers closing around hers like something drowning finally finding purchase. "You get to be angry about this. You get to be exactly as scared as you actually are, as loud or as ugly as it needs to be. I just need you to keep telling us, even when it's ugly, even when it's unfair, because the alternative is you carrying it alone the way Eleanor did, and we already know how that story ends, decades of silence that solved nothing and only delayed the reckoning."

Dev let out a long, shaking breath, some of the fight draining out of him, replaced by something closer to exhausted resignation, his body finally allowing itself to sag against Priya's shoulder. "What do we do about the memory. The one that isn't mine. Do I just wait for more of them, sitting around wondering when the next one's going to land."

"We start documenting them," Mara said, already thinking several steps ahead the way she always did when a problem finally had enough shape to work with, her voice steadying with the certainty of someone who'd found, at last, a task she knew how to do. "Every detail, every fragment, no matter how small or strange it seems in the moment. If these are echoes from the Whitlock line, they might eventually tell us something none of the records can. A face. A place. Something that helps us understand what we're actually dealing with, beyond guesses and folklore and seventy-year-old marriage records."

"And if it's not helpful," Dev said, quiet and tired, his voice barely above a whisper now. "If it's just more terrifying things landing in my head with no purpose except to scare me half to death."

"Then we still write it down," Wren said, gentler than her usual bluntness, crouching down to Dev's level so their eyes met evenly. "Because even the parts that don't make sense yet sometimes make sense later. That's how the well worked. Half of what mattered didn't mean anything until we already knew the whole shape of the thing, until pieces that seemed random suddenly clicked into place."

The sun had started sinking toward the tree line by the time they finally got Dev back into the car, wheat seed still caught in his socks, the memory of a stone room and a stranger's careful hands sitting heavy and unfamiliar in a mind that was, piece by piece, no longer entirely his own to navigate alone, every mile back toward town a reminder of how far his body had carried him without permission, and how much farther this thing still intended to go.
