CHAPTER TWO

Dev never got the chance to rehearse the conversation the way he'd planned, because Thursday came before he was ready for it, and he was still halfway through deciding how to start when the fourth blackout took the decision entirely out of his hands.

He'd made it to Priya's porch steps, the good bottle of wine he'd bought that afternoon still in one hand, when the world simply folded shut around him mid-sentence, taking whatever he'd been about to say and swallowing it whole.

What little he registered from inside it came later, in fragments, the way a dream sometimes surfaces hours after waking rather than all at once. A pressure behind his eyes, the same pressure he'd felt at the stone wall two days earlier, like a fist pressing outward from the inside of his skull rather than in. A sound like wind moving through something that wasn't wind at all, closer to voices layered on top of each other than to weather, though he could never afterward pin down a single word of it, no matter how many times he turned the memory over trying to find an edge to hold onto. And underneath all of it, the unmistakable sense of being watched from the inside, as if he'd quietly stepped back to make room for someone else standing at the controls, patient, unhurried, in no rush at all to introduce itself.

Priya, standing in her open doorway watching him climb the steps, saw the whole thing happen in real time, and it was worse, far worse, than anything Dev himself had experienced in his own three blackouts, because she had no gap in her own memory to soften it. She watched her brother's eyes go suddenly distant mid-word, watched his body sway, watched the bottle of wine slip from fingers that had simply forgotten, for a moment, that they were holding anything at all.

"Dev." She caught the bottle before it shattered on the steps, catching his arm with her other hand a half second later as his knees started to buckle. "Dev, look at me."

He didn't look at her. His eyes stayed open but unfocused, fixed on a middle distance that wasn't the porch, wasn't her face, wasn't anything Priya could see. His lips moved slightly, no sound coming out, and for one terrible, suspended moment Priya thought of every worst possibility at once, seizure, stroke, a catastrophic failure of a body she'd only just finished learning to trust again after everything the county had put it through during his trial.

She did what six years on patrol had trained into her without conscious thought, pressing two fingers to the pulse point at his wrist even as her own heart hammered, counting automatically, and noting, with the small detached part of her mind that never fully switched off no matter how frightened the rest of her was, the exact time on her watch. Nine forty-one. Whatever this was, it would have a start time and an end time, the way every incident she'd ever logged in six years of patrol had a start time and an end time, and instinct told her that detail might matter later even if she couldn't yet say why.

Then, as quickly as it had come, it passed. Dev blinked, staggered slightly, and found himself sitting on Priya's porch steps with no memory of sitting down, his sister's hand gripping his arm hard enough to leave marks, her face white with a fear he'd never once seen her wear, not even during the worst of his own trial.

"Priya." His voice came out rough, disoriented, and there it was again, the same strange metallic sweetness at the back of his throat he'd tasted at the stone wall, along with a headache already building behind his eyes with a speed that frightened him more than the confusion did. "What happened. What just happened."

"Four minutes." Priya checked her watch again, her voice gone flat and procedural in the way he recognized from every time she'd talked herself through something too big to feel all at once. "Nine forty-one to nine forty-five. You tell me the rest." She knelt in front of him, both hands now on his shoulders, steadying him and herself at the same time. "You went somewhere, Dev. Right in front of me. Your eyes were open and you weren't there. How long has this been happening?"

He wanted, badly, to lie again, to reach for the same automatic reassurance he'd been offering everyone for days now, the easy half-truths that had gotten him through worse conversations than this one. But looking at his sister's face, white and frightened and utterly without suspicion, without even a trace of the old wariness that used to live behind her eyes whenever something about him didn't add up, he found he couldn't manage it.

"Three days," he admitted. "This makes four. The first one, I was working, lost maybe four minutes. Second one, walking home, lost twenty. Third one." He stopped, swallowed hard, the metallic taste sharpening at the back of his tongue as though the memory itself could bring it back. "Third one I lost most of an afternoon. Woke up out by the old Voss property wall with a rock in my hand I don't remember picking up."

Priya's grip on his shoulders tightened. "Why didn't you tell me. Any of it. The very first time."

"Because I know how it sounds." The words came out harder than he meant them to, old shame surfacing fast, the same shame that had sat under every conversation he'd had with anyone in this town for the better part of a year now. "Because the one person everyone still half expects to relapse is standing here telling you he's been losing hours of his own life and can't explain why, and I didn't want that to be the first thing you thought when you looked at me. Not after everything it took to get you looking at me the way you do now."

"Dev." Priya's voice cracked, the sound of it carrying nothing like suspicion, only pure, uncomplicated fear for him. "I wasn't going to think you'd relapsed. I was going to think you were sick, or hurt, or in danger, and I was going to want to help you. That's it. That's the whole thought. You don't get to make that decision for me anymore, deciding what I'm allowed to worry about on your behalf."

He looked at her, and the old, defensive posture he'd been carrying since long before his sentence, since maybe long before any of this, finally cracked under the weight of how plainly she meant it.

"I'm scared," he said, quiet, the admission costing him more than almost anything he'd said in the past year. "I don't know what's happening to me. It doesn't feel like anything I've heard of. It doesn't feel like a seizure, or a blood sugar crash, or anything a doctor's going to have a clean answer for. There's this taste in my mouth every time, after, metallic and sweet at once, and my head feels like something's pushing on it from the inside, right behind the eyes, like pressure looking for a way out. It feels like something's using my body while I'm not in it, and I don't know how to stop it, and I don't know who to ask."

Priya was quiet for a long moment, and Dev watched her mind visibly working, the same steady, procedural instinct that had carried her through every hard case since she'd pinned on a badge, now turned fully toward the one problem she couldn't approach with any procedure she'd ever actually been trained for.

"The Voss well," she said finally. "You said you woke up near there. With a stone in your hand."

"Yeah."

"Dev." Her expression went cold and still. "After everything that happened with Denise. After what we learned that place can do to people. You're telling me your body walked you there on its own, three separate times you can account for and God knows how many you can't, and picked something up."

Dev felt the same cold settle into his own chest, hearing it said out loud by someone else, someone whose instincts he'd learned, slowly and painfully, to trust more than his own. "I didn't think about it that way. I didn't want to think about it that way."

"We need to get you to Mara and Caleb tonight," Priya said, already standing, already pulling him up with her, the deputy and the sister moving as one single, decisive person for the first time since this had started. "And Wren, if she'll do it, though God knows I don't love asking her for another read after what the last one cost her. This isn't something we figure out over dinner and wine, Dev. Something's happening to you that's connected to what almost happened to this whole valley six weeks ago, and we don't have the luxury of waiting to see if it gets better on its own."

"And if it's what I think it might be." Dev's voice had gone very quiet. "If somehow, some way, whatever Denise carried for fifty years has found its way into me."

Priya didn't answer right away, and the silence between them stretched long enough that Dev understood, without her needing to say it, that she was thinking the exact same terrible thought he was.

"Then we deal with it," she said finally, steady despite everything Dev could see it cost her to sound that way. "The same way we dealt with everything else. Together, carefully, and a lot faster than we dealt with Denise, because I have a feeling, looking at you right now, that we don't have fifty years to figure this one out slowly."

She didn't let go of his arm the entire walk to her car, checking her watch once more as they went, an old habit now attached to a new fear, marking the time as though it might matter later exactly how long it had taken them to finally start moving. The porch light clicked off behind them on its timer, and neither of them looked back at it, both already thinking three steps ahead to the drive across town, to what they'd say to Mara, to how much of this Wren would need to know before she'd agree to anything.

Dev climbed into the passenger seat out of old habit, the same seat he'd taken as a kid every time their father had needed to drive somewhere after dark, and something about the familiarity of it, the worn fabric under his hands, the faint smell of Priya's coffee thermos rolling under the seat, steadied him more than any reassurance could have.

"You should eat something before we go," Priya said, starting the engine, her voice trying for normal and almost getting there. "Whatever that thing takes out of you, it's probably not going to leave more behind if your blood sugar's already low."

"I'm not hungry."

"I didn't ask if you were hungry." She pulled out of the driveway, headlights sweeping briefly across the wine bottle still sitting forgotten on the porch steps where she'd set it down. "There's a protein bar in the glovebox. Eat it. Humor your sister, who is currently doing an excellent job of not screaming."

Despite everything, despite the headache still building and the taste still sitting sour at the back of his throat, Dev found himself managing something close to a real laugh, small and short-lived but real. He found the bar, unwrapped it, ate it in three bites because she was watching to make sure he did.

"Better," she said, and reached over without looking away from the road to squeeze his hand once, hard, before putting hers back on the wheel.

At the second stop sign she pulled out her phone one-handed, thumb working the screen at a red light. "I'm calling ahead. Mara doesn't need to hear this cold when we walk in, and if Caleb's still out at the Petrov place, we need to know that now, not after we've driven the whole way for nothing."

"It's almost ten."

"He answers." She said it the way she said most things about Caleb these days, flat and certain, like a fact she'd already tested and filed away. The call connected after two rings, and Dev listened to his sister's half of it, watching the town slide by dark and familiar outside the window, porch lights and mailboxes he'd known his whole life suddenly looking strange to him, like scenery in a story he'd wandered into by accident. "It's me. It's Dev, he had another one, four minutes this time, right in front of me. No, he's talking, he's fine right now. I need you and Mara both tonight if you can. And Wren, if she's willing, but I'll ask her myself." A pause, Caleb's voice too low for Dev to make out the words. "Yeah. Twenty minutes."

She ended the call and set the phone in the cupholder, both hands back on the wheel, jaw tight in a way Dev recognized as the shape her worry took when she didn't have time to feel it properly yet.

"He's not going to make you feel stupid for waiting three days to say anything," she said, apparently reading it in Dev's silence. "Neither is Mara. I need you to actually believe that before we get there, because I watched your face just now and you're already bracing for it."

"Old habit."

"Break it tonight." She said it gently, but it wasn't a request. Neither of them said anything else the rest of the way, but Dev noticed she kept glancing at him at every stop sign, checking, the way she'd checked his pulse on the porch, the way she'd probably keep checking for as long as this lasted.

That decision, it seemed, had already been made, quietly and completely, sometime during the year he hadn't been watching for it.
