CHAPTER FOUR

Adelaide's letter had been read so many times since the wedding that its folds had softened to something closer to cloth than paper, and Mara handled it now with the same careful, practiced reverence she'd developed over six weeks of returning to it whenever the investigation needed grounding.

"Here," she said, spreading it flat on the kitchen table where Wren, Caleb, and Priya had gathered again the morning after the reading of the stone. "The Whitlock line. Read it slow this time. We skimmed past this the first ten times because we were looking for Denise, not for what came before her."

She traced the line with one finger, reading it aloud though everyone at the table had it half memorized by now.

*Ambrose Whitlock taught it to me, the autumn after my mother's death, in the same quiet way, I suspect, it was taught to him by someone before, and to that person by someone before that, stretching back further than this valley's own written history can account for.*

"Taught," Caleb said, turning the word over. "Not passed down. Not inherited. Taught, like a skill, like something you learn on purpose from a willing teacher."

"That's what we assumed it meant," Mara said. "Because that's how Adelaide describes her own experience of it. But Wren got a name off that stone last night without anyone teaching Dev anything. He's never met a soul who could have sat him down and explained a bargain like this to him, and he still came away from a single unexplained afternoon carrying that name like it had been waiting inside him the whole time."

Priya, sitting close beside Dev with one hand resting on the back of his chair, spoke carefully, the way she asked every hard question these days, weighing each word before it left her mouth. "You think it's blood. Not teaching at all, or not only teaching."

"I think it might be both," Wren said. "I think Adelaide's story tells us how it can spread on purpose, one frightened person choosing to pass it to another. But nothing in her letter says that's the only way it moves. She doesn't claim to know where it started. She just knows Whitlock taught her, and Whitlock had to learn it somewhere too, from somebody, and there's no guarantee that somebody chose to teach it any more deliberately than whatever's happening to Dev right now feels deliberate."

Dev sat very still through this, his hands wrapped around a coffee mug he hadn't touched in several minutes, listening to four people discuss the possible mechanics of something living inside him with the same detached, clinical language Mara had once used to discuss the structural load of an irrigation line.

"I don't buy it," Caleb said, more sharply than he probably meant to. "Not the blood part. Adelaide's whole letter is about choice, about what she chose to accept and what it cost her to accept it. If you're right, if this thing just picks people by blood whether they want it or not, then everything she wrote about weighing the decision, about knowing what she was agreeing to, none of that matters for Dev at all. He never got a decision. That's not the same story. That's a different, worse one."

"I'm not saying I like it either," Wren said, meeting his eyes evenly. "I'm saying the stone didn't ask Dev's permission before it handed him a name he'd never heard. Whatever this is, it already showed us it doesn't need consent the way Adelaide's version did. I'd rather we plan around the worse possibility and be wrong than plan around the kinder one and be wrong."

"That's not the same as knowing," Caleb said.

"No," Wren agreed. "It isn't. But we don't get to wait for knowing before we start moving, not with him losing whole afternoons already." She nodded toward Dev without quite looking at him, a settled instinct for not making a frightened man the direct subject of an argument about his own fate. "I'd rather argue about this with you now than explain to him later why we didn't."

Caleb didn't answer right away, and Mara watched something pass between him and Wren, not quite an apology and not quite a concession, but close enough to both that the room's tension eased a degree.

"Fine," Caleb said finally. "We plan for both. But I want it on record that I still think there's a version of this where somebody, somewhere, made a choice on Dev's behalf without telling him, and that's a different kind of wrong than blood just being blood."

"Noted," Mara said, already writing it down anyway, the same instinct that made her write down everything that might matter later even when she couldn't yet say why.

"Odette," Caleb said suddenly. "If anyone in this valley would know old family stories that never made it into any official record, it's her. She gave us the Denise thread, all those months back. If there's a version of this that runs through blood instead of choice, she might have heard a fragment of it somewhere along the way, even if she never understood what she was hearing."

Mara was already reaching for her jacket. "I'll go this morning. Wren, keep working the genealogy angle from this end, see what the county records actually say about Whitlock descendants, whether the line supposedly died out or just went quiet. Priya, Dev, stay together. If whatever's happening to him is accelerating the way it looks like it's accelerating, I don't want him alone for longer than it takes to walk to the bathroom."

Dev managed a thin, tired smile at that. "You say that like I have any say in it anymore."

"You don't," Priya said, gentle but immovable. "Not until we understand this better. I already watched you disappear into yourself once at my own front door. I'm not doing that again without someone close enough to catch you."

Odette's cottage sat at the far edge of the orchard road, and Mara found her exactly where she usually was at this hour, sitting on her front porch with a cup of tea going cold beside her, watching the morning light move slowly across her garden the way she'd been watching mornings move for longer than most of the valley had been alive. The apple trees behind the fence were already heavy with early fruit, and somewhere further off a dog barked twice and went quiet, the ordinary sounds of a morning that had no idea what was being discussed on the porch above it.

"I wondered when someone would come asking again," Odette said, before Mara had even reached the steps. "I heard about Dev. This valley doesn't keep secrets as well as it thinks it does." She gestured at the empty chair across from her without quite looking up, an old habit of hospitality that had survived every hard conversation this valley had put her through in the last six weeks. "Sit. Whatever you've come to ask, it'll go easier with tea in your hands, even tea as cold as mine's gotten."

"Then you already know why I'm here." Mara sat down across from her, the old porch boards creaking familiarly beneath her weight. "We need to know if you've ever heard anything, any old story, any half-remembered warning, about this thing passing through a family without anyone choosing it. Not Adelaide's kind of teaching. Something that just, arrives."

Odette set her cup down on the porch rail, the small clink of china against wood the only sound for a moment besides the dog somewhere off in the orchard rows. "You understand, most of what I could tell you is nothing. Old women's sayings, half-remembered from childhood, worn smooth from being repeated without anyone ever asking what they meant. I've spent sixty years assuming most of what my grandmother told me was decoration. Furniture for a story, not the story itself. It's only these past six weeks I've started wondering how much of that furniture was actually load-bearing the whole time."

"Anything you remember could matter," Mara said. "Even the parts you're sure are nothing."

"That's the trouble with these old sayings," Odette said, something wry and tired in her voice. "The ones that turn out to matter never announce themselves in advance. They sound exactly like the ones that don't."

Odette was quiet for a long moment, her eyes distant, reaching back through decades of stories the way she always did before deciding which ones were worth telling.

"My own grandmother used to say something," she said finally. "Not about Denise, not about anyone specific. Just an old saying, the kind you hear so many times as a child you stop noticing the words mean anything at all. She used to say, some debts choose their own collectors."

Mara felt something cold settle in her chest. "What does that mean."

"I never asked her, God rest her. I was young, and it sounded like nothing more than an old woman's turn of phrase, the kind every family has a few of." Odette wrapped both hands around her tea, more for the comfort of holding something than any hope of warmth left in it. "But I've thought about it more than once these past six weeks, since everything came out about Denise. I think, now, she might have been telling me something true, dressed up as nothing at all, the way this valley has always done with its most dangerous truths."

"Some debts choose their own collectors," Mara repeated slowly. "Not the person who created the debt. Not even someone taught to carry it on purpose. Just, whoever the debt decides is close enough, whenever it decides the time is right."

"That's how it reads to me, hearing it again now, with everything I know that I didn't know then." Odette's eyes met Mara's, sharp despite her age, carrying a weight that told Mara this conversation was costing the old woman a quiet grief of her own for a valley she'd loved her whole life and was only now beginning to fully understand. "I'm sorry I don't have more than that for you. An old saying and an old woman's guess."

"It's more than we had an hour ago," Mara said, echoing without meaning to the same words Wren had offered Dev the night before. "Thank you, Odette. Truly."

She walked back toward the kitchen with Odette's words turning over and over in her mind, some debts choose their own collectors, and thought about Dev standing at the well through the entire length of Denise's release, present at exactly the moment something ancient and starving had been asked to move, redistribute, find somewhere new to settle.

The orchard road ran quiet under the late-morning sun, gravel crunching underfoot, the same walk Mara had made a dozen times since Denise's case had first opened, though it had never once felt like this, heavy with a phrase she couldn't yet fully explain to anyone waiting for her back at the kitchen table. She found herself rehearsing how she'd say it to Caleb, to Wren, to Dev especially, turning the old woman's words over for an angle that sounded less like a verdict and more like information, and not finding one.

She thought about the word choose, and wondered, with a dread she hadn't let herself feel until this exact moment on the empty orchard road, whether that word had ever, in the whole history of this valley, meant anything close to mercy. By the time the substation roof came into view above the tree line, she'd decided there was no gentler way to carry it back than exactly as Odette had given it to her, plain and unadorned, and that Dev deserved the truth delivered whole rather than softened into something easier to hand over.

Dev was still at the kitchen table when she walked in, the same untouched coffee gone fully cold in front of him, and he looked up at her with an expression that told her he'd already guessed, from her face alone, that she hadn't come back with nothing.

"Tell me," he said, before she'd even set her bag down.

So she did, word for word, Odette's grandmother's saying laid out plain on the table between them the same way Wren had laid out the stone the night before. Some debts choose their own collectors. She watched Dev turn it over, watched him arrive, faster than she'd expected, at the same cold conclusion she'd carried the whole walk home.

"So it doesn't matter what I want," he said quietly. "It never did. Not with this."

"We don't know that yet," Priya said, though her voice lacked its usual certainty.

"You don't have to soften it," Dev said, and managed something almost like a smile, thin and tired but real. "I'd rather know exactly what we're up against than spend another day hoping it's something smaller than it is."
