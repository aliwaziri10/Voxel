CHAPTER SIX

Denise's cottage sat at the edge of the orchard land like it had grown there rather than been built, and Mara had visited often enough now, over the months since the well, that she no longer had to think about which gate latch stuck.

"You'll want the tea before you say whatever you've come to say," Denise told them from the doorway, before anyone had spoken a word. She looked older than she had at the well, softer somehow, the sharpness that had once made her seem ageless replaced by something closer to ordinary. Her hands shook slightly around the kettle handle. Eighty-eight years old now, aging plainly and honestly for the first time since the 1970s, and wearing every one of those years like a woman finally allowed to set down something heavy. "Whatever it is, it's sitting on all four of you like a stone."

Priya glanced at Dev, who had gone quiet the way he'd been going quiet more and more often lately, and answered for the group. "It's about the bargain, Denise. Something's happening that we don't fully understand yet."

Denise's hands stilled on the kettle. "Sit," she said, not unkindly. "All of you. I have a feeling this is going to take longer than tea."

They sat around her small kitchen table, Caleb easing into the chair across from Denise with the particular caution of a man who still occasionally caught himself flinching at rooms that reminded him of the worst months of his own life, though he'd told Mara once, quietly, that visiting Denise here never felt like one of those rooms. This one felt like an apology being lived out, slowly, in small kindnesses, and he'd made his peace with that months ago.

Priya laid it out plainly. Dev's blackouts. Wren's read on the stone. The name Whitlock. The buried Eleanor Whitlock, married and renamed, dead husband Ambrose whose cause of death had gone unrecorded in 1945. She did not soften any of it, and Mara watched Denise's face carefully as the story unfolded, watching for the flicker of recognition or denial she'd learned to read in this woman years ago, back when Denise had still been someone to catch rather than someone to trust.

What crossed Denise's face instead was something closer to grief.

"I always thought it might be possible," Denise said quietly, once Priya had finished. "That it could pass by blood, not just by choosing and teaching. I never had proof. Ambrose never told me one way or the other, and by the time I understood enough to ask the right questions, he was long past answering anything."

"You knew him," Dev said, his voice sharper than Mara had heard it in weeks. "You knew Ambrose Whitlock."

"I knew of him," Denise corrected gently. "Adelaide spoke of him rarely, and only ever in the careful way you speak of someone you're afraid of and grateful to in equal measure. She never called him cruel. She never called him kind either. She called him old, in a way that had nothing to do with years, and she said once that he'd told her the bargain finds the people who are ready to carry it, whether or not they ever agreed to be found."

"Dev didn't agree to anything," Priya said, an edge creeping into her voice that Mara recognized as fear wearing the shape of anger. "He didn't choose this. He didn't even know it was possible to choose it."

"No," Denise agreed. "And that's exactly what frightens me about what you're describing, because I chose mine, in a manner of speaking. Adelaide taught me, slowly, over months, and by the time I understood the full weight of what she was offering me, I'd already decided I wanted to carry it rather than let it fall to someone less prepared. I had years to grow into it. Years to learn how to take small and take carefully, to renew it on my own schedule instead of letting it take me by surprise." She looked at Dev with something that might have been the closest thing to fear Mara had ever seen on her face. "You're describing a boy who's had none of that. No teaching. No choosing. No years to grow into the size of the thing waking up in him."

"What happens," Caleb asked quietly, "to someone who gets none of that."

Denise was silent for a long moment, her hands wrapped around her own untouched teacup, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped low enough that Mara had to lean in to catch it.

"I don't know," Denise said. "I want to be honest with you, all of you, because I think honesty is the only thing I have left to offer that's actually worth something. I don't know what happens to an untrained carrier, because I have never known of one. Every account I ever heard, every fragment Adelaide ever let slip, every story Odette's grandmother apparently passed down, all of it assumed a teacher and a student, a slow apprenticeship into something enormous. Nobody ever described what happens when the blood simply decides on its own, with no one there to guide it."

"Guess," Dev said. His voice had gone rough. "Please. You've lived closer to this than anyone alive. Guess."

Denise studied him for a long moment, and Mara watched something pass over her face that looked almost like the weight of a decision being made in real time.

"If what Adelaide told me is even half true," Denise said slowly, "then the bargain doesn't wait patiently for the ready. It takes what it needs, on its own schedule, from whoever is carrying it, trained or not. I took small because I chose to take small, because I had the years and the guidance to understand that restraint was possible. Someone who never had that choice, who's frightened and untrained and completely unprepared for what's stirring in him, I think that person's body and mind absorb the full weight of a taking meant to be spread across a lifetime, compressed into whatever short window the bargain decides it wants." She looked directly at Dev. "I think it will not wait fifty years for you the way it waited for me. I think it's already moving faster than anything I ever experienced, and I think, if no one intervenes, it will eventually take everything it's owed in one motion instead of many small ones."

The kitchen went very quiet. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the orchard, the sound of it carrying faintly through Denise's old windows.

"You said you don't know for certain," Priya said finally. "You said this is a guess."

"It is," Denise said. "But it's an educated one, built from seventy years of living closer to this thing than any of you ever have, and I think you'd be foolish to treat it as anything less serious than what it is." She reached across the table, unexpectedly, and took Dev's hand in both of hers, her grip surprisingly strong for a woman her age. "I spent fifty years being afraid someone would catch me. I never once considered what it might feel like to be the one who could actually help, instead of the one who needed catching. I'd like to be that, now, if you'll let me. Whatever I know, whatever I remember, whatever small pieces of Adelaide's teaching I still carry, they're yours. All of you. For whatever they're worth against something I've never faced myself."

Dev's composure cracked slightly at that, the fear he'd been holding at arm's length all afternoon finally showing plainly on his face. "I don't want to end up like you did," he said. "Fifty years of hiding, taking small pieces of people I love without them knowing. I don't want that to be my whole life."

"I know," Denise said gently. "And I don't think that's the shape of what's coming for you, not exactly. I think what's coming is faster and more dangerous than what I lived, not the same slow, sustainable ache I carried. Which means the solution, whatever it turns out to be, likely won't look like mine either. You won't get fifty years to learn restraint. You'll need something else. Something quicker, and probably something none of us have tried before."

"That's not comforting," Dev said, a thin, unsteady attempt at humor that didn't quite land.

"No," Denise agreed, almost smiling. "But it's true, and I think you'd rather have true than comforting, given everything else you've already had to swallow this week."

Mara caught Caleb's eye across the table, and something passed between them without words, the shared understanding of two people who'd once sat in this same kitchen on the other side of a very different kind of fear, back when Denise had still been the mystery instead of the ally. Caleb reached over and set a hand briefly on Dev's shoulder, the gesture of a man who knew exactly what it felt like to have something enormous and unchosen living inside him, waiting.

"We're going to figure out the shape of it," Caleb said quietly. "Together. Faster than we figured out Denise's, because we already know more than we did the first time, and because we're not waiting fifty years to start."

Denise nodded slowly, releasing Dev's hand at last and settling back into her chair with the careful stiffness of a woman whose body had only recently begun to feel its true age.

"Then let's start with what I actually remember," she said. "Every fragment. Every half-finished lesson Adelaide ever gave me. It won't be everything you need, but it's more than any of you had yesterday, and I intend to make every year I have left count for something better than fifty years of hiding."

Outside, the light over the orchard had begun to shift toward evening, the same gold-going-gray Mara remembered from the day she'd first understood what Denise had been carrying, except this time the fear in the room belonged to someone new, and the woman who might have once been the danger sat instead at the center of the table, offering everything she had left to make sure it didn't happen the same way twice.
