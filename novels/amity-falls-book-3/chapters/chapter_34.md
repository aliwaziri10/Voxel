CHAPTER THIRTY-FOUR

The attack came on the fifth night, not at the silo, and not at any of the ten homes Priya had spent a week fortifying, but at the one place nobody had thought to guard closely enough: the stretch of county road between Farrow's house and the silo, where the retired teacher drove himself to every rehearsal out of a stubborn refusal to be chauffeured like an invalid.

Priya got the call at 9:47 p.m., Farrow's own voice on the line, tight with fear but admirably steady. "Truck's blocking the road past the old mill bridge," he said. "Two men out of it already. I'm not stopping. I'm going to try to reverse around them."

"Don't engage, don't get out of the car, I'm four minutes out," Priya said, already moving, snatching her keys off the hook by the door with one hand while she shouted for Caleb with the other. "Stay on the line."

What followed, Priya would later tell Yusuf in her flat, factual debrief voice, happened too fast for heroics and too slow for mercy. Farrow's reverse stalled against a second vehicle that had pulled in behind him while he was watching the men ahead, boxing him in cleanly, professionally, the kind of maneuver that spoke of rehearsal rather than opportunism. By the time Priya's headlights swung around the bend, one of the two men from the lead truck was already at Farrow's door, not forcing it, simply standing beside it with the unhurried patience of someone who expected the door to open on its own eventually.

Priya didn't slow down.

She came around the bend at a speed that made her own stomach lurch, cutting the wheel hard enough to put her cruiser broadside across the narrow road behind the second vehicle, effectively boxing the boxers, and was out of the car with her weapon drawn before the dust had finished settling.

"Sheriff's department," she called, her voice carrying the same flat authority she'd trained into it across fifteen years of exactly this kind of moment, though her heart was hammering hard enough that she could feel it in her throat. "Step away from the vehicle. Now."

The man at Farrow's door turned, unhurried, and Priya got her first real look at what Corwin Drake's people actually looked like: younger than she'd braced for, mid-thirties perhaps, dressed like someone heading to an ordinary job rather than an abduction, nothing about his posture suggesting panic even with a deputy's weapon leveled directly at him.

"We're not here for trouble, Deputy," he said, his voice carrying an unsettling echo of the same calm patience the woman on Dev's porch had used weeks ago. "We only wanted to talk with Mr. Farrow. He's under no obligation to listen, of course."

"You blocked his car on a public road at night with two vehicles," Priya said. "That's not a conversation, that's an ambush, and you're going to step back from that door right now or we're going to have a very different kind of conversation."

For one long, suspended moment, nobody moved, and Priya became acutely aware of the second man still sitting in the lead truck, hands visible on the wheel but his eyes fixed on her with an attention that felt less like fear and more like assessment, the way a predator studies an obstacle before deciding whether it's worth the trouble of removing.

Then, unhurried, the man at Farrow's door raised both hands and stepped back, exactly as instructed, and something in the ease of his compliance told Priya this hadn't been the plan at all, that whatever they'd intended to accomplish tonight had already failed the moment her headlights appeared around the bend.

"Understood," he said. "We'll go."

"You'll stay exactly where you are until backup arrives," Priya said, though even as the words left her mouth she understood, with a sinking certainty, that they wouldn't, that nothing about tonight's encounter had been designed to end in her custody.

She was right. The lead truck's engine roared to life before she'd finished speaking, and both vehicles peeled away in a single coordinated motion, executed with the same rehearsed precision as the ambush itself, gone around the bend and out of sight before Priya could do anything but watch them go, adrenaline curdling fast into fury at her own helplessness to stop it.

Caleb arrived four minutes later, breathless from having run the last stretch when his truck wouldn't start fast enough, and found Priya still standing in the road, weapon lowered now, watching Farrow climb slowly, shakily, out of his own car.

"I'm alright," Farrow said, before either of them could ask, though his hands were trembling badly enough that Priya doubted the claim even as he made it. "Scared out of ten years of my life, but alright."

"What did they say to you," Priya asked, gentler now, helping him to sit on the cruiser's bumper while Caleb radioed the details in to Yusuf.

"Nothing, really. That's the strange part." Farrow's voice wavered slightly, working through the shock in real time. "He just stood there by my window, smiling, like we had all the time in the world, and said Mr. Drake was hoping I'd reconsider my commitment to the ring before things got, and I'm quoting him exactly, deputy, 'unnecessarily complicated for everyone involved.'"

"A warning shot," Caleb said grimly, crouching beside Farrow to check him over properly, the same steady care he'd brought to Dev through every blackout. "Not an abduction attempt after all. A message."

"A message that says they know exactly which of the ten drives himself alone at night," Priya said, her fury sharpening now into something colder, more useful. "Which means they've been watching this house a great deal more closely than our security sweep accounted for."

By the time they made it back to the silo, the rest of the group had already gathered, alerted by Yusuf's radio call, and Priya found herself repeating the same flat, factual account three more times, first to Mara and Wren, then to Yusuf directly, then finally to Dev, who had gone very quiet and very still the moment he understood what almost happened.

"This is my fault," Dev said, the words coming out low and rough. "Farrow's out there alone on that road because of me, because of a choice I made asking him to be part of this."

"It's Drake's fault," Odette said firmly, arriving with the rest of the ring candidates who'd been roused by the news, her voice carrying the same steady authority she'd brought to every meeting since volunteering. "You didn't force any of us into that car, Dev. A man with a truck and two accomplices did that, choosing violence over persuasion because persuasion failed him with Halloran and it's failing him with all of us now too."

"She's right," Farrow said, still pale but steadier now, sitting wrapped in a blanket near the stove. "I've taught children for thirty years, Dev. I know the difference between a boy blaming himself for something done to him and a boy who actually did something wrong. You're the first one, not the second."

Yusuf moved to the center of the gathered group, his expression hard in a way Priya had rarely seen from him, the careful diplomat replaced entirely by something closer to a man done negotiating. "This changes our posture completely," he said. "No more individual travel for any of the ten, under any circumstances, starting tonight. Everyone relocates to guarded housing within walking distance of this silo until the eclipse. I don't care about convenience anymore. I care about not standing in front of Farrow's family explaining how we let this happen a second time, worse."

"They wanted a message," Mara said slowly, working through the tactics with the same cold clarity she brought to any structural problem. "Not a capture. That tells us something important. If Drake believed he could simply take one of the ten by force tonight, he would have tried. Instead he tested our response time, our coverage gaps, and retreated the moment real resistance appeared."

"Reconnaissance," Priya said, understanding landing with grim clarity. "He wasn't trying to take Farrow tonight. He was finding out exactly how fast we'd respond, and exactly how far he could push before we pushed back."

"Then he knows now," Dev said, some of the earlier guilt hardening back into the same cold resolve Priya had watched grow in him for weeks, "that we respond fast, and that we don't fold."

"He also knows Farrow drives himself at night," Wren said quietly. "Which means whatever gap let him learn that in the first place still exists. We patched one leak with Elias. We found the sightline gap in the orchard. This is a third hole in our security, and I don't think we should assume it's the last one."

The silo fell quiet at that, the weight of a third breach settling over a room that had already absorbed two others across the past weeks, and Priya felt something in her chest tighten at the thought of hunting for yet another gap with six days left instead of seven.

"We'll find it," Yusuf said, with the same flat determination he'd carried since Halloran's death four days earlier. "Tonight, if we have to. I am not letting this valley lose another person to this fight, not Farrow, not any of the other nine, not Dev. We find the leak, we close it, and we get every single one of these ten people to that eclipse alive and whole."

Outside, the frost had turned to a light, freezing rain, tapping steadily against the silo's tin roof, and somewhere beyond the ridge, in whatever place Corwin Drake had retreated to after tonight's failed reconnaissance, Priya suspected he was already planning something sharper, something that wouldn't retreat so easily the next time her headlights came around a bend.
