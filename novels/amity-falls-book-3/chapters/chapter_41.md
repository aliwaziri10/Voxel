CHAPTER FORTY-ONE

The valley clinic kept Dev overnight for observation, more out of caution than any specific concern once the initial bloodwork came back clean, and by the second morning Dr. Alvarez stood at the foot of his bed with a chart in hand and an expression that suggested she still wasn't entirely sure what she was looking at.

"Your labs are normal," she said, for perhaps the third time since he'd been admitted. "Genuinely, boringly normal. Whatever was happening to you before the eclipse, elevated cortisol, the irregular heart rhythms we caught during that first blackout workup, all of it's gone. If I hadn't seen your file from three weeks ago myself, I'd have no way of knowing anything had ever been wrong."

"Is that good or bad," Dev asked, only half joking.

"It's good," Alvarez said. "It's also the strangest good result I've had in twenty years of practicing medicine in this valley, and given some of what I've quietly signed off on this past year without asking too many questions, that's saying something." She closed the chart, studying him a moment longer. "How do you actually feel. Not the numbers. You."

Dev considered the question seriously, the way he'd learned to consider most things carefully these past six weeks. "Tired," he admitted. "Tired in a way that feels like it's going to take longer than a few nights of sleep to fix. But not sick. Not like something's sitting on my chest anymore. Just, ordinary tired. The kind you earn instead of the kind that happens to you."

"That's about as good an answer as I could hope for," Alvarez said. "I'm discharging you this afternoon, with the usual instructions to rest and not do anything strenuous for a week, instructions I fully expect you to ignore within about three days given everything I've heard about your family."

Priya arrived not long after, carrying a bag of clothes and the particular brisk energy she brought to any task she'd assigned herself, though Dev caught the way her eyes lingered on him a beat longer than necessary, checking for something the doctor's chart couldn't measure.

"Alvarez says you're being discharged," she said, setting the bag on the foot of his bed. "I brought real clothes. You've been wearing that hospital gown for two days and it's honestly a little tragic."

"Thank you," Dev said, and meant it for more than the clothes. "For all of it. The bag, the two days you've spent basically living in this room, everything."

Priya sat down in the chair beside his bed, some of her brisk energy softening into something quieter. "You don't have to thank me for that. I think I'd have slept here every night for a month if they'd let me."

"I know," Dev said. "That's exactly why I'm thanking you."

They sat in comfortable silence for a moment, the kind that had become easier between them these past six weeks than it had ever been before, forged through fear and honesty in a way that felt, to Dev, like something genuinely new between them rather than a return to anything they'd had before his sentence.

"I keep thinking about Castellan," Dev said finally. "And Delacroix. And Odette especially. What this actually cost them, long term. I got to walk out of that field and, three days later, get a clean bill of health. They're carrying pieces of what I was carrying for the rest of their lives, and I don't know how I'm supposed to feel about that."

"Grateful, I'd think," Priya said gently. "And maybe a little guilty, which is human, and probably unavoidable, but not something you should let yourself drown in. They chose this, Dev. Every single one of them, with their eyes open, knowing exactly what it would cost."

"I know. I still feel it, though. Every time I think about how light I feel now, I think about how they're not as light as they used to be, and it sits strange."

Priya reached over and took his hand, the same gesture she'd offered him countless times these past six weeks, in the supply closet and the records office and the frost-covered field. "Then let that feeling mean something," she said. "Not guilt that eats you alive. Purpose. You said it yourself, at the council session. If there's more work to do, documenting this, warning other families, you have every reason in the world to be the person leading that work, because you understand the cost in a way none of the rest of us fully can."

Dev turned that over, feeling something in his chest settle slightly at the reframing. "I'd like that," he said. "Actually doing something with all of this, instead of just surviving it."

A soft knock came at the door, and Odette stepped in slowly, leaning on her cane more heavily than she had before the eclipse, though her eyes carried the same sharp, steady clarity they always had. "I heard you were being released today," she said. "I wanted to see for myself before you disappeared back into ordinary life and I had to make an appointment to visit you like everyone else."

"Odette." Dev sat up straighter, waving her toward the empty chair on his other side. "You didn't need to make the trip. I could have come to you once I was properly discharged."

"I wanted to come," Odette said simply, settling into the chair with visible effort. "I've spent most of my life being the one who tells other people old, frightening stories and then goes home to sleep soundly in my own bed, safe from whatever those stories describe. This time I got to be part of the ending instead of just the telling of it. I wanted to see the proof that it worked, with my own eyes, before I let myself fully believe it."

"How are you," Dev asked, and the question carried real weight, real concern beneath the ordinary phrasing. "Actually. Not the version you'd tell the council."

Odette considered this honestly, the way she considered most things. "Tired, in the way that goes bone-deep at my age. A little sad, in a way I didn't expect, though I think that's simply what it feels like to have carried something and set it down, even willingly. And glad. Mostly, underneath everything else, simply glad. I've spent my whole life half-believing my grandmother's old warnings without ever having proof they meant anything real. Now I have proof, and I helped make it, and there's a satisfaction in that I don't think I fully appreciated until this exact moment, sitting here looking at you healthy and whole."

Dev felt something tighten in his throat, gratitude too large for easy words. "Thank you," he managed. "For everything. For the folklore that turned out to matter, for standing in that field, for all of it."

"You're welcome," Odette said simply. "Though I'd ask one thing in return, if you're willing."

"Anything."

"Visit me sometimes. Not because you owe me anything for the ring. Because I think, after everything we've been through together, I'd genuinely like the company." Something almost shy crossed her weathered face, unexpected from a woman who'd faced down an ancient hunger three nights earlier without visible fear. "I don't have much family left of my own, and I find I've grown rather fond of yours."

"I'd like that," Dev said, and meant it fully. "I'd like that a lot."

Priya squeezed his hand once, watching the exchange with something warm and quiet in her own expression, and Dev found himself thinking, sitting in a hospital bed with clean bloodwork and his sister's hand in his and an old woman's fondness settling comfortably into the space where fear used to live, that whatever came next, whatever work still waited to be done documenting and warning and watching for Drake's return, he was facing it from a place entirely different than the one he'd occupied six weeks ago.

Not alone. Not ashamed. Simply, finally, himself.

"When do I actually get to go home," he asked, glancing toward the door where Alvarez's discharge paperwork presumably still waited to be processed.

"This afternoon," Priya said. "I already checked. I'm driving you, and before you argue, I already told my captain I'm taking the rest of the week to make sure my idiot brother actually follows doctor's orders for once in his life."

"I'm not an idiot," Dev said, though warmth colored the protest rather than any real offense.

"You stood in a frozen field at midnight fighting off something older than this valley itself," Priya said. "I reserve the right to call you an idiot with love for at least another week."

Odette laughed at that, a real, warm sound that eased something in the room's remaining tension, and Dev found himself laughing too, the sound feeling foreign and welcome in equal measure after six weeks that had left so little room for anything so simple as genuine joy.

Outside the clinic window, the valley moved through another ordinary autumn day, and Dev, watching it from his hospital bed with his sister's hand still in his, allowed himself, for the first time in longer than he could easily remember, to simply feel glad to be alive to see it.
