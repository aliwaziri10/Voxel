CHAPTER TWENTY-EIGHT

The council convened the following morning in the same room where Elias Thorne had once argued for caution and lost, and Yusuf arrived early enough to be standing at the head of the table before anyone else came through the door, a stack of papers in front of him that Priya recognized, from the doorway, as Mara's cross-checked timeline of every meeting attendance since the orchard.

"I want to start with something I should have said out loud weeks ago," Yusuf said, once the full council had settled into their seats, Elias's chair conspicuously empty at the far end, no one having quite decided yet whether to remove it or simply leave it as a visible reminder. "When Denise's story first came to light, I held back. I told myself I was being careful, weighing the valley's stability against the truth, and some of that was even true. But underneath the caution was fear, plain fear, of what this council and this valley might do with information I couldn't fully control the shape of once it was out. I let that fear cost people time they didn't have to spare. I'm not doing that again."

A murmur ran around the table, not hostile, more the sound of people recalibrating their expectations of a man they'd watched hesitate once before.

"Full mobilization," Yusuf continued. "Every resource this council can authorize, starting today. Security around the household, doubled, not because I distrust anyone specifically but because doubling it removes the question of whether any single person's access is the weak point. Council funds released for whatever equipment Mara says the ring's mechanism requires. And full transparency, within this room at least, about the mole risk that let Elias operate as long as he did, so we don't repeat that failure with whoever comes next."

An older councilwoman near the far end, one of the few who'd sat through Denise's entire reckoning years earlier, leaned forward. "You're saying there could be another one. Another Elias."

"I'm saying we don't know there isn't," Yusuf said. "The orchard contact knew a four-minute window this council itself only decided on three days before it happened. Elias is watched, accounted for, and by every indication I have, no longer capable of passing anything along even if he wanted to. Which means either the family found another way to guess correctly, which Mara's timeline suggests is unlikely given how specific the information was, or someone else in a position to know is still talking to them."

The room went quiet at that in a different register than before, less recalibration now and more genuine unease, and Priya watched several council members glance instinctively around the table at each other, the same reflexive suspicion that had cost the valley trust once already threatening to surface again in a new shape.

"I'm not going to ask anyone in this room to defend themselves against an accusation I haven't made," Yusuf said, catching the mood and addressing it directly rather than letting it fester unspoken. "What I am going to do is narrow access. Starting today, only five people besides myself will have advance knowledge of household security schedules, ring recruitment details, or Dev's movements outside the fence: Priya, Mara, Wren, Caleb, and Odette. Everyone else in this room will be briefed after the fact, not before, until we've either found the second leak or ruled it out entirely."

"That's a significant reduction in council oversight," the older councilwoman said, not quite an objection, more a statement laid on the table for the record.

"It is," Yusuf agreed. "I'm asking this council to accept a smaller circle of knowledge in exchange for a smaller circle of risk. I recognize that's uncomfortable for people who were elected to be trusted with exactly this kind of decision. I'm asking you to trust me on this one regardless, the way I failed to trust myself quickly enough with Denise, and the way this council eventually chose to trust the smaller group during her crisis too, once it mattered enough."

The vote, when it came, wasn't unanimous, but it passed, and Priya watched something in Yusuf's shoulders ease slightly once the count was read aloud, not relief exactly, more the steadiness of a man who had finally done the hard thing instead of merely deciding to.

Elias's matter came next, and the room's mood shifted again, quieter this time, more careful. Yusuf laid out what the council already knew in outline, the confession at the grange hall, the fear rather than malice behind it, the family history of a refusal that had cost a stranger's child two counties over decades earlier.

"I'm recommending he keep his position," Yusuf said, and the room stirred at that, a few sharp intakes of breath around the table. "Watched, restricted from anything touching this current crisis, but not removed outright. I've spent this whole ordeal learning that fear, even fear that causes real harm, isn't the same thing as malice, and that how this valley treats its own frightened people when they finally tell the truth is exactly the precedent we're trying to set differently this time, with Dev, in the public gathering two nights ago. If we destroy Elias for confessing honestly once he was caught, we teach the next frightened person in his position to keep lying instead."

"He fed information to people actively trying to harm someone," a younger council member said, arms crossed. "That's not a small thing to simply forgive because he cried about it afterward."

"I'm not asking anyone to forgive him," Yusuf said. "Dev hasn't forgiven him either, and I don't expect that to change quickly if it changes at all. I'm asking this council to distinguish between forgiveness and proportionate consequence. He loses his records access permanently. He loses any position touching this crisis specifically. He does not lose his home, his standing in this valley entirely, or his ability to eventually earn back some measure of trust through years of demonstrated change, the same chance this valley eventually extended to Denise."

The vote on Elias took longer, the room genuinely split for a while, but it passed too, narrower than the mobilization vote, and Yusuf accepted the result without visible relief this time, only tired acknowledgment that some decisions cost something regardless of which way they landed.

Afterward, walking out into the cold morning with Priya beside him, Yusuf looked older than Priya had ever seen him, the accumulated weight of two difficult votes settling visibly into his frame now that the performance of leadership inside that room was no longer required of him.

"Was that the right call," he asked, not quite to her, more to the air in front of him. "Keeping Elias in some capacity. I keep running the alternative in my head, removing him entirely, and I can't decide if that would have been justice or just fear wearing justice's coat."

"I don't know," Priya said honestly. "I don't think there was a clean version of that decision available to you. I think you made the version that costs the least in the long run, even if it costs plenty right now."

"That's not much comfort," Yusuf said.

"It's not meant to be comfort," Priya said. "It's meant to be true. I think you're done needing the comfortable version of things, given everything else you've had to decide correctly in the last two weeks."

Yusuf managed something close to a tired laugh at that, brief and real. "Fair enough," he said. "Two weeks and four days now. I'd better start being right about the rest of it too."

They walked the rest of the way to the truck in silence, the cold pressing in around them, the valley behind them slowly waking into an ordinary morning that no longer felt entirely ordinary underneath, every fence post and grocery line now carrying the faint, unspoken weight of a secret half told and a clock nobody could stop counting down.

Back at the house, Mara had already turned the council's authorization into motion before Priya and Yusuf had even finished the drive back, two unfamiliar trucks parked in the yard by the time they arrived, council-funded contractors unloading fencing and floodlights under Mara's direction with the brisk, unhurried efficiency of someone who had clearly been waiting for permission rather than for inspiration.

"Perimeter lighting first," Mara said, barely glancing up from the diagram spread across the truck's tailgate as Priya and Yusuf climbed out. "Motion-triggered along the whole tree line, not just the orchard side. Whoever's watching us learned the old blind spots. I'd rather light up every inch of ground around this house than trust that they haven't found new ones."

"And the ring equipment," Yusuf asked.

"Ordered," Mara said. "Some of it's specialized enough that I had to get creative with the paperwork, but nothing that should draw attention from anyone who isn't already looking for it. I've also started drafting the actual briefing materials for candidates, the honest version Dev insisted on. It's a hard document to write. Every sentence has to be true and none of them can be reassuring, which is a strange kind of writing to do at seven in the morning with two hours of sleep behind you."

Dev found them in the yard a few minutes later, watching the contractors work with an expression Priya couldn't quite read, something between relief and fresh discomfort.

"It's strange," he said, when Priya asked. "Watching all of this happen because of me. Floodlights and fencing and council funds, all mobilized around keeping one person safe. I keep thinking about that man in the crowd last night, the one who lost a summer years ago with nobody organizing anything like this for him at all. It doesn't feel fair, being the one this valley finally decided to build a fortress around."

"It's not really about fairness," Caleb said, joining them from the porch, coffee in hand despite the early hour. "It's about what this valley learned it cost, not doing this the first time. Denise didn't get a fortress built around her because nobody understood what she was carrying until it was almost too late to help her safely. You're getting one because they finally understand faster this time. That's not unfair to the people who came before you. It's just what learning looks like, applied late but applied honestly."

Dev absorbed that for a moment, watching a contractor test one of the new floodlights, a hard white circle snapping on against the pale morning sky before switching off again. "Five people," he said, after a while. "You, Priya, Mara, Wren, Odette. That's who gets to know things before they happen now. That's a small circle to trust this much to."

"It's the same five who've carried the hardest parts of this from the beginning," Priya said. "Yusuf isn't shrinking the circle because he trusts us more than the council. He's shrinking it because every extra person who knows something in advance is one more person who could be the next Elias, whether through malice or simple exhaustion. Smaller isn't safer because it's more trustworthy. It's safer because it's smaller."

"I understand the logic," Dev said. "I still don't love being the reason a room full of elected people just voted to trust each other less than they used to."

"They didn't vote to trust each other less," Yusuf said, coming around the side of the truck in time to catch the end of it. "They voted to trust me more, for now, with a decision that's genuinely mine to carry the weight of if it goes wrong. That's a different thing entirely. I'd rather carry that weight myself than spread the risk across a room that can't move as fast or as quietly as five people who already know how to keep each other's secrets."

Wren appeared on the porch steps then, another legal pad tucked under one arm, looking marginally less exhausted than she had two days earlier, some of the earlier week's sleepless edge softened by whatever progress she'd clearly made overnight. "First candidate's already said yes," she announced, before anyone could ask. "I went to see her before the council meeting even started. Didn't want to wait on official permission when Dev already told us the honest version was the only version worth offering."

"Who," Priya asked.

"Odette," Wren said. "I told her everything, the nine to twelve number, the likely cost, the fact that nobody can promise her what shape it takes afterward. She didn't hesitate more than a few seconds. Said something about having spent her whole life keeping this valley's old stories alive, and that standing in that ring would just be one more way of doing the same work she'd always done, carrying something heavy so the people around her didn't have to carry it alone."

Dev's face did something complicated at that, gratitude and guilt moving across it almost simultaneously. "She's old," he said quietly. "Older than most of the names Yusuf's probably considering. Doesn't that make the risk worse for her, not better."

"I asked her the same thing," Wren said. "She told me that was exactly why she wanted to go first. Said the young ones on the list should get to see someone choose it clearly, with open eyes, before they're asked to choose it themselves."

Dev was quiet for a long moment, something shifting in his face that looked almost like gratitude, tangled up with the discomfort that hadn't fully left it. "Tell her thank you," he said finally. "Whatever else happens, tell her I know what it means, going first."

"Two weeks and four days," Yusuf said, glancing between the floodlights, the contractors, and the small circle of faces gathered in the cold morning yard. "One name down. I intend to spend every one of the rest of them earning back whatever this valley lost trusting me too slowly the first time around."

The floodlight snapped on again somewhere behind them, testing its range against the gray morning, and for a moment the whole yard was lit sharp and bright, every fence post and gathered face thrown into stark relief, before the light clicked off once more and the ordinary softness of morning settled back over all of them, narrower now, watched more closely, but no less determined for the added weight of it.
