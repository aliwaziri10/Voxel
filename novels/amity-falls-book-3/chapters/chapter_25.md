CHAPTER TWENTY-FIVE

Wren showed up at Mara's kitchen table before seven the next morning, hair unwashed, eyes ringed dark, three legal pads and a laptop with a cracked corner spread out in front of her like she'd been at it since the porch conversation ended and never actually gone to bed. Mara took one look at her and started the coffee without being asked.

"I found something," Wren said, before anyone had even sat down properly. "I want to say it out loud once, all the way through, before anybody interrupts me, because if I stop in the middle I might talk myself out of how bad it sounds."

"Go," Priya said, sliding into the chair across from her, Dev settling in beside her with the careful, watchful stillness that had become his default posture in every one of these meetings lately.

"Denise's cycle worked because it was small and slow," Wren said. "One memory, once every few years, taken and given somewhere else, and her body had fifty years to learn how to be a channel for that without breaking under it. What's building in Dev isn't small and it isn't slow. It's been compressing for months, and by the time we get anywhere near the eclipse it's going to want to come out all at once, in one motion, the way a dam doesn't leak, it fails. A single release point can't hold that. Denise was one pipe carrying a trickle. Dev is going to be a reservoir looking for the fastest way down, and if we try to run that through one person, one receiver, the way the well ritual worked in the last book, it kills whoever's standing at the end of it. Might kill Dev too, from the backlash of trying to force that much through one channel that fast."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Caleb had gone very still beside the stove, coffee pot forgotten in his hand.

"So we don't run it through one person," Mara said slowly, already working the problem the way she worked everything, turning it over for its structural weak points. "You're talking about distribution. Load-bearing across multiple points instead of one column trying to hold up the whole roof."

"Yes," Wren said, pointing at her like she'd landed on exactly the right shape without needing the whole explanation spelled out. "That's exactly it. Instead of one receiver taking the full weight the way the well took Denise's redistribution, we split it. A circle. Enough people standing in enough places that no single one of them takes more than they can survive carrying. Think of it like grounding a lightning strike through a dozen rods instead of one, so the charge spreads thin enough that nobody gets burned holding it."

"How many is enough," Dev asked. His voice was quiet, but there was a steadiness under it that hadn't been there in earlier weeks, the sound of someone who had stopped flinching away from the numbers and started demanding them instead.

Wren hesitated, which told Priya more than the hesitation probably meant to.

"I don't know exactly," Wren admitted. "I've never done this at this scale, nobody has, not that I've found any record of. My best working number is nine. Maybe twelve if we want a real safety margin instead of a bare minimum. Below nine I don't trust the math. Above twelve we start running into a different problem, which is that every extra person is one more person who has to be trusted, briefed, and kept safe from the quiet family finding out what's happening before the eclipse."

"Nine to twelve people," Priya repeated, and Caleb could hear the procedural gears turning behind her voice, already sorting the number into categories: who, how vetted, how fast, how quiet. "Standing where Denise once stood alone."

"Not standing exactly where she stood," Wren said. "Around her, if that makes sense. A ring instead of a point. Each person takes a piece small enough to hold, the way Denise held one memory at a time for fifty years, except instead of stretching it across decades we're stretching it across bodies in a single night."

"And what happens to them," Dev asked. "The people in the ring. After."

The kitchen went quieter still. Wren looked down at her legal pad instead of at him, which was its own kind of answer before she said anything at all.

"I don't know that either," she said. "Not for certain. But if I'm being honest with you the way Caleb's apparently decided honesty is the whole house style around here now, I think it leaves something behind. Not the memories themselves, those get redistributed the way Denise's redistribution worked in the last book, dispersed out into people who won't remember taking them, the way it's always worked. But the act of being a channel, of standing in that ring and carrying even a small piece of what Dev's been carrying, even for one night. I think that might leave a mark. The way Caleb still carries something from the well, even with the memories themselves fully returned to him."

Caleb set the coffee pot down very carefully on the counter, like something in him needed the deliberate weight of the motion to keep from reacting too fast.

"You're saying we'd be asking nine to twelve people to volunteer for something that might permanently change them," he said. "Not just risk. A guaranteed cost, even in the version where everything goes right."

"I'm saying I think that's likely, yes," Wren said. "I wanted to say it plainly instead of letting anybody find out after the fact and feel lied to. We did enough of that with Elias. I'm not doing it again, not with something this big."

Dev pushed his chair back slightly, not standing, just putting a small amount of distance between himself and the table, the way he did lately when something needed room to land in him before he could respond to it out loud. Priya watched him do it and didn't reach for him, which had become its own quiet language between them since the porch confession, trusting him to come back to the table on his own timing instead of being pulled back before he was ready.

"Then we don't ask people who don't understand exactly what they're signing up for," Dev said finally. "Not in general terms. Not 'there's some risk, are you willing to help.' The actual thing. That it might cost them something permanent, the way it's cost Caleb something permanent, and that we can't promise them what shape that cost takes or how heavy it ends up being."

"That's going to shrink our pool fast," Priya said. "People say yes to vague danger a lot more easily than they say yes to a named, specific cost. You know that as well as I do."

"Then it shrinks," Dev said, and there was an edge in his voice now that Caleb hadn't heard from him before this whole ordeal started, something that sounded less like fear and more like the beginning of authority over his own situation. "I've spent this entire process being managed. Protected. Kept in rooms while other people decided what I could handle knowing. I understand why. I even understand that some of it was probably necessary in the moment. But I am not doing that to somebody else. Not for this. If nine to twelve people are going to carry a piece of what's happening to me, every single one of them gets to look at exactly what they're carrying before they agree to it. No softer version. No easier framing. The truth, the way Wren just gave it to us, or we don't do it at all."

Mara looked at him for a long moment, something that might have been pride and might have been grief moving across her face in roughly equal measure, the two feelings close enough together lately that Caleb had stopped trying to tell them apart in her.

"That's the right call," Mara said quietly. "It's going to make Yusuf's job harder. It's still the right call."

"Speaking of Yusuf," Priya said, glancing at the clock over the stove, "he's going to want this in front of him today, not tomorrow. Four weeks isn't a lot of runway to vet, brief, and rehearse nine to twelve people for something nobody has ever actually done at this scale before."

"Rehearse," Dev repeated, something flickering across his face that Caleb recognized, a fresh piece of fear finding a new place to land now that the old fears had gotten slightly more familiar. "You can rehearse this."

"Not the real thing," Wren said. "You can't practice carrying a piece of an active bargain without actually doing it, that's not how any of this works. But you can rehearse the structure of it. Where people stand. How the timing works. What the abort signal is if something starts going wrong partway through, because I promise you, we are building an abort signal into this, I don't care how much extra time it costs us to work one out. Denise never had one. She didn't need one, because her version never moved fast enough to require stopping mid-motion. Ours might. I'd rather have a way to stop and never use it than need one and not have it."

"An abort signal," Mara said, already reaching for a fresh sheet of paper, the engineer in her visibly relieved to have a concrete problem to attach itself to instead of the larger, softer, harder-to-solve question of cost and consent still sitting heavy in the room. "Walk me through what that would even look like, mechanically."

Wren did, and for a while the conversation shifted into dense technical back-and-forth, timing windows and standing distances and what counted as an acceptable margin of error when nobody had ever run the calculation before. Caleb had learned, across two of these ordeals now, to let that part wash past him without trying to follow every strand of it, watching instead the way Dev leaned in closer to it than he had to any of the earlier planning sessions, asking sharper questions than Caleb had heard from him before, wanting not just to be told the plan but to understand it well enough to have real opinions about its weak points. It struck Caleb, watching him, that this was the first morning since the blackouts began that Dev had looked less like a patient and more like a man studying the blueprint of his own rescue, determined to know every load-bearing wall in it before he let anyone build it around him.

At one point Wren sketched a rough circle on the back of one of Mara's discarded pages, marking nine small x's around its edge and one larger mark at the center where Dev would stand. Dev studied it for a long moment, tracing the space between the marks with one finger like he was trying to feel out the distance in his own body before it became real.

"It looks small," he said. "On paper. Nine people standing in a circle."

"It'll feel bigger in the field," Wren said. "Trust me, distance changes when you're the one standing in the middle of it, and I say that as someone who's had to stand in the middle of worse circles than this one before I learned how to be careful."

"You mean the well," Dev said, not quite a question.

"I mean a few things," Wren said, and didn't elaborate, which by now everyone at the table understood meant the subject had reached the edge of what she was willing to say out loud on a given morning, and that pressing further wouldn't get anyone anywhere except somewhere Wren didn't want to go before her coffee had finished working.

It was nearly nine when Yusuf arrived, letting himself in through the back door the way he'd started doing since the council briefing, some old formality between him and this household finally worn down by months of shared crisis. He looked older than he had at the briefing, the weight of the Elias situation still visibly sitting on him even a full day later.

"Priya said you had something," he said, settling into the last open chair, accepting the coffee Mara handed him without asking if he wanted it.

Wren walked him through it again, faster this time, the version she'd clearly refined across the last two hours of talking it through with the others. Nine to twelve people. A ring, not a single point. A real, named cost to every person who stood in it, disclosed fully before anyone agreed to anything. An abort signal Mara was already sketching the mechanics of on a fresh page.

Yusuf listened without interrupting, the way he'd learned to listen since Denise, the earlier version of himself who might have reached for caution and process before people would have flinched entirely absent from the man sitting at this table now.

"Nine to twelve people," he said finally, "fully informed, in four weeks, without the quiet family finding out before we're ready. That's the job."

"That's the job," Priya confirmed.

"I know where to start," Yusuf said, and something in the plainness of it, the total absence of hesitation, told Caleb more about how far this man had traveled since his first appearance in this story than any speech could have managed. "I've spent months since the briefing thinking about who in this valley actually deserves to be trusted with something this heavy, instead of who simply holds a position that makes it look like they should be. I have a list. It's shorter than nine, but it's a real start, and every name on it is someone who's earned being asked, not just someone who happened to be sitting in the room when trust ran out for Elias."

Dev sat up a little straighter at that, some of the tension easing out of his shoulders for the first time all morning. "I want to be part of that," he said. "Not just approving names after the fact. Actually part of deciding who gets asked to carry a piece of this, since it's my life we're building the ring around."

"You'll be part of it," Yusuf said, without a beat of hesitation, and Caleb watched Priya's hand find Dev's under the table, quick and quiet, the two of them exchanging something that didn't need words to carry its whole weight.

"Four weeks," Wren said, gathering her legal pads back into an uneven stack, exhaustion finally catching up visibly with her now that the hardest part of the telling was behind her. "Four weeks to build something nobody's ever built before, with people who don't exist yet on a list that isn't finished, against a deadline that isn't moving no matter how much we'd like it to."

"Four weeks," Mara agreed, still bent over her abort-signal sketch, pencil moving steady and unhurried even now, the one constant this whole household had learned to lean on when everything else felt like it was accelerating past anyone's ability to hold it steady.

Dev looked around the table at all of them, at Priya's hand still folded over his, at Caleb watching him with the same quiet attention he'd offered on the porch the night before, at Mara's pencil and Wren's exhausted eyes and Yusuf's list of names not yet written down anywhere but already forming somewhere behind his tired, steady face.

"Then we'd better start," Dev said, and for the first time since the blackouts began, it sounded less like a man bracing for something happening to him, and more like a man finally standing up to meet it.
