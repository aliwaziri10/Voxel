CHAPTER TEN

Wren spread the almanac pages across Mara's kitchen table the next morning, six of them, brittle and yellowed at the edges, each one covered in a spidery, cramped handwriting that belonged to some long-dead valley recordkeeper who'd apparently believed paper was too precious to waste on generous margins.

"I went back through everything again last night," Wren said, "not looking for the eclipse pattern this time. I already found that. I was looking for something else. A trigger. Something that explains why now, after two generations of nothing, the blood decided to wake up in Dev specifically, at this exact moment, instead of staying quiet the way it stayed quiet in Eleanor and in your mother." She glanced at Priya, softening slightly at the mention of their mother, before pressing on. "And I think I found it, though I want to say up front that this is still partly a guess, built out of pattern-matching rather than proof."

"Tell us anyway," Priya said, sitting forward. Dev sat beside her, paler than he'd been even yesterday, dark circles carved deep beneath his eyes, but alert, focused, leaning in like a man who'd rather hear the worst than sit with more uncertainty.

Wren tapped a page near the middle of the stack, a passage describing what the recordkeeper had called, in the flat clinical language of someone documenting rather than storytelling, an incident of memory disturbance following a period of what she termed active local practice.

"Every prior awakening I could find in these records," Wren said, "and there are only four total across a hundred and fifty years, happened to someone who'd been in close physical proximity to an active taking. Not just aware of the bargain existing somewhere in the valley's history. Actually present, in the room or the space, while a release or a renewal was actively happening." She looked up, meeting Dev's eyes directly. "Dev, you stood at the well through the entire redistribution ritual. The whole thing, start to finish, close enough to watch it happen, close enough that some part of whatever moved through that space that night might have moved through you too, without anyone realizing it at the time because nothing seemed to happen to you in the moment."

The kitchen went quiet. Dev's hand found the edge of the table, gripping it as if the floor had tilted slightly beneath him.

"I was just standing there," he said slowly, working through the memory. "I wasn't part of the ritual. I didn't touch anything, didn't speak any of the words, didn't do anything except stand at the edge and watch, because Priya asked me to be there in case something went wrong and someone needed to run for help."

"That might not matter," Mara said, her engineer's mind already working the angles of it, the way she'd worked every structural failure since the day she'd first understood what Denise had been carrying. "Think about what actually happened at that well. An enormous weight, fifty years of carefully hoarded memory, being deliberately redistributed across six willing people. That's not a contained event. That's closer to an explosion, controlled, yes, aimed carefully at the six of you who volunteered, but explosions have blast radius. Things at the edge of a controlled event still feel some of the force, even if they weren't the intended target."

"You think I caught some kind of splash," Dev said, something bitter creeping into his voice. "Standing at the edge of somebody else's ritual, not even part of it, and I still ended up carrying a piece of it home with me without anyone noticing for months."

"I think it's the most consistent explanation available," Wren said gently. "And I think it explains something else too, something that's been bothering me since Denise first told us about her own experience of the taking. She said it grows with proximity and repetition. The more someone's exposed to an active practice, the more it wakes what's already dormant, if there's anything dormant there to wake in the first place. You had two years of memory, Caleb, and that was enough to wake something structural in you, even though nobody thought of it that way until much later. Dev had generations of buried Whitlock blood, dormant since Eleanor deliberately buried it in nineteen forty-seven, and one night standing at the edge of an active release was apparently enough to finally stir it."

Caleb had gone very still across the table, something working behind his eyes. "If that's true," he said slowly, "then this is partly because of what we did at the well. What we chose to do, to save Denise, to fix what happened to me. We solved one problem and it created another, one none of us could have possibly seen coming."

"You couldn't have known," Priya said quickly, fiercely, before the guilt visibly settling over Caleb's face could take root any deeper. "None of us could have known. Nobody had ever documented anything like this before. There was no way to predict that Dev standing at the edge of that ritual, purely as support, purely to help, would end up costing him something none of us understood was even at risk."

"Doesn't change what it cost him," Caleb said quietly.

"No," Dev said, surprising all of them, his voice steadier than it had been in days. "It doesn't change what it's costing me. But it also doesn't make it your fault, Caleb, or yours, Priya, or anyone's, except maybe whatever set this whole cycle moving generations before any of us were born. I was there because I chose to be there. Because I wanted to help, because you'd all done so much to help me trust this family again after everything that happened before, and I wanted to be useful for once instead of just being the person everyone was still cautiously rebuilding trust with." He looked around the table, something firming in his expression. "If this is the price of that night, then it's a price I chose to risk, even if I didn't know I was risking it. I'm not going to let anyone here carry guilt for a choice that was actually mine."

The kitchen sat with that for a long moment, the weight of his generosity almost harder to bear than blame would have been.

"That's a kind thing to say," Mara said finally. "And I appreciate it more than I can easily put into words. But kindness doesn't change the fact that we need to understand this properly if we're going to help you, and understanding it properly means accepting that the well is part of the mechanism here, not just Whitlock blood sitting quietly forever until it happened to activate on its own random schedule."

"What does that change, practically," Priya asked. "Knowing the trigger was proximity to an active release. Does it change what we do about the eclipse, about the six weeks we have left?"

"It might," Wren said, thinking it through as she spoke, the pieces visibly rearranging themselves in her head. "If proximity to an active event is what woke this in Dev, then whatever solution we build might need to account for the fact that any future controlled release, any ritual we design to help him, carries its own risk of doing to someone else what the well accidentally did to him. We can't just repeat what worked for Denise without thinking carefully about who's standing at the edges this time, who else might be close enough to catch whatever moves through that space."

Dev's face went pale at the implication. "So whatever we do to save me might end up creating this exact problem in someone else."

"Not necessarily," Mara said quickly, though her tone carried real caution rather than easy reassurance. "But it's a risk we now know to guard against, which is more than we had yesterday. We can control who's present. We can control proximity in a way nobody thought to control it the first time, because nobody knew it mattered. That's not nothing, Dev. That's an actual, actionable piece of information we didn't have this morning."

Caleb reached over and set a hand on Dev's shoulder, the same grounding gesture he'd offered on the porch steps days earlier. "We're not going to make this mistake twice," he said. "Whatever we build to help you, we build it knowing what we know now. Careful about who stands where. Careful about what proximity actually means, this time, going in with our eyes open instead of finding out afterward what we accidentally cost someone."

Dev nodded slowly, some of the color returning to his face, though the exhaustion underneath it remained, etched into the lines around his eyes in a way that made him look older than his years, older than he had any right to look this young into whatever was building toward the eclipse.

"Six weeks," he said quietly, half to himself. "Six weeks to figure out how to fix something that took generations to build, using a method we now know can accidentally break someone else the way it accidentally broke me."

"Six weeks," Wren agreed. "And every one of us in this kitchen, plus Denise, plus Yusuf and whatever resources he's already pulling together. That's more than Eleanor ever had. More than Adelaide ever had. We're not starting from nothing this time, Dev. We're starting from the most information anyone in the history of this bargain has ever had access to at once."

Outside, the morning had turned bright and cold, sunlight cutting sharp lines across Mara's kitchen floor, and Priya found herself watching her brother's face as he absorbed this newest piece of the puzzle, watching him fold it into himself the way he'd folded in every other terrible truth this past week, and thinking, not for the first time, that whatever else this bargain had inherited through their family's blood, it had also apparently inherited a stubborn, quiet resilience that neither Eleanor nor their mother had ever gotten the chance to fully use.
