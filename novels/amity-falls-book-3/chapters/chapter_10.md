CHAPTER TEN

Wren spread the almanac pages across Mara's kitchen table the next morning, six of them, brittle and yellowed at the edges, each one covered in a spidery, cramped handwriting that belonged to some long-dead valley recordkeeper who'd apparently believed paper was too precious to waste on generous margins. Outside the window, frost had settled thin and silver over the grass for the first time that autumn, an early warning of the season tightening its grip faster than usual.

"I went back through everything again last night," Wren said, smoothing one brittle page flat with careful fingers, "not looking for the eclipse pattern this time. I already found that. I was looking for something else. A trigger. Something that explains why now, after two generations of nothing, the blood decided to wake up in Dev specifically, at this exact moment, instead of staying quiet the way it stayed quiet in Eleanor and in your mother." She glanced at Priya, softening slightly at the mention of their mother, before pressing on. "And I think I found it, though I want to say up front that this is still partly a guess, built out of pattern-matching rather than proof, the kind of conclusion I'd normally want more evidence for before I said it out loud."

"Tell us anyway," Priya said, sitting forward, her coffee going cold and forgotten in front of her. Dev sat beside her, paler than he'd been even yesterday, dark circles carved deep beneath his eyes, but alert, focused, leaning in like a man who'd rather hear the worst than sit with more uncertainty stretching out ahead of him unanswered.

Wren tapped a page near the middle of the stack, a passage describing what the recordkeeper had called, in the flat clinical language of someone documenting rather than storytelling, an incident of memory disturbance following a period of what she termed active local practice, the phrase itself carrying a strange, clinical distance from whatever suffering it had actually described.

"Every prior awakening I could find in these records," Wren said, "and there are only four total across a hundred and fifty years, happened to someone who'd had direct physical contact with an active taking or release. Not just aware of the bargain existing somewhere in the valley's history, the way half this valley's grown up knowing vague old stories without any of it ever touching them directly. Actually part of the working itself, touching whatever the practice moved through, close enough that it reached them the same way it reached whoever it was meant for." She looked up, meeting Dev's eyes directly, something apologetic in her expression even though none of this was remotely her fault. "Dev, you were one of the six who put a hand on that well. You spoke the words when it came to you, same as the rest of us. You made an offering and the well answered it, same as it answered everyone else's. Nobody thought to ask what happens when the person receiving a piece of someone else's fifty years already has generations of the exact same practice sitting dormant in their own blood, waiting for something exactly like that to wake it."

The kitchen went quiet, the only sound the faint creak of the old house settling around them. Dev's hand found the edge of the table, gripping it as if the floor had tilted slightly beneath him.

"I put my hand on that well like everyone else," he said slowly, working through the memory, pulling it up from somewhere he clearly hadn't let himself sit with in a while. "I said the words when it was my turn. I offered what I offered, and I felt it land, the way everyone said theirs did. I never thought there'd be a cost beyond what I already knew I was giving. I never imagined it could wake something else entirely underneath that, something that had nothing to do with what I actually chose to offer that night."

"That might not matter, which offering was which," Mara said, her engineer's mind already working the angles of it, the way she'd worked every structural failure since the day she'd first understood what Denise had been carrying, pulling a blank sheet of paper toward herself almost without noticing she'd done it. "Think about what actually happened at that well. An enormous weight, fifty years of carefully hoarded memory, being deliberately redistributed across six willing people, each of you shaped by what you gave and what came back to you for it. That's not a clean, contained exchange, one offering in, one piece out, nothing else touched. That's contact with something ancient and enormous, and ancient, enormous things don't only interact with the part of you that's holding still and paying attention. If there was already something dormant in your blood, waiting, that same contact could easily have reached it too, simply because nobody knew there was anything there to guard."

"So the offering did exactly what it was supposed to do," Dev said, something bitter creeping into his voice for the first time all morning, "and it still cost me something nobody priced in. I put my hand on that well and gave away the worst thing I had to give, same as the rest of you, and it turns out that same hand touching that same well also cracked open a door nobody knew existed, and now I'm carrying two things instead of one, and only one of them was ever mine to choose."

"I think it's the most consistent explanation available," Wren said gently. "And I think it explains something else too, something that's been bothering me since Denise first told us about her own experience of the taking. She said it grows with proximity and repetition. The more someone's exposed to an active practice, the more it wakes what's already dormant, if there's anything dormant there to wake in the first place. You had two years of memory taken from you directly, Caleb, and that was enough to wake something structural in you eventually, even though nobody thought of it that way until much later, until the well made the shape of it undeniable. Dev had generations of buried Whitlock blood, dormant since Eleanor deliberately buried it in nineteen forty-seven, and one night with his hand on an active release was apparently enough to finally stir it, to wake something that might otherwise have stayed asleep his entire life."

Caleb had gone very still across the table, something working behind his eyes, his jaw tightening in a way Mara recognized from the worst weeks after his own memory first started coming back. "If that's true," he said slowly, each word deliberate, "then this is partly because of what we did at the well. What we chose to do, to save Denise, to fix what happened to me. We solved one problem and it created another, one none of us could have possibly seen coming, one that's now threatening to take Dev's life the way it never quite took mine."

"You couldn't have known," Priya said quickly, fiercely, before the guilt visibly settling over Caleb's face could take root any deeper. "None of us could have known. Nobody had ever documented anything like this before. There was no way to predict that Dev putting his hand on that well, offering exactly what the rest of you offered, would end up costing him something none of us understood was even at risk. You were trying to save your husband's memory and free a dying woman from fifty years of guilt. That's not a mistake, Caleb. That's the only choice any of you had, given what you knew at the time."

"Doesn't change what it cost him," Caleb said quietly, his voice rough.

"No," Dev said, surprising all of them, his voice steadier than it had been in days. "It doesn't change what it's costing me. But it also doesn't make it your fault, Caleb, or yours, Priya, or anyone's, except maybe whatever set this whole cycle moving generations before any of us were born, some choice made by Ambrose or whoever came before him, long before this family even existed in its current shape." He looked around the table, something firming in his expression, a steadiness that hadn't been there a week ago. "I chose to be one of the six. I chose to put my hand on that well and give away the thing I was most ashamed of, because I wanted to help, because you'd all done so much to help me trust this family again after everything that happened before, and I wanted to be useful for once instead of just being the person everyone was still cautiously rebuilding trust with, still watching carefully to see if I'd changed. If this is the price of that choice, then it's a price I chose to risk, even if I didn't know I was risking it. I'm not going to let anyone here carry guilt for a choice that was actually mine to make."

The kitchen sat with that for a long moment, the weight of his generosity almost harder to bear than blame would have been, the frost outside the window catching the morning light in a way that made the whole scene feel suspended, held briefly outside of time.

"That's a kind thing to say," Mara said finally, something thick in her own voice now. "And I appreciate it more than I can easily put into words. But kindness doesn't change the fact that we need to understand this properly if we're going to help you, and understanding it properly means accepting that the well is part of the mechanism here, not just Whitlock blood sitting quietly forever until it happened to activate on its own random schedule."

"What does that change, practically," Priya asked, pulling herself back toward the useful, procedural questions that had always been her way of managing fear. "Knowing the trigger was contact with an active release. Does it change what we do about the eclipse, about the six weeks we have left?"

"It might," Wren said, thinking it through as she spoke, the pieces visibly rearranging themselves in her head, her fingers drumming an absent rhythm against the table's edge. "If direct contact with an active event is what woke this in Dev, then whatever solution we build might need to account for the fact that any future controlled release, any ritual we design to help him, carries its own risk of doing to someone else what the well accidentally did to him. We can't just repeat what worked for Denise without thinking carefully about who's actually touching the working this time, who else might be carrying something dormant that none of us have any way of knowing about, who else might walk away from whatever we do carrying something they never asked for."

Dev's face went pale at the implication, the color draining from it visibly. "So whatever we do to save me might end up creating this exact problem in someone else."

"Not necessarily," Mara said quickly, though her tone carried real caution rather than easy reassurance, the honesty of someone unwilling to promise more than she could actually deliver. "But it's a risk we now know to guard against, which is more than we had yesterday. We can control who takes part. We can ask, this time, whether anyone carries something like this in their own family before we ever let them near it, because nobody knew it mattered the first time. That's not nothing, Dev. That's an actual, actionable piece of information we didn't have this morning, and information is the one thing that's actually been on our side since this started."

Caleb reached over and set a hand on Dev's shoulder, the same grounding gesture he'd offered on the porch steps days earlier. "We're not going to make this mistake twice," he said. "Whatever we build to help you, we build it knowing what we know now. Careful about who takes part. Careful about what contact with this thing actually means, this time, going in with our eyes open instead of finding out afterward what we accidentally cost someone else."

Dev nodded slowly, some of the color returning to his face, though the exhaustion underneath it remained, etched into the lines around his eyes in a way that made him look older than his years, older than he had any right to look this young into whatever was building toward the eclipse.

"Six weeks," he said quietly, half to himself, testing the shape of it. "Six weeks to figure out how to fix something that took generations to build, using a method we now know can accidentally break someone else the way it accidentally broke me."

"Six weeks," Wren agreed. "And every one of us in this kitchen, plus Denise, plus Yusuf and whatever resources he's already pulling together, plus a whole valley that's already shown it can rally faster than anyone expected when it actually matters. That's more than Eleanor ever had. More than Adelaide ever had. We're not starting from nothing this time, Dev. We're starting from the most information anyone in the history of this bargain has ever had access to at once, and I intend to make every single day of that count."

Outside, the morning had turned bright and cold, sunlight cutting sharp lines across Mara's kitchen floor, the frost already beginning to burn away at the edges of the window glass, and Priya found herself watching her brother's face as he absorbed this newest piece of the puzzle, watching him fold it into himself the way he'd folded in every other terrible truth this past week, and thinking, not for the first time, that whatever else this bargain had inherited through their family's blood, it had also apparently inherited a stubborn, quiet resilience that neither Eleanor nor their mother had ever gotten the chance to fully use, a resilience she was only now beginning to understand had been waiting in him all along, long before any of this started.
