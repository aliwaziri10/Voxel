CHAPTER THIRTY-SEVEN

The fourth shot came eleven minutes into totality, and this time it wasn't aimed at the tree line's own shadows for effect. It found the ground three feet from Wren's position at the ring's edge, kicking up a spray of frozen earth that made her flinch back hard enough to nearly lose her footing.

"They're walking it in," she called out, forcing her voice steady even as her pulse hammered. "That wasn't cover fire. That was aimed."

Yusuf's voice cracked across the radio, tight with fury. "Drake. Stand down."

"Hollis is still breathing," Yusuf added, quieter, clearly not meant for Drake at all, a private update thrown across the same channel for whoever on the ring needed to hear it most. "Medic's got him stable for now. Focus on your own positions."

"That was not my order." Drake's voice carried from the tree line, unhurried even now, even with a wounded deputy bleeding into the frost thirty yards behind him. "Some of mine believe more strongly than I do that force still has its uses tonight. I am restraining them. My restraint has limits, the same as yours."

Mara's eyes never left the ring itself, tracking the ten candidates with the same fierce concentration she'd have given a failing beam. "Distribution's still holding," she said, mostly to herself, mostly to keep her own hands from shaking. "Eleven minutes in. Four to go. Whatever they're doing out there, it hasn't touched the mechanism yet."

"Yet," Caleb said grimly, not taking his eyes off Dev.

At the center stake, Dev's face had gone the color of old paper, sweat freezing on his skin in the cold night air, his whole body trembling with an effort that had stopped looking sustainable several minutes ago. His eyes were open now, fixed on some middle distance none of the rest of them could see, and when he spoke, his voice came out cracked and strange, layered with something that wasn't quite his own cadence.

"It's not evenly weighted," he said. "Something's pulling wrong. West side. Whoever's on the west side, something's dragging against them."

Priya's head snapped toward the western point, where Delacroix, a retired nurse who'd volunteered in the second wave of candidates, had gone rigid, her whole body straining against the stake as though fighting a current the rest of the ring couldn't feel.

"Delacroix," Wren called, urgent. "Talk to me."

"It's catching." Delacroix's voice came out strangled, barely audible over the wind picking up across the open field. "Like there's a snag in the line and everything's backing up behind it."

At the southwest point, Castellan's breath went ragged, sympathetic strain visible even from across the circle, as though the wrongness in Delacroix's channel had started leaking sideways into the ring itself. "I feel it too," he called out, teeth gritted. "Not as bad. But I feel it."

"Everyone hold your own line," Odette snapped, her voice cutting through the rising panic with the flat authority of someone twice everyone else's age. "Whatever's happening to her, it isn't yours to carry. Let Mara work."

Mara was already moving, circling toward the western point without crossing into the ring itself. "Wren. Is this the failure mode we built the abort for?"

"Not exactly." Wren's face had gone pale, her eyes darting between the ring and a calculation she'd run a hundred times that suddenly felt useless. "We planned for total collapse. This is holding, but wrong. Water finding a partial blockage instead of a clean break."

"Can it correct itself," Caleb asked.

"I don't know," Wren admitted, and the honesty in it landed harder than any reassurance could have. "Denise's version never had this problem. One channel, one taking. We've never tested ten under live conditions, only rehearsal, and rehearsal never had gunfire."

Delacroix cried out again, her knees buckling slightly before she caught herself, and Priya felt her own chest tighten with a fear that had nothing to do with the grief fragments still moving steadily through her own channel.

"Sound the abort," Priya said. "If it's failing, we pull back before it fails worse."

"No." Dev's voice cut across the field, strained but absolutely clear. "Wren, is the whole ring failing, or just her channel?"

Wren's eyes swept the circle again. "Just hers. Everyone else is holding steady. It's isolated."

"Then we don't abort ten channels for one snag." Dev's jaw was clenched so tight Priya could see the muscle working even from across the circle. "Delacroix. Can you hold. Just until we figure out what's catching."

Delacroix's face was white with pain, but she nodded, sharp and determined. "I can hold."

Mara reached the western point, close enough now to study the ground around Delacroix's stake without touching either the woman or the wood, her engineer's eyes scanning for anything physical that might explain a snag in something that had no physical mechanism at all. Nothing. Frost, churned earth, the same pale ash wood as every other stake in the ring.

"There's nothing here," she said, more to herself than anyone. "There has to be something here."

She thought of every hour she'd spent designing this ring, every contingency she'd tried to plan for, and hated, in this moment, every single one she hadn't thought of. Six weeks of calculation had not accounted for someone else's hands in the dirt before hers ever arrived.

She dug her fingers into the frozen earth at the base of the stake anyway, half-blind, working by feel in the dark, and for three long seconds found nothing but cold dirt and her own rising panic.

Then her hand closed on iron.

A second stake, smaller, driven into the ground at an angle, dark metal instead of the pale ash wood they'd used for the ring itself, positioned close enough to Delacroix's own stake that in the darkness, in the chaos of setup, no one had noticed it wasn't part of the original design.

"Someone's been here," Mara said, her voice going cold with a different kind of fear. "This isn't ours. This was planted."

"Drake's people," Priya said, already understanding, already furious. "Before we ever arrived tonight. They didn't need to disrupt the ring from outside. They only needed one bad point built into it from the start."

She thought of the eastern ridge property Mara had flagged weeks ago as a lead worth watching, of every hour of surveillance that had somehow still missed this, and filed the failure away for later, alongside every other thing tonight that would need a harder look once the sun came up.

"Can you pull it," Caleb called.

Mara wrapped her fingers around the iron stake and eased it, testing the give, and Delacroix screamed, a raw, involuntary sound that tore across the field before she clamped it off with a gasp.

"Stop," Delacroix managed. "Stop, that's worse, whatever you're doing, that's worse."

Behind Mara, Priya heard Wren's sharp intake of breath, the sound of someone watching a plan she'd helped build come apart in real time, six weeks of careful preparation suddenly worth exactly as much as the next thirty seconds of one woman's improvisation.

Mara froze, hand still on the iron, her whole body gone rigid with the sudden, sickening possibility that she'd just made it worse instead of better. "It's fighting the removal," she said. "It's rooted into whatever's already moving through her."

Priya's stomach dropped. She had promised nobody would be hurt for this, had said it out loud to every one of the ten volunteers during the training weeks, and for one endless second it looked like that promise was about to break in front of her.

Caleb's hand found her shoulder, brief and hard, grounding her before she could do anything reckless. "Mara's got her," he said, low, more command than comfort. "Watch Dev. That's your job right now. Let her do hers."

Priya forced her eyes back to her brother, still upright, still rigid at the center stake, and made herself breathe through the fear clawing at her chest instead of acting on it.

"Thirty seconds," Dev's voice came, strained nearly to breaking now. "Mara, we don't have time for certainty tonight. We only have time for the best guess of the person who's spent six weeks understanding this better than anyone else alive."

Mara closed her hand around the iron stake, cold enough to burn against her palm even through her glove, and this time, instead of easing it, she pulled it clean and hard in one motion, betting everything on speed over caution.

It came free with a sound like tearing cloth, and Delacroix screamed again, sharper, shorter, before her body sagged against her own stake and then straightened, color flooding back into her face in a rush that left her gasping.

"It's clear," she managed. "Oh god, it's clear, I can feel it moving properly now."

Mara let out a breath she hadn't realized she'd been holding since her hand first closed on the iron, her knees briefly unsteady beneath her before she forced herself upright again. There would be time to shake later. Not now.

"Distribution restored," Wren called, relief cracking her voice for the first time all night. "All ten channels even. Mara, whatever that was, you just fixed it."

A ragged sound of relief moved around the ring, nine other voices too spent to cheer but unwilling to stay entirely silent either, Farrow's low exhale, Odette's murmured word of thanks in whatever old language she'd been praying in all night, Castellan's short, disbelieving laugh cut off almost as soon as it started. None of them let go of their stakes. All of them, for one brief moment, let their shoulders drop half an inch.

Mara stood, the iron stake still clutched in her hand, cold enough that she could feel it even through the adrenaline, and looked toward the tree line where Drake stood watching, his expression unreadable in the darkness.

"You planted this," she called out. "Before we ever arrived."

"A contingency." For the first time something like genuine respect colored Drake's voice. "One I confess I did not expect you to solve this quickly. I built it to fail slowly, in a way that would look like your own design's weakness rather than mine, and still you found it inside four minutes."

"There were others," Priya said, cold understanding settling into her chest. "Before us."

"There have always been others," Drake said, and something almost like grief moved beneath the words now. "I have watched containment fail more times than I care to count. I had hoped tonight might prove me right one more time."

He said nothing further after that, and the silence from the tree line felt, for the first time all night, less like a threat than like a man quietly recalculating everything he thought he understood.

"Two minutes," Wren called, her eyes fixed on her watch. "Hold. Everyone hold."

Dev's eyes found Priya's across the circle, some silent communication passing between them that needed no words, six weeks and a lifetime of trust finally converging on this single, narrowing window of time.

"Hold," he said again, quieter now, almost gentle despite everything still moving through him. "We're almost there."

Somewhere out past the tree line, one of Drake's people shifted position, a small sound in the frozen brush, and Yusuf's voice cracked across the radio one more time, low and lethal. "Nobody crosses that line. Not one step. I will not say it again tonight."

Above them, the eclipse's shadow began, almost imperceptibly, to shift, the first faint edge of returning light not yet visible but felt somehow, and ten hands held steady against ten stakes, refusing, after everything, to let go before the work was finally done.

At the western point, Delacroix breathed through the last of the ache Mara's fix had left behind, testing her own grip once, twice, and found it steady. Whatever Drake had planted in the ground beneath her had failed. That, at least, was hers now, not his.
