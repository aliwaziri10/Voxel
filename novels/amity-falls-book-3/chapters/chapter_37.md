CHAPTER THIRTY-SEVEN

The fourth shot came eleven minutes into totality, and this time it wasn't aimed at the tree line's own shadows for effect. It found the ground three feet from Wren's position at the ring's edge, kicking up a spray of frozen earth that made her flinch back hard enough to nearly lose her footing.

"They're walking it in," she called out, forcing her voice steady even as her pulse hammered. "That wasn't cover fire. That was a warning shot at a specific target."

Yusuf's voice cracked across the radio, tight with fury. "Drake. I see you moving. Stand down or my people return fire, and I don't care what your doctrine says about patience after that."

"My apologies, Mr. Chair." Drake's voice carried from the tree line, unhurried even now, even with a wounded deputy bleeding into the frost thirty yards behind him. "That was not my order. I have people here who believe rather more strongly than I do that force still has its uses tonight. I am attempting to restrain them. I would ask you to understand that my restraint has limits, the same as yours."

Mara's eyes never left the ring itself, tracking the ten candidates with the same fierce concentration she'd have given a failing beam. "Distribution's still holding," she said, mostly to herself, mostly to keep her own hands from shaking. "Eleven minutes in. Four to go. Whatever they're doing out there, it hasn't touched the actual mechanism yet."

"Yet," Caleb said grimly, not taking his eyes off Dev.

At the center stake, Dev's face had gone the color of old paper, sweat freezing on his skin in the cold night air, his whole body trembling with an effort that had stopped looking sustainable several minutes ago. His eyes were open now, fixed on some middle distance none of the rest of them could see, and when he spoke, his voice came out cracked and strange, layered with something that wasn't quite his own cadence.

"It's not evenly weighted," he said. "Something's pulling wrong. West side. Whoever's on the west side, something's dragging against them."

Priya's head snapped toward the western point, where a woman named Delacroix, a retired nurse who'd volunteered in the second wave of candidates, had gone rigid, her whole body straining against the stake as though fighting a current the rest of the ring couldn't feel.

"Delacroix," Wren called, urgent. "Talk to me. What's happening."

"I don't know." Delacroix's voice came out strangled, barely audible over the wind picking up across the open field. "It feels like it's catching on something. Like there's a snag in the line and everything's backing up behind it."

Mara was already moving, circling toward the western point without crossing into the ring itself, her mind working the problem the way it always did, structural, immediate, refusing panic room to take hold. "Wren, the abort signal, is this the failure mode we built it for."

"Not exactly." Wren's face had gone pale, her eyes darting between her notebook and the ring, calculations she'd run a hundred times now suddenly feeling inadequate against the reality unfolding in front of her. "We planned for total collapse, for someone losing contact entirely. This is different. This is holding, but wrong, like water finding a partial blockage instead of a clean break."

"Can it correct itself," Caleb asked.

"I don't know," Wren admitted, and the honesty in it landed harder than any reassurance could have. "Denise's version never had this problem because it was one channel, one taking. We've never actually tested ten channels under live conditions, only in rehearsal, and rehearsal never had gunfire and a wounded deputy and Drake standing at the tree line applying whatever pressure he might actually be capable of applying beyond bullets."

Delacroix cried out again, her knees buckling slightly before she caught herself, and Priya felt her own chest tighten with a fear that had nothing to do with the grief fragments still moving steadily through her own channel.

"Sound the abort," Priya said. "If it's failing, we pull back before it fails worse."

"No." Dev's voice cut across the field, strained but absolutely clear, the same hardened authority Priya had heard from him minutes before. "Not yet. Wren, is the whole ring failing, or just her channel?"

Wren's eyes swept the circle again, cataloging each candidate with rapid, practiced assessment. "Just hers. Everyone else is holding steady, distribution intact. It's isolated."

"Then we don't abort the whole ring for one snag." Dev's jaw was clenched so tight Priya could see the muscle working even from across the circle. "Delacroix. Can you hold. Just a little longer, just until we figure out what's catching."

Delacroix's face was white with pain, but she nodded, sharp and determined, the same resolve every one of these ten strangers-turned-family had carried since the day they'd volunteered. "I can hold."

Mara reached the western point, close enough now to study the ground around Delacroix's stake without touching either the woman or the wood, her engineer's eyes scanning for anything physical that might explain a snag in something that had no physical mechanism at all. Then she saw it, half-buried in the frost-churned earth at the base of the stake.

A second stake, smaller, driven into the ground at an angle, dark iron instead of the pale ash wood they'd used for the ring itself, positioned close enough to Delacroix's own stake that in the darkness, in the chaos of setup, no one had noticed it wasn't part of the original design.

"Someone's been here," Mara said, her voice going cold with a different kind of fear. "This isn't ours. This was planted."

"Drake's people," Priya said, already understanding, already furious. "Before we ever arrived tonight. They didn't need to disrupt the ring from outside. They only needed one bad point built into it from the start."

"Can you pull it," Caleb called.

"I don't know what happens if I do," Mara said, crouching low, studying the iron stake's angle, the way it seemed to lean deliberately toward Delacroix's own position, as though designed specifically to interfere with whatever passed through that exact channel. "It might release whatever's snagging. It might make it worse. I have thirty seconds to decide and no data to decide with."

"Then decide with what you have," Dev's voice came, strained nearly to breaking now. "Mara. We don't have time for certainty tonight. We only have time for the best guess of the person who's spent six weeks understanding this better than anyone else alive."

Mara closed her hand around the iron stake, cold enough to burn against her palm even through her glove, and pulled.

It came free with a sound like tearing cloth, and Delacroix screamed, a short, sharp cry that cut off almost as quickly as it started, her body sagging against her own stake before straightening again, color flooding back into her face in a rush that left her gasping.

"It's clear," she managed. "Oh god, it's clear, I can feel it moving properly now."

"Distribution restored," Wren called, relief cracking her voice for the first time all night. "All ten channels even again. Mara, whatever that was, you just fixed it."

Mara stood, the iron stake still clutched in her hand, cold enough that she could feel it even through the adrenaline, and looked toward the tree line where Drake stood watching, his expression unreadable in the darkness.

"You planted this," she called out, not caring anymore whether it was wise to engage him directly. "Before we ever arrived. You built a flaw into our own ring."

"A contingency," Drake called back, and for the first time something like genuine respect colored his voice. "One I confess I did not expect you to solve quite this quickly. You are, all of you, considerably more capable than the last group I watched attempt something like this, decades ago, in a different valley, with a different family carrying a similar burden."

"There were others," Priya said, cold understanding settling into her chest. "Before us. Other Dev's. Other families."

"There have always been others," Drake said, and something almost like grief moved beneath the words now, unexpected and unwelcome in its sincerity. "That is precisely why I do what I do, Deputy. I have watched containment fail, in one form or another, more times than I care to count. I had hoped, perhaps, that tonight's contingency might prove me right one more time. I find, watching your engineer's hands, that I am less certain of that than I was an hour ago."

"Two minutes," Wren called, her eyes fixed on her watch, on the almanac page worn soft with weeks of handling. "Two minutes left in totality. Hold. Everyone hold."

Dev's eyes found Priya's across the circle, some silent communication passing between them that needed no words, six weeks and a lifetime of trust finally converging on this single, narrowing window of time.

"Hold," he said again, quieter now, almost gentle despite everything still moving through him. "We're almost there. All of us, together, we're almost there."

Above them, the eclipse's shadow began, almost imperceptibly, to shift, the first faint edge of returning light not yet visible but felt somehow, the way the whole valley seemed to feel the turning of every season before it fully arrived, and ten hands held steady against ten stakes, refusing, after everything, to let go before the work was finally, fully done.
