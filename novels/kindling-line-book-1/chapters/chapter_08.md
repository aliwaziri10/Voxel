The patrol had been routine. Three guards from House Vane, two from House Ashworth, moving along the lower ledge where the shale gave way to something harder and older. They carried lanterns instead of Kindling. The Reckoning Accord forbade open flame near the ward stores, and the council's new edict forbade Kindling use without logged justification. Sol had argued for the lanterns. She had won that argument, and the victory tasted like ash.

She heard the crack before she felt the tremor. A dry sound, like bone snapping in a closed fist. Then the ledge shuddered. Dust puffed from the fissures in the cliff face. The lantern light swung wild.

"Hold," Kael said. His voice cut through the settling grit. He stood at the patrol's center, his auditor's badge catching the dim glow. The guards froze. Years of training made them statues.

Sol did not freeze. She moved toward the sound.

The rockfall had taken a thirty-foot section of the path. Two guards were on the far side, waving lanterns through the dust. Three were on her side. One of them, a young Ashworth recruit named Pell, had been caught by the edge. His leg pinned beneath a slab the size of a dining table. He was not screaming. His jaw was locked. His hands clawed at the stone.

Kael was already there. He braced his shoulder against the rock. "Together. On three."

Sol grabbed the opposite edge. The stone was cold. It bit her palms. She smelled iron and old water.

"One. Two. Three."

The slab shifted. A grinding scream from the cliff's throat. Pell gasped. His leg came free, mangled below the knee. Kael hauled him back. Sol followed, boots sliding on gravel.

Then the second crack came.

It started above them. A spiderweb of fractures racing across the overhang. Sol looked up. She saw the mass of it, a slab twice the size of the first, detaching in slow horror. The patrol had seconds. The path behind them was gone. The ledge ahead ended in a sheer drop to the next tier, two hundred feet down.

"Kindling," she said. The word left no room for argument.

Kael's head snapped toward her. His face was gray in the lantern light. "The edict —"

"Is void when the alternative is death." She stepped into the open space between the patrol and the falling rock. Her boots found purchase on the narrow rim. The wind coming up the cliff face tasted of snow and sulfur.

She inhaled. The Kindling answered. It always answered. A coil of heat unfurled behind her sternum, familiar and terrible. She had trained for this. Breath control. Muscle memory. The visualization her mother taught her: a furnace with a regulated damper. She could not stop the burn. She could only narrow the channel.

Her wings manifested. Not feathers. Not membrane. Ribbons of concentrated force, invisible to the eye but palpable as pressure against the skin. They caught the updraft. She rose.

The falling slab met her palms. The impact drove her down six inches. Her knees buckled. The Kindling roared through her veins, consuming the years she had banked, the years she had stolen from sleep and hunger and the quiet moments between heartbeats. She pushed.

The slab arrested. Hovered. Groaned.

She shoved it sideways. It crashed into the cliff face, sending a new shower of debris. The patrol scrambled. Kael hauled Pell. The other guards pulled each other. Sol held the sky.

The burn intensified. Her vision tunneled. She felt the transfer happen — that sickening lurch as the cost sought the nearest vessel. She had hoped, foolishly, that distance might matter. That the patrols' spread might dilute it. She had not accounted for Kael refusing to leave Pell's side.

The first mark had been a hairline fracture across his left pectoral, faint as a scratch from a thorn. This one drove deeper. She felt it through the stone beneath her boots, a resonant vibration in the cliff's bones. Kael did not cry out. She saw his back arch. Saw his fingers gouge the rock as he kept hold of Pell. Saw the lantern fall from his other hand, shattering.

She lowered the slab. Her wings dissolved. The updraft dropped her onto trembling legs.

The patrol was moving. Kael was moving. He had Pell slung over his shoulder, the recruit's broken leg bound with strips torn from a uniform shirt. The other guards formed a cordon. They moved toward the stairwell cut into the cliff, the only way up from this tier.

Sol followed. Her chest burned hollow. The Kindling always took its due. She had lost three months this time. Maybe four. She would not know until she checked her ledger. The ledger she kept hidden beneath a loose floorboard in her room, written in code her mother devised.

Kael set a punishing pace. He did not look at her. He did not speak. The badge on his chest caught the lanterns of the guards ahead, flash flash flash, a rhythm like a heartbeat.

They reached the stairwell. The climb was four hundred steps carved into the living rock. Sol counted them. She always counted. Numbers gave the pain a shape she could hold.

At the second landing, Kael stopped. He set Pell down gently. The recruit's face was wet with sweat and something darker. Kael unclasped his auditor's coat. Let it fall. Pulled his shirt over his head in one fluid motion.

The lantern light revealed the mark.

It cut diagonally from his right shoulder to his left hip. A line of raw, angry red, raised and weeping. Beside it, the old mark — the one from the warehouse district three weeks past — had faded to a silvery thread. The new one crossed it at a sharp angle. An X carved by forces neither of them controlled.

Two marks. Undeniable. Documentable. The kind of evidence an auditor presented to the council to prove Kindling misuse. The kind of evidence that triggered seizure of assets, revocation of lineage rights, the full weight of the Accord.

Sol stood three steps above him. The wind tore at her hair. She could not breathe.

Kael turned. His face was hollowed by pain, but his eyes were clear. They found hers in the dimness. He said nothing. He did not need to. The marks spoke for him. They said: I have been burned twice in your presence. They said: The transfer is real. They said: House Vane's heir is a liability the council cannot ignore.

He pulled his shirt down. The fabric stuck to the fresh wound. He did not flinch. He buttoned his coat. The badge settled over his heart.

"Move," he said to the guards. His voice was rough. "The recruit needs a surgeon."

They moved. Sol moved with them. Her body knew the rhythm. Her mind was elsewhere.

She felt it still. The phantom heat across her own chest, mirroring his. Not imagination. Not sympathy. Something older. The stone beneath her boots had carried the vibration of his pain, conducted it through the cliff's deep roots into her soles, up her legs, into the hollow where the Kindling lived. She had felt the moment the cost struck him. Felt the years tear loose from his future. Felt the silent scream he swallowed.

A bond. That was the word her mother would have used. A resonance. The Kindling recognized its own echo in another body. The cost did not care about house allegiance or auditor's badges or the Accord's careful categories. It sought the nearest life. It found Kael. It would keep finding Kael.

Unless she stopped using the Kindling. Unless she let the next rockfall crush whoever stood beneath it.

The stairwell opened onto the third tier. The surgeon's station was a cave widened by generations of healers, smelling of herb smoke and blood. Kael deposited Pell into waiting hands. He gave orders. The guards dispersed. The lanterns guttered.

Sol remained in the doorway. The cold air bit her sweat-damp skin. She watched Kael wash his hands in a basin of cold water. The red water swirled. He dried them on a cloth. He did not look at his chest. He did not look at her.

"The report," he said. "You will write it. I will countersign. The Kindling use was authorized by auditor discretion. Patrol safety. Standard protocol."

His tone was flat. Procedural. The armor he wore over the wound.

"The patrol was not authorized for Kindling support," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears. Thin. "The edict —"

"Is superseded by the auditor's judgment in life-threatening circumstances. You know this. You wrote the brief for House Vane's compliance office last month."

She had. She had also known he would read it. He read everything.

"Three months," she said. "Maybe four. That's what it took from me."

He turned then. His face was pale. The pain lines around his mouth deepened. "And from me?"

She could not answer. The truth sat in her throat, hot and sharp. The first mark had taken two weeks from him. She knew because she had watched him afterward, cataloguing the changes. The way he tired faster. The way his hands shook when he thought no one looked. The way he checked his own ledger with increasing frequency.

This mark would take more. She did not know how much. The transfer was not proportional. It was chaotic. Greedy. The Kindling took what it wanted from whoever stood closest.

"I will not report the second mark," he said. "Not yet."

The words hung in the herb-smoke air. A breach of his oath. A breach of the Accord. The kind of breach that ended careers. Ended houses.

"Why?"

His gaze held hers. In the dim light, his eyes were the color of storm water. "Because the council would ask how an auditor acquired two Kindling burns in three weeks without filing a single misuse report. Because the answer would lead them to House Vane. Because House Corrin is already circling your claim, and they would use this to accelerate the seizure."

House Corrin. The name tasted like copper. They controlled the ward trade. They held the contracts for every consenting cost-absorber in the Reach. They had bankrupted House Vane by buying the debt on Sol's mother's failed Reckoning, then calling it in at the worst possible moment. Now they wanted the Vane ledge. The mineral rights. The Kindling lineage itself.

"And when they find out anyway?" she asked. "When the surgeon sees? When the guards talk?"

"The surgeon is Vane-contracted. The guards are bound by patrol confidentiality. The report I countersign will state the Kindling use was mine." He said it simply. A lie offered like a gift. "I was demonstrating emergency protocol. You were observing. The cost transferred because I stood too close."

"That is not what happened."

"It is what the record will show." He buttoned his coat to the throat. The badge disappeared. "You will not contradict it. You will not speak of the transfer to anyone. Not your house. Not your trainers. Not the ward brokers. Is that clear?"

The command rung in his voice. Auditor to subject. The power dynamic the Accord had written into their bones.

She should refuse. She should tell him the truth was the only weapon they had against Corrin. She should walk back down those four hundred steps and carve the reality into the council's door herself.

Instead she heard herself say, "Clear."

The word cost her nothing. The truth cost everything.

Kael nodded once. He moved past her, up the corridor toward the Ashworth aerie. His gait was even. He did not limp. He did not favor his left side. The pain was a thing he carried inside the armor, invisible to the world.

Sol stood alone in the surgeon's cave. The herb smoke curled. The basin water grew cold. She pressed her hand against her own chest, over the hollow place. She felt the phantom heat pulse in time with her heartbeat. Two lines crossed on a body that was not hers. An X marking a spot on a map she had not drawn.

The Kindling burned in her veins, quieter now. Waiting. Patient. It had years left to take. It would take them from her, or from whoever stood nearest when she called it forth.

She thought of the warehouse district. The first time. She had been cornered by Corrin's collectors. Kael had intervened. He had stood between her and the contract they waved, the one that would have signed her life to the ward trade. She had flared the Kindling to break the door. He had caught the cost.

She had not asked him to. She had not wanted him to. The Kindling did not ask. It did not want. It simply took.

Now he carried two marks. The council would see pattern. The council would see intent. The council would see an auditor compromised by a fallen house's heir.

And she would carry the silence. She would write the false report. She would watch him check his ledger with increasing frequency. She would feel the phantom heat across her own chest every time the wind blew cold through the Reach.

The bond her mother named. The resonance. It was not love. It was not trust. It was the terrible knowledge that her survival cost him his future, and she would keep surviving anyway.

She turned toward the Vane aerie. The climb was longer from here. The ledge narrower. The wind sharper.

Behind her, the surgeon's cave swallowed Kael's bloody water. Ahead, the cliff face waited. Somewhere in the dark, House Corrin counted their contracts and sharpened their knives.

Sol began to climb.
