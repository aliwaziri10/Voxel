The ledger's leather cover had cracked along the spine years ago. Isolde Vane ran her thumb over the split, feeling the grit of cliff-dust caught in the fibers. Dust from the Reach settled on everything. It coated the brass candlesticks on her desk. It layered the stacked receipts for rope and anchor-iron and the quarterly tithe to the Ascendancy. It would outlast the ink she used to track it all.

She opened the book to the current fiscal quarter. The columns blurred for a moment, numbers swimming in the dim light of a single oil lamp. Her eyes were tired. She had been up since before dawn, climbing the interior stairwell to the aerie to check the wind-vanes, then descending to the cellar stores to count salted fish against the manifest. The ledger was the last thing. It was always the last thing.

Outside the narrow window, a bell tolled. Once. Twice. Three times. The city criers' signal for proclamation.

Isolde set down her pen. She knew that rhythm. She had heard it every year of her life, echoing off the limestone faces of the lineage-houses, carried on the updrafts that rose from the valley floor. The criers stood on the Gallery Bridge, the only span wide enough to hold a crowd, and their voices climbed the rock face like the wind itself.

"Hear all!" The words drifted through the cracked window frame, muffled by distance and stone. "By the authority of the Ascendancy and the Reckoning Accord, the Thirty-Seventh Reckoning of the Lineage Houses is hereby declared! The audit shall commence on the twenty-ninth day of Frostveil, Year Three of the Reckoning Accord. All houses shall present their ledgers, their wards, and their Kindling accounts for judgment. Let the ledgers be true. Let the weights be fair."

Twenty-ninth of Frostveil. Eight weeks. Fifty-six days.

Isolde exhaled slowly through her nose. She picked up her pen again and wrote the date at the top of a fresh page. *1 Emberfall. Reckoning announced. 56 days to solvency.*

The numbers on the page did not lie. She had checked them three times this month alone. House Vane's assets totaled forty-seven thousand crowns in liquid reserves. Their debts to the rope-makers' guild and the anchor-iron consortium came to fifty-two thousand. The gap was five thousand crowns. Five thousand crowns they did not have and could not borrow. Not with House Corrin holding every marker in the Reach.

A ward cost thirty thousand crowns for a Reckoning cycle. A proper ward — contracted, consenting, registered with the Ascendancy — cost thirty thousand crowns minimum, and House Corrin's agents took a fifteen percent brokerage fee on top. Forty-five thousand crowns total. Money House Vane had not possessed when Isolde's mother stood for her Reckoning. Money they did not possess now.

The bell tolled again. Four times. The criers were moving on to the next district.

Isolde capped her inkwell. Her fingers were stained black at the tips, a permanent condition. She stood and crossed the room to the window, pressing her forehead against the cold glass. The Reach spread below her, a vertical city carved into the cliff face like layers of sediment. Lineage-houses clung to the rock in tiers, connected by bridges and stairways cut into the stone. Banners snapped in the updraft — Ashworth's silver hawk, Corrin's crimson serpent, Vane's broken arrow. The broken arrow hung limp today. The wind was light.

She could see the Gallery Bridge from here, a slender curve of woven steel cable and timber slats suspended over the abyss. Tiny figures gathered on its deck. The criers in their blue coats. A knot of Corrin agents in crimson sashes, already distributing broadsheets. The Reckoning schedule. The ward registry. The Kindling accountability forms.

Isolde's reflection stared back from the glass. Pale skin. Dark hair pulled back in a severe knot. Eyes the color of storm-clouds. Her mother's eyes. Her mother's jaw. Her mother's hands, though Isolde's were rougher now. Callused from rope-work. Scarred from anchor-iron.

She turned away from the window and moved to the far wall, where a narrow door stood concealed behind a tapestry depicting the First Ascent. The tapestry was moth-eaten at the edges. She pulled it aside and slipped through the door, closing it carefully behind her.

The space beyond was not a room. It was a cleft in the rock, a natural fissure widened by generations of Vane hands. No light reached it but what she brought. She set her lamp on a ledge of stone and reached for the coil of rope hanging from a peg.

This was the secret her mother had never known. The training her mother had never had time to learn.

Isolde's fingers found the knots by touch alone. She had tied them so many times her body remembered what her mind had long since stopped needing to direct. Figure-eight. Bowline. Double fisherman's. The rope moved through her hands like water.

She secured one end to a piton driven deep into a crack in the floor. The other end she fastened around her waist, just above the hip bones. The harness was old leather, softened by sweat and oil, molded to her body. She had made it herself from a discarded climbing harness and the straps of a broken pack.

Breathing. She stood in the center of the cleft. Feet shoulder-width. Weight balanced. Shoulders down. Jaw relaxed.

*Kindling.*

The word was a trigger. She had chosen it herself, a private command to separate the gift from the chaos of daily thought. The heat rose in her chest, familiar and terrible. It started behind her sternum, a coal dropped into dry grass. It spread through her veins in heartbeats. Her skin flushed. Her pulse hammered in her throat.

She did not fly. She would not fly in a cleft three paces wide. The Kindling did not require flight. Flight was only its most visible manifestation. The gift was lighter than air. The gift was the rejection of gravity. The gift was the body becoming something other than weight.

Isolde reached for the heat. She did not push it away. She did not let it consume her. She *shaped* it.

The drill was simple. Hold the Kindling at the threshold of manifestation — the moment before lift, before the feet leave the ground — and keep it there. Second by second. Breath by breath. The burn consumed years of life at the threshold. Every second held cost roughly three weeks of natural span. But the threshold was where control lived. The threshold was where she could shave the cost down.

Her mother had never mastered the threshold. Her mother had only known the binary: ground or sky. Off or on. The Kindling took or it did not. When the Reckoning came, her mother had flown the audit pattern — three circuits of the Gallery Bridge, a climb to the High Aerie, a controlled descent — and the burn had taken eighteen years in forty minutes. Eighteen years. She had landed shaking, skin gray, eyes sunken. She had lived three more winters.

Isolde held the threshold. Sweat broke on her forehead. Her muscles trembled with the effort of staying earthbound while every fiber screamed to rise. The heat pressed against her ribs. Her vision sharpened. The dust motes dancing in the lamplight became distinct, each one a tiny world.

*One Mississippi. Two Mississippi. Three.*

She counted in the old tongue, the counting-rhyme her grandmother had taught her. The rhyme had no magic. It was only a metronome.

*Four. Five. Six.*

The burn bit deeper. She felt it in her joints now, a phantom arthritis. Her hair grayed at the temples — not truly, only the illusion of it, the Kindling's toll painting itself across her perception. She would not actually gray for months. The cost was real. The markers were not.

*Seven. Eight. Nine.*

She was at the edge. The threshold was a knife's edge. Push further and she would lift. Pull back and the Kindling would collapse, wasted, the years already burned gone for nothing. The skill was in the staying. The skill was in the *less*.

*Ten.*

She released.

The heat vanished like breath on cold glass. Her feet pressed solid against the stone. The trembling stopped. The phantom arthritis faded. She stood in the cleft, breathing hard, lamp-light steady on the rock walls.

She had held for ten seconds. Ten seconds at threshold cost roughly thirty weeks of life. Seven and a half months.

Five years ago, her first attempt had cost her two years for three seconds. Three years ago, eight months for six seconds. Last month, nine months for nine seconds.

The efficiency was improving. The math was working.

She unfastened the harness and coiled the rope. Her hands were steady now. She returned the lamp to its peg and slipped back through the hidden door, letting the tapestry fall into place.

The main room was colder. The oil lamp had burned lower. She should trim the wick. She should return to the ledger. She should calculate exactly how many more drills she could afford before Frostveil.

A knock came at her chamber door. Sharp. Official.

Isolde froze. The knock came again. Three raps. A pause. Two raps.

The auditor's signal. She had read the protocol in the Ascendancy manuals her mother had hoarded. Three raps for a summons. Two for a courtesy visit.

She crossed the room and unbarred the door.

Kael Ashworth stood on the threshold.

He wore the charcoal coat of a Reckoning auditor, the silver thread at the cuffs catching the lamplight. His hair was dark, cut short above his ears. His face was pale, angular, the cheekbones sharp enough to cut. He did not look like a man who climbed cliffs for a living. He looked like a man who read about cliffs in reports.

His eyes found hers. Gray-green. The color of shallow seawater.

"Isolde Vane," he said. His voice was low, pitched for the corridor. "Auditor Kael Ashworth. I'm here to deliver the formal notice of Reckoning and to conduct the preliminary asset review."

Isolde did not move aside. "The criers just announced it. I heard them from my window."

"Protocol requires personal service." He held up a folded document sealed with red wax. The serpent of House Corrin was impressed beside the Ascendancy's scales. "May I enter?"

The corridor behind him was empty. No guards. No witnesses. The preliminary review was always private, a courtesy extended to houses of good standing. House Vane had not been a house of good standing in twelve years.

Isolde stepped back. "The ledger is on the desk."

Kael entered. He closed the door behind him. He moved to the desk without hesitation, as if he had been here before. Perhaps he had. The Ashworth auditors had been reviewing Vane's books since the Accord was signed.

He broke the seal. He read the document. He set it down and opened the ledger.

Isolde watched him. She watched the way his eyes scanned the columns. The way his fingers turned pages without smudging the ink. The way he did not react to the deficit. He had seen it before. He had calculated it before.

"Five thousand crowns," he said. He did not look up. "The same gap as last cycle. The same gap as the cycle before."

"Interest on the rope-guild debt increased," Isolde said. "The anchor-iron consortium called in their marker early."

"Corrin's consortium."

"It's the only consortium."

Kael turned a page. His expression did not change. "Have you secured a ward?"

The question was routine. The answer was predetermined.

"No."

"Have you applied for a ward-bond through the Corrin exchange?"

"We cannot afford the brokerage fee."

"Have you petitioned the Ascendancy for a hardship waiver?"

"Petitions require a sponsor house. No house sponsors Vane."

Kael snapped the ledger shut. The sound was final. He looked at her then, really looked, and something shifted in his face. A muscle at his jaw tightened.

"The Reckoning is in fifty-six days," he said. "If House Vane presents without a ward, the Kindling audit will be conducted unbuffered. You understand what that means."

"I understand the mechanics."

"Do you." It was not a question. "The unbuffered audit burns the full cost on the Kindling wielder. No transfer. No mitigation. Eighteen years minimum for a standard pattern. More if the pattern is extended. More if conditions are adverse."

"I have prepared."

"Prepared." He said the word like a verdict. "Your mother prepared. She flew the pattern. She burned eighteen years. She died three winters later."

"She died of a fever."

"She died of a body that had no reserves left to fight a fever." Kael's voice hardened. "The Kindling does not kill quickly. It hollows. It takes the years that would have been your margin against illness, against accident, against time. You are twenty-four. Eighteen years burned leaves you forty-two. Another Reckoning cycle, another eighteen, leaves you sixty. The average lifespan in the Reach is seventy-three. You would have thirteen years of margin. One bad winter. One climbing accident. One epidemic."

Isolde's hands curled into fists at her sides. "I am not my mother. My control is better. I can reduce the burn."

"By how much."

"Enough."

"Enough is not a number. The Ascendancy requires numbers. The Reckoning requires numbers. Corrin's ward exchange requires numbers." He took a step closer. The lamplight caught the silver thread at his cuffs. "Isolde. I have reviewed your house's books for three cycles. I have watched the deficit grow. I have watched Corrin's agents circle. I know what happens to houses that fall to the ward monopoly. They become ward-farms. Their Kindling-wielders fly for Corrin's clients until they burn out. The lineage-house becomes a dormitory. The name becomes a ledger entry."

"And House Ashworth does nothing because the Accord forbids interference."

"House Ashworth does nothing because the Accord *is* the law. I am an auditor. My duty is to verify the ledger and witness the Kindling account. I do not make policy. I do not allocate funds. I do not interfere in the ward trade."

"Then why are you here? The notice could have been delivered by a clerk."

Kael was silent for a long moment. The lamp flared. A draft stirred the tapestry.

"Because I wanted to see you," he said quietly. "Before the machinery starts. Before the formalities bury us both. I wanted to know if there was anything I missed. Any asset unlisted. Any claim unpressed. Any path I haven't seen."

Isolde studied him. The auditor's coat. The silver thread. The gray-green eyes. House Ashworth was wealthy. House Ashworth was neutral. House Ashworth had held the auditor's seat for four generations, since the Accord was signed. They were the referees in a game where Corrin owned the field and the balls and the scoreboard.

"Why?" she asked. "Why does an Ashworth auditor care if Vane falls?"

Kael's mouth twisted. "Because I knew your mother. Because she flew the pattern for my father's Reckoning when I was a boy. Because she landed shaking and gray and she looked at me — a child in the gallery — and she said, 'Tell your father the numbers are honest. Tell him I did not cheat.' And then she climbed the stairs to her room and she did not come down for three days."

Isolde's throat tightened. "She never spoke of that."

"She wouldn't. She was Vane. Vanes do not speak of weakness."

"Vanes do not have the luxury of weakness."

"Neither do Ashworths." Kael's gaze held hers. "Fifty-six days. The preliminary review is complete. I will file my report noting the deficit and the absence of a ward. The formal audit begins on the twenty-ninth of Frostveil. I will be the presiding auditor. My father will not sit this cycle. He has recused himself."

"Your father recused?"

"Health grounds. He is seventy. The altitude affects him." Kael reached into his coat and withdrew a second document, thinner than the first. He set it on the desk beside the ledger. "This is a notice of Corrin's claim. They have filed a lien on the Vane lineage-house, citing the unpaid anchor-iron debt. If the Reckoning finds Vane insolvent, the lien executes automatically. The house transfers to Corrin. The Kindling-wielder — you — becomes a ward-asset of the Corrin exchange."

Isolde stared at the document. The crimson serpent stared back.

"Can they do that?"

"They have done it to three houses in the last decade. The Accord permits liens against Kindling-assets. The logic is that the Kindling is a productive asset of the house, like the rope-works or the anchor-forge. If the house defaults, the asset transfers."

"The Kindling is not a forge. It is a person."

"The law disagrees."

Kael turned toward the door. He paused with his hand on the latch.

"Isolde," he said. "If you have a path — any path — you need to walk it now. Before the twenty-ninth. Before the formal audit. Before the machinery grinds us both."

He left.

The door clicked shut. The lamp burned steady.

Isolde stood alone in the room with the ledger and the lien notice and the taste of ash in her mouth from the drill. She went to the desk and picked up the Corrin document. The ink was fresh. The seal was crisp. The language was precise and merciless.

She set it down atop the ledger. She picked up her pen.

*1 Emberfall,* she wrote. *Reckoning announced. Lien filed. 56 days.*

She capped the inkwell. She turned toward the hidden door.

The cleft was waiting. The rope was waiting. The threshold was waiting.

She had fifty-six days to shave the burn down to something survivable. She had fifty-six days to find five thousand crowns that did not exist. She had fifty-six days before Kael Ashworth stood on the Gallery Bridge with his ledger and his silver thread and watched her fly the pattern that would either save her house or kill her.

She pulled the tapestry aside.

The first drill of the day had been a test. The second would be real.

She stepped into the dark.
