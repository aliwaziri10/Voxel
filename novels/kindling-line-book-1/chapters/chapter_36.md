The council chamber smelled of old vellum and the sharp mineral tang of the cliff face bleeding through the stone. Kael stood at the center of the semicircle, the seal of his office heavy against his chest, and felt the weight of every gaze in the room settle on him like a physical thing. Seventeen councilors. Three senior auditors. His father in the high seat, face carved from the same gray stone as the walls.

He had not slept. The proof lay in the satchel at his feet, bound in red twine, the same documents he had carried for weeks while the rot spread beneath everything he thought he knew. The engineered failure of Sol's mother. The ward contract Corrin had slipped into the records. The dated offer from House Ashworth to rule against Vane, signed in his father's hand.

His fingers found the clasp of the auditor badge. Cold metal. The Kindling in his veins stirred, a low heat behind his ribs that had nothing to do with flight and everything to do with the moment stretching thin.

"Lord Auditor Ashworth," Councilor Veyne said. Her voice echoed in the vaulted space. "You requested this audience. The floor is yours."

Kael did not look at his father. He did not look at the door where Sol might be standing, where she had been every day since the rupture, silent and watching. He opened the satchel. The twine fell away. He lifted the first sheet, the ink still sharp despite the handling.

"On the eighteenth of Deepwinter, Year 2," he said, and his voice did not shake, "House Corrin submitted a ward contract for the Reckoning of Mara Vane. The contract named a ward who did not exist. The signature of the ward was forged. The auditor who certified it was Valerius Ashworth."

A murmur rose. Councilor Veyne raised a hand. The room fell back into the kind of silence that presses against eardrums.

"The ward trade is controlled by House Corrin," Kael continued. "The records show a payment of twelve thousand crowns from Corrin coffers to the ward registry three days before the Reckoning. The same registry that certified the ward's existence. The same registry that reports to this council." He turned the page. "The offer from House Ashworth to rule against Vane's claim bears my father's seal and the date of the seventeenth. One day before the Reckoning. The terms are explicit. A favorable ruling for Corrin in exchange for the ward contract's certification."

He set the page down. His hands were steady. The heat in his chest burned cleaner now, hotter.

"I swore an oath to the Accord," he said. "To truth before house. To justice before kin. I held this proof for weeks. I did not surface it. That failure is mine. The proof is real. The corruption is real. And I will not carry it another day."

He unclasped the badge. The metal sighed against his palm. He placed it on the stone dais, the sound final as a bell strike.

"I surrender my office," he said. "I surrender my name."

"Kael." His father's voice cracked the air. "You will stop this. Now. You are Ashworth. You are my son. You do not throw away three generations of service for a Vane girl and a grudge against Corrin."

Kael looked up then. The resemblance was brutal. Same jaw. Same eyes the color of storm-clouded slate. Same pride that had built a house on the edge of the world and called it destiny.

"I am not throwing it away," he said. "I am returning it. The house you built stands on a lie. I watched you sign the offer. I watched you certify the ward. I carried the proof and I stayed silent because I was afraid of what it would cost. That silence nearly killed a ward in the square. It nearly killed Sol. It will kill the next one, and the next, until someone stops paying the price."

"The price is order," Valerius said. "The price is the Accord holding. You think Corrin falls alone? You think the ward trade collapses and the Kindling stops burning? There are no wards without the trade. There are no Kindling-wielders who survive without wards. You tear down the system and everyone burns."

"Then we build something that doesn't require burning." The words came from somewhere deeper than calculation. "But not on the backs of women who couldn't afford the cost. Not on the backs of mothers who died because the price was rigged."

"Strike his name," Valerius said to the council. The command landed like a hammer. "Strike the name Ashworth from the auditor rolls. Let the record show that Kael Ashworth has renounced his house, his office, and his oath. Let no door in the Reach open to him. Let no contract bear his witness."

The senior auditors moved. Quills scratched on parchment. The sound was obscenely ordinary.

Kael felt something sever inside him, clean as a blade through silk. No dramatic tear. No shuddering collapse. Just the sudden absence of a weight he had carried since childhood, the name that had opened every door and closed every exit. He was no one now. A man with a satchel of proof and a heat in his chest and no shield against what came next.

He bent. Retrieved the badge. The metal was warm from his palm. He slid it into his coat pocket, not onto his chest.

"The proof remains," he said to the room. "The records are entered. The ward contract is void. Mara Vane's Reckoning was fraudulent. Her daughter's claim stands unless this council finds new grounds to deny it. Grounds that do not come from Corrin gold."

He turned. Walked toward the doors. Did not run. Did not look back at his father's face, though he felt the gaze like a brand between his shoulders.

The doors opened before he reached them. Sol stood there.

She wore the gray wool coat she had worn the night of the chase, the one with the torn sleeve she had never mended. Her face was pale, the freckles standing stark against skin drained of color. Her hands hung loose at her sides. She did not move to let him pass.

He stopped. The heat in his chest pulsed, a rhythm he had learned to read. Three years. Two years. The numbers shifted with every beat, the calculus of survival he had memorized in the dark.

"Sol," he said.

Her name in the chamber. The councilors behind him gone quiet. The auditors still scratching. His father's breathing, ragged and controlled.

"You knew," she said. The words were flat. Not a question. "You knew the ward was forged. You knew my mother's Reckoning was rigged. You knew your father signed the offer. And you held it. For weeks. You held it while I trained. While I begged you to help me understand why the numbers didn't add up. While I thought..." Her voice cracked. "While I thought you were the one person who might actually see me."

"I know."

"Don't." The word whipped. "Don't say you know. Don't make this about what you know. You chose. Every day you chose not to tell me. Every night you came to the ledge and watched me burn years off my life and you chose your name over my mother's truth."

The heat in his chest spiked. He felt the Kindling rise, the familiar rush of power that wanted wings, that wanted to lift him away from this moment and every moment like it. He breathed through it. The technique Sol had taught him, the counting, the anchoring. One. Two. Three. The years burned slower.

"I chose wrong," he said. "Every day. I chose wrong. And I am sorry. That word is not enough. I know it is not enough. But it is the only one I have that does not ask you to forgive me."

Sol's eyes were dry. Her jaw was set. The muscle in her cheek jumped once.

"You stood up there," she said, "and you read it all. Every line. You gave up everything. Your name. Your house. Your father. You burned it all down in front of them. And you did it knowing I was watching."

"I did it because you were watching."

The admission hung between them. The council chamber held its breath.

"Why?" she asked. "Why now? Why not three weeks ago? Why not before the square? Before the ward died?"

"Because I was a coward." The words tasted of ash. "Because I thought I could fix it quietly. Because I thought the system would correct itself if I just found the right lever. Because I was raised to believe that Ashworths do not break things, we repair them. And I forgot that some things cannot be repaired. They can only be broken open."

Sol was silent. The councilors behind him shifted. Someone cleared a throat. The scratching of quills had stopped.

"Is that true?" she asked. "Is that all of it?"

He met her eyes. The gray-green of them, the flecks of gold he had memorized in the dark of the ledge, the way they caught light when she flew. He had no shield left. No badge. No name. No house. Just the truth, raw and bleeding.

"No," he said. "It is not all of it. There is more. The ward trade. The contracts. Who really controls them. I have proof of that too. But it is not in this satchel. And it will cost more than my name to surface it."

Sol's gaze dropped to the satchel at his feet. Then to his pocket where the badge lay. Then back to his face.

"More," she repeated. "There is always more with you."

"There is always more with all of us. The difference is whether we let it stay buried."

She studied him. The silence stretched, filled with the weight of every lie he had told by omission, every night he had held her while the proof burned in his pocket, every time he had said *I will help you* and meant *I will help you on my terms*.

The Kindling in his chest settled into a low hum. The years ticked by, measurable now in heartbeats. He had perhaps forty left. Perhaps fewer. The count had always been approximate, the training only slowing the bleed, never stopping it. He had spent three years of them in the last month alone, the stress and the proximity and the nights he had flown to her ledge and back, burning distance like fuel.

"I don't know if I can trust you," Sol said. "I don't know if trust is a thing that exists between us anymore. Or if it ever did."

"I do not expect trust," he said. "I expect you to watch what I do next. And the next. And the next. That is all I have to offer."

A councilor stood. Veyne, her face impassive. "Lord Ashworth. The record is entered. The name is struck. You are dismissed from this chamber. The council will deliberate on the Vane claim in light of the new evidence. You will be summoned if your testimony is required further. As a private citizen."

Private citizen. The words landed in the space where his identity had been.

Kael inclined his head. "Thank you, Councilor."

He moved toward the door. Sol did not step aside. He stopped before her, close enough to smell the cold air clinging to her coat, the faint scent of pine resin and sweat and something underneath it all that was simply *her*. The Kindling stirred, hungry. He held it down.

"Go," she said. "Before I change my mind about something. Before I decide whether the woman who watched you read those lines is the same woman who stood on the ledge and let you hold her while you lied."

He passed her. The door closed behind him. The corridor stretched ahead, stone and shadow and the distant sound of wind against the cliff face. He walked. His boots on the flags. His breath in the cold. The satchel bumped against his hip with every step.

He had expected the falling to feel like falling. He had expected the void to open beneath him, the name stripped away leaving nothing but the drop. Instead he felt solid ground. Strange. Unfamiliar. The ground of having chosen, finally, the thing that could not be taken back.

The corridor opened onto the balcony over the Reach. The vertical city spread below, lineage-houses clinging to the rock like barnacles, bridges arcing between them, the Kindling-lanterns beginning to wake as Frostveil's short day bled into evening. Somewhere in that maze of stone and rope and light, House Vane clung to its narrow ledge. Somewhere, Sol's sister was training. Somewhere, Corrin's agents were watching.

Kael leaned on the railing. The stone was cold through his coat. The wind tasted of salt and ice and the metallic tang of the Kindling that ran through every bloodline in the Reach, diluted and distant and undeniable.

He was no one's son. No one's auditor. No one's ward. He was a man with a satchel of proof and a heat in his chest and a woman who might never forgive him watching the door of the council chamber, waiting to see what he would burn next.

The Kindling pulsed. Three years. Two years. The count continued.

He pulled the badge from his pocket. Turned it over in his hands. The silver surface caught the last light, the Ashworth sigil worn smooth by generations of thumbs. His grandfather's thumb. His father's. His own.

He dropped it over the railing.

It fell. A flash of silver against the gray stone. A glint against the lanterns waking below. It struck a bridge and bounced, spun, vanished into the shadows between houses.

The sound of its landing was lost to the wind.

Kael watched the space where it had disappeared. His fingers were empty. His chest was warm. The count ticked on.

Behind him, the council chamber door opened. He did not turn. He knew the footfalls. Light. Deliberate. The rhythm of someone who had walked ledges in the dark and learned to measure each step.

" You dropped your badge," Sol said.

Her voice came from close behind. Not the doorway. Right behind him.

He turned. She stood there, the balcony wind tugging at her coat, her hair escaping its braid in wild strands. Her eyes were still dry. Her jaw was still set. But something in the set of her shoulders had shifted, a fraction of a degree, the difference between bracing and leaning.

"It was not mine to keep," he said.

"Neither was the proof. You kept that."

" The proof belonged to the truth. The badge belonged to the lie."

Sol stepped closer. The Kindling in his chest surged, recognize, *reach*, the involuntary pull that had bound them from the first forced proximity in the training courtyard. He felt the years burn, a sudden sharp cost, and knew without looking that she felt the echo of it, the involuntary transfer that had no contract and no consent and no mercy.

He did not step back. He did not shield her. He stood in the wind and the fading light and let the cost take what it would.

"Why are you here?" he asked. "The council dismissed me. The deliberation is closed. You could be with your sister. With your claim. With anything that matters."

Sol's gaze dropped to his chest, where the Kindling burned visible now beneath his coat, a faint glow through the wool. The transfer marks on his skin, the evidence he had hidden and hidden and finally worn into the light.

"Because you dropped the badge," she said. "And you didn't watch it fall. You watched the space where it landed. Like you were checking something."

Kael was silent. The wind tore at his hair. The count in his chest ticked, *thirty-nine, thirty-eight*, the proximity cost steep and relentless.

"What were you checking?" she asked.

"Whether it was caught," he said. "Or whether it hit the bottom."

"And?"

"And I don't know yet. The shadows are too deep. But I will find out. That is what I do now. I find out what happens at the bottom of things."

Sol was close enough now that he could see the individual freckles across her nose. The small scar at her chin from a childhood fall. The way her pupils dilated in the dimming light, the Kindling in her own blood responding to his, the resonance they had never been able to break and never been able to name.

"Then I will come with you," she said.

The words were simple. Final. The kind of declaration that rearranged the architecture of a life.

"You have a Reckoning to prepare for," he said. "A claim to prove. A house to save."

"The claim is proven. The fraud is entered. The house stands or falls on its own merits now. My sister is ready. I have taught her everything I know. Everything except how to stop the burn when someone you..." She stopped. Swallowed. "Everything except that."

"The burn doesn't stop. It only slows. You taught me that."

"I taught you how to count. You taught me how to lie."

The words landed. He deserved them. He deserved every one.

"I did not lie about the proof," he said. "I did not lie about the badge. I did not lie about the name. I lied by silence. I lied by omission. I lied by choosing my safety over your truth. That is the only lie I will ever tell you again, because it is the only one I cannot take back."

Sol was silent for a long moment. The wind howled around the balcony, tearing at the lanterns on the bridges below. Somewhere a Kindling-winged messenger passed, a streak of fire against the darkening sky.

"Then tell me the rest," she said. "The more you mentioned. The ward trade. Who controls it. All of it. Tell me now, here, in the wind, where no one can hear and no one can stop you. Tell me and I will decide if the woman who watched you read those lines can become the woman who stands beside you while you burn the rest down."

Kael looked at her. Really looked. The gray-green eyes. The stubborn jaw. The hands that had held blades and ledgers and his face in the dark. The woman who had flown into a storm to save a ward she didn't know and burned three years doing it. The woman who had watched him choose silence and then watched him choose truth and was still standing here, asking for more.

He thought of the proof in the satchel. The deeper records. The name at the top of the ward registry that was not Corrin. The name that was older than the Accord, older than the Reach, older than the Kindling itself. The name that meant the trade was not a trade at all. It was a harvest.

He thought of the cost. The years. The count that had dropped from forty to thirty-eight in the space of this conversation. The knowledge that every moment near her burned him faster, and every moment away burned him slower but colder.

He thought of his father's face in the council chamber. The pride. The fear. The calculation that had built a house on corruption and called it order.

He thought of the badge falling. The shadows. The bottom he could not see.

"All right," he said. "All of it. But not here. The walls have ears even in the wind. There is a place. An old aerie on the north face. Abandoned since the Winter Collapse. No wards. No listeners. Just stone and sky."

"Then we go there. Now."

"Now. Tonight. The climb is hard. The ledges are slick with frost. You will burn years."

"I burn years breathing. Lead the way, private citizen."

The title landed between them, a joke and a wound and a promise all at once. Kael felt something in his chest loosen, a knot he had tied so long ago he had forgotten the rope.

"Kael," he said. "Just Kael."

"Kael," she repeated. The name sounded different in her mouth. Older. Heavier. Like a stone set into a foundation. "Lead the way, Kael."

He turned. The north face waited, a vertical labyrinth of ice and stone and memory. The satchel weighed against his hip. The Kindling burned in his veins, a furnace he had learned to feed with truth instead of silence.

Behind him, Sol followed. Her boots on the stone. Her breath in the cold. The sound of someone choosing, finally, the same dangerous path.

The wind took the rest.
