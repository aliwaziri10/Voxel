The lower market smelled of wet iron and rotting kelp. Kael moved through the press of bodies with his ledger tucked against his chest, the Auditor's seal visible on the leather. Three days since he had submitted the Corrin ledger extracts to the Central Archive. Three days since his father's messenger had found him in the records room with a summons he had burned rather than answered.

He should have expected the response to be faster than official channels.

The alley behind the salt-merchant stalls narrowed to a slit between two lineage-house foundations. Kael's boot struck something soft. He looked down. A rat, crushed flat. The market's refuse chute emptied here, a black throat swallowing waste from the levels above.

"Ashworth."

The voice came from above. Kael tilted his head back. Three figures on the catwalk twenty feet up. Corrin livery stitched dark on dark at their collars. No masks. They did not need masks in the lower market.

"Inquisitor Vane sends his regards," the lead figure said. "And a warning. The Archive copy is... incomplete."

Kael's hand drifted to the baton at his belt. Not a weapon. A badge of office. Useless against three Kindling-touched blades.

"Inquisitor Corrin," Kael corrected. "Vane holds no inquisitorial seat."

The lead figure smiled. "Not for long."

They dropped.

Kael moved. Not up. The catwalk was theirs. Not back. The alley mouth had filled with two more silhouettes. He went down, into the refuse chute.

The drop was fifteen feet. He hit sludge and something that cracked beneath his shoulder. Bone, perhaps. Old waste compacted into strata. The stench gagged him. He scrambled up, boots slipping on slime-slick stone, and found a maintenance hatch rusted shut.

A blade punched through the metal inches from his fingers. Daylight speared the darkness.

"Cornered," the lead assassin called down. "Like the rat you are."

Kael pressed his back to the curved wall. His marks burned hot along his forearm, the Kindling-scars pulsing in time with his heart. Two years since the first transfer. Two years since the night chase across the ledges when Sol's desperation had seared itself into his flesh without permission.

He had reported neither incident. The auditor's code demanded immediate declaration of any Kindling contact. He had lied in his quarterly filings. He had lied to his father. He had lied to the Reckoning Court.

The hatch shuddered. Metal screamed. They were prying it open from above.

Then the air changed.

A pressure dropped into the chute like a stone into still water. Kael's ears popped. The hair on his arms stood vertical. The marks on his forearm flared white-hot, visible through his shirt sleeve.

Sol.

She landed beside him in a crouch, boots silent on the sludge. Her face was streaked with soot. A cut bisected her left eyebrow, clotting dark. Kindling fire crawled along her fingers, not the controlled brushstroke of training but a wild corona that licked at her wrists.

She did not look at him. Her eyes tracked the hatch above.

"Three above," she said. "Two at the mouth. Five total."

"Corrin inquisitors."

"Corrin killers." She flexed her hands. The fire steadied, drawing into a controlled sheath along her forearms. "The ventilation shaft. Third junction. It connects to the old smelter flue."

"Blocked since the collapse."

"Cleared last week. I checked."

She moved before he could answer. Straight up the chute wall, fingers finding purchase in mortar gaps too shallow for a normal climb. Kindling enhanced grip. He had read the theory. Seeing it in practice was different.

Kael followed.

His boots found the same holds. His fingers burned with cold sludge and older grime. The hatch exploded outward behind them as they reached the junction. A cross-tunnel of brick and rusted iron, wide enough to walk doubled over. The ventilation shaft yawned to the left, a square throat swallowing darkness.

"Go," Sol said. She did not turn.

Kael went.

The shaft climbed at a steep angle. Rungs bolted into brick, half eaten by salt air. He climbed with his back to the void, Sol's boots clanging above him. The marks on his arm throbbed. Each pulse sent heat radiating toward his elbow, toward his shoulder.

Two years. The first transfer had taken two. The night chase had taken three more. The marks had not faded. They accumulated.

Behind them, shouts echoed. Metal on metal. The assassins entering the junction.

"Faster," Sol called down. Her voice carried strain.

Kael's lungs burned. His arms trembled. He had not climbed like this in years. Not since the auditor's trials, when physical fitness was tested alongside legal knowledge. He had passed. Barely.

A rung gave way. His foot slipped. He caught himself on the next rung down, shoulder jarred, breath knocked loose.

Sol's boot appeared beside his face. She had descended two rungs. Her hand clamped onto his wrist. Not a grip. A lock. Kindling fire flowed from her palm into his forearm, hot and sharp.

"Breathe," she said.

The fire did not burn. It seeped into the marks, into the hunger that lived beneath them. For a heartbeat the pain vanished. Then it returned, changed. Duller. Contained.

"Two years," she said. "Maybe three. The transfer took more than I intended."

Kael stared at her hand on his wrist. The marks glowed through her fingers, white light bleeding between them.

"You feel it," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Good." She released him and climbed on. "Keep climbing."

He climbed.

The shaft opened onto a platform. The old smelter flue gaped ahead, a tunnel of blackened brick large enough for a cart. Sol dropped onto the platform and turned, offering a hand down.

Kael took it. Her palm was callused, hot. The marks on his arm flared again as their skin contacted. Not a transfer. A resonance. Like striking a tuning fork beside another tuned to the same note.

He swung up beside her. The platform shuddered. Dust sifted from the ceiling.

"They're in the shaft," Sol said. "Two minutes. Maybe three."

She led him into the flue. The air grew hotter. Sulfur and old ash coated the walls. Side tunnels branched at intervals, sealed with brick and warning signs.

"Where does this exit?" Kael asked.

"Upper forge district. Abandoned since the gas pocket killed the shift crew in Year One."

"Sealed by Court order."

"Sealed by Corrin order. The Court ratified it." She did not slow. "There's a maintenance hatch at the thirty-yard mark. Rust-sealed. I can breach it."

"And if you can't?"

"Then we fight in the open. Better than fighting in a tube."

The flue stretched straight and featureless. Their boots raised clouds of ash. Kael's breathing steadied. The marks on his arm had settled to a dull throb. Two years. The knowledge settled in his chest like a stone in still water.

Two years of his life. Gone into the dark.

He had known the cost was real. He had read the medical reports, the auditor case studies, the Corrin propaganda about ward contracts protecting both parties. He had not known the hunger.

It was not pain. Pain was sharp. This was a hollow place behind the ribs, a mouth opening in the dark, waiting. When Sol's fire had touched him, the mouth had snapped shut. Satisfied. For a moment.

Now it opened again.

"What is it?" Sol asked. She had glanced back. Her eyes caught the faint light from the shaft behind them.

"What?"

"The hunger. You're feeling it."

Kael almost denied it. The auditor's instinct: conceal weakness, control information. The words died in his throat.

"Yes."

Sol's face did not change. She faced forward. "It's the Kindling recognizing a compatible vessel. The marks create a... channel. The more transfers, the wider the channel. The hunger grows."

"Can it be closed?"

"No." She spoke flatly. "Only managed. Wards manage it by contract. Consent. Controlled burns on a schedule. You..." She hesitated. "You have no contract. No schedule. The hunger feeds on proximity."

"On you."

"On the source. The Kindling wants what it gave. It pulls."

Ahead, the flue widened. A circular hatch dominated the ceiling, its iron ring fused with rust. Sol stopped beneath it. She pressed her palm flat against the metal.

Fire bloomed.

Not the controlled sheath. A white lance of heat that screamed against the iron. The metal groaned, cherry-red spreading from her touch. Rivets popped. The hatch sagged.

Sol gritted her teeth. Her free hand clenched at her side. The marks on her own arms, faint tracery visible at her cuffs, pulsed in counterpoint to Kael's.

The hatch tore free. It crashed into the flue above, sending a cascade of brick dust and cold air.

Sol sagged. Kael caught her elbow before she could fall.

The contact sent a jolt through both of them. His marks flared. Hers answered. The hunger in his chest snapped shut again, then reopened wider. Deeper.

"Two years," she breathed. "Maybe three. Each time."

She pulled away. The connection severed. Cold air rushed in where her warmth had been.

"Go," she said. "I'll follow."

Kael climbed through the hatch into a corridor of fitted stone. Upper forge district. Abandoned machinery loomed in shadows. A ladder ascended to a street grate sealed with wax and the Corrin sigil.

He waited at the top. Sol emerged behind him, moving slower now. The Kindling fire had guttered to embers along her forearms. Sweat darkened her hairline.

The grate yielded to a kick. Night air hit them. Cold. Clean. Stars overhead, sharp as blown glass.

They stood in a narrow alley between silent forges. The Reach's great chain curved overhead, the lineage-houses hanging like lanterns from its links. House Ashworth's spire caught starlight on its warding crystals. House Corrin's bulk blotted out the stars to the east.

Kael's breath fogged. His arm throbbed. Two years. The knowledge was physical now, not abstract. A weight in his bones.

"Your father's summons," Sol said quietly. "You burned it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Kael looked at her. The cut on her eyebrow had reopened, a thin trickle tracing her temple. Her eyes held no accusation. No demand. Only a question offered freely.

"Because it was an order to rule against your house. Dated yesterday. Sealed with his signet." Kael's voice was rough. "Because I have the proof of what Corrin did to your mother. Because I hid it from you for weeks. Because every day I delay, the Reckoning approaches and I have done nothing."

Sol wiped blood from her temple. "You submitted the ledger extracts to the Archive."

"Three days ago. Corrin's response was tonight."

"Then you're doing something."

"Am I?"

She studied him. The hunger in his chest pulsed. Her marks answered, a silent conversation beneath skin.

"The repair isn't a single act," she said. "It's every act after. You know that."

"I know."

"Then climb."

She turned toward the great chain's nearest anchor point. A maintenance ladder climbed the cliff face, bolted into living rock. The legitimate path. The watched path.

Kael followed.

Halfway up, his boot slipped on frost-slick iron. He caught himself. The marks burned. Below, in the alley, figures emerged from the forge shadows. Corrin livery. More than five.

They had not lost the pursuit. They had only delayed it.

"Contact," Sol called down. "Six. Maybe eight."

"Archers?"

"Two. Crossbows. The rest blades."

Kael climbed faster. The hunger in his chest gnawed. Each rung brought him closer to Sol above him, closer to the source. The marks on his arm grew hotter. The pull strengthened.

He understood now why wards were contracted. Why the trade existed. The hunger was not metaphor. It was a living thing, a parasite born of stolen years, feeding on proximity to its maker.

And Sol carried the source.

She reached the anchor platform. Turned. Extended a hand down the final ten feet.

"Come on."

Kael reached. His fingers brushed hers. Fire jumped the gap.

Not a transfer. A sharing. The hunger in him met the fire in her and for a heartbeat they burned together, two candles fed from the same wax. The years lost settled into place. Two years. Three. Five. Seven. The count stabilized.

He hauled himself onto the platform. Sol did not release his hand.

Below, crossbow bolts struck iron. Sparks showered into the dark.

"Move," she said.

They ran along the anchor platform to the chain itself. The great links, each the width of a man's torso, connected to a winch house carved into the cliff. The winch house was Ashworth territory. Neutral ground by ancient treaty.

Corrin assassins would not follow onto Ashworth steel. Not openly.

Kael's boots rang on the chain. Sol matched his pace. The wind tore at them, cold and clean. The marks on his arm pulsed in rhythm with his strides.

Two years. The number settled into his bones.

He had spent twenty-seven years building a career on precision. On the auditor's creed: truth measured, recorded, reported. He had lied in his filings. He had concealed evidence. He had burned his father's summons.

And tonight, hanging from a chain above the abyss, holding the hand of the woman he had failed, he felt the Kindling's hunger for the first time not as a curse visited upon him but as a burden they carried together.

The winch house loomed. Ashworth guards stood at the door, halberds leveled. They lowered weapons as Kael approached, recognizing the Auditor's seal.

"Lord Auditor," the sergeant said. "Your father waits inside."

Kael released Sol's hand. The hunger opened wide in his chest, a mouth gaping in the dark.

"Tell him I'm coming," Kael said. "After I see the Archive copy verified. The Corrin ledger extracts. I need confirmation they match what I submitted."

The sergeant hesitated. "Lord Valerius ordered—"

"I know what he ordered." Kael's voice cut the wind. "I also know what the law requires. The Archive verification is my right as sitting auditor. Inform him I'll report to him after."

The sergeant nodded sharply. "As you command, Lord Auditor."

Kael turned to Sol. She stood at the chain's edge, wind whipping her hair. The embers on her forearms had died. She looked small against the night. She looked infinite.

"Tomorrow," she said. "The Archive opens at sixth bell. I'll be in the public gallery."

"Sol."

She met his eyes. "Tomorrow."

He watched her descend the maintenance ladder on the far side of the platform, moving into Corrin territory, into the dark between houses. The hunger in his chest screamed at the distance. Two years. Maybe three. The cost of proximity paid in instalments.

He turned toward the winch house. Toward his father. Toward the proof he had buried and now must exhume.

The Reckoning was coming. The date was fixed. The countdown continued.

And for the first time since the night chase, Kael Ashworth did not walk alone into the dark.
