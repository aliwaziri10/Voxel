The ink had not yet dried on the page when Kael Ashworth realized he had already decided to lie.

His quill hovered over the official auditor's log, the vellum stiff and cold beneath his fingertips. The lamplight in his office at the Reckoning Spire threw long shadows across the stone walls, and somewhere below, the great chains that bound the lineage-houses to the cliff face groaned in the wind. Eight Emberfall. The date sat at the top of the report in his precise hand, the numerals sharp and deliberate.

*Incident Report: Unsanctioned Kindling Manifestation. Location: Lower Terrace, House Vane boundary marker. Time: 23:47, 7 Emberfall.*

He had written the facts first. That was the auditor's discipline. Facts before interpretation. Sequence before consequence.

*Auditor Kael Ashworth, House Ashworth, conducting routine boundary verification pursuant to Article Twelve of the Reckoning Accord. Subject: Isolde Vane, sole claimant of House Vane. Observed unauthorized Kindling deployment at territorial boundary. Manifestation duration: approximately four seconds. Visual confirmation of cost-transfer phenomenon.*

His hand had not trembled. It never did. The Ashworth line bred steadiness into the bone, or so his father said. Valerius Ashworth had held the High Auditor's seat for twenty years before the Accord reshaped the title, and the weight of that lineage pressed against Kael's shoulders every time he signed his name.

He read the next line he had written, the one that made the report official, the one that would be copied into the Spire's archives and forwarded to the Corrin ward-registry within the week.

*Recipient of transferred cost: [REDACTED].*

The brackets were neat. The word inside them was a blade he had sheathed in protocol language. He stared at the redaction until the letters blurred.

Protocol demanded identity. The ward-registry existed to track every soul who absorbed Kindling burn, every contracted ward who consented to the exchange, every coin that changed hands in the Corrin-led trade. An unnamed recipient was a ghost in the ledger. A violation of Article Seven. A gap in the chain of accountability that the Corrin auditors would flag within hours of receipt.

He should have written *Kael Ashworth, Auditor, House Ashworth.* He should have written his own name and accepted the consequence: a formal inquiry, a medical examination by the Spire's physickers, a notation in his permanent record that he had stood too close to a Vane during manifestation. The cost was measurable. The physickers would find the markers in his blood, the faint scarring at his fingertips where years had been shaved away in the space of four seconds. Four seconds. He had counted them in the dark, pressed against the cold stone of the boundary marker, feeling the heat crawl up his arms like something alive.

Four seconds. Approximately three months of natural lifespan. A trivial sum for a man of twenty-eight with no dependents and a career measured in decades. The physickers would confirm it. The inquiry would clear him. The notation would follow him, but notations were the currency of auditors. He had dozens already.

Instead he had written *[REDACTED].*

The quill scratched as he added the next line, the justification he knew no one would accept.

*Redaction authorized under Auditor's Discretion Clause, Article Nine, Section Three: protection of ongoing investigation integrity.*

A lie. There was no ongoing investigation. There was only a woman who had pulled him back from the terrace's edge when the wind shifted, who had burned her own years to stop his fall, who had stood shaking in the aftermath while the transfer marks bloomed across his skin like bruises made of light.

Isolde Vane. Sol.

He had watched her hands afterward, the fingers she pressed to her own wrists as if checking a pulse. She knew what she had done. She knew what it cost. She had not asked his permission. The Kindling did not ask. It took from the nearest living body when the wielder pushed past their own limit, and she had pushed past hers to catch him.

The memory surfaced unbidden: her face in the lantern-light, pale and drawn, the dark hair escaping its tie, the eyes that held his with a mixture of fury and something that might have been fear. *Stay down,* she had said. *Don't move. Let it settle.*

He had not moved. He had lain on the cold terrace stones while the heat worked through him, while his body absorbed what she could not hold, while the years burned clean from his future and settled into his marrow. She had knelt beside him, her breathing ragged, one hand hovering over his chest without touching. Waiting. Watching. Counting.

*Auditor,* she had said when the worst passed. *You'll have to report this.*

*Yes,* he had replied. *I will.*

She had nodded once, sharp and short. *Then report it. But don't you dare tell them it was me who caught you.*

The wind had carried her voice away. She had vanished into the vertical labyrinth of House Vane before the Spire's lanterns could find her, climbing the sheer face with the ease of someone born to the stone. He had watched her go, his fingers pressed to the transfer marks on his forearm, feeling the phantom heat pulse in time with his heart.

Now, in the quiet of his office, the report waited for his signature.

The Ashworth seal sat in its wax warmer, the metal head catching the lamplight. A stylized hawk, wings spread. His father's seal, lent to him when he passed the auditor's examination three years past. *Use it well,* Valerius had said. *The seal is the law made visible. What you stamp, you answer for.*

Kael lifted the quill. His hand formed the name without conscious thought: *Kael Ashworth, Auditor.*

The ink pooled. He set the quill down.

The report was complete. Official. Ready for the morning courier. The redaction would be noticed. Questions would be asked. He would answer them with the clause he had cited, and the Corrin auditors would challenge it, and the matter would escalate to a review board where he would have to defend a decision he could not explain without exposing her.

Exposing her. The phrase tasted of betrayal.

She was a Vane. A fallen house. A claimant whose lineage teetered on the edge of dissolution, whose mother had died in the Reckoning three years past because no ward could be afforded, whose Kindling burned hot and uncontrolled because training required masters House Vane could not pay. She was everything the auditor's oath required him to observe, document, and judge.

She had saved his life.

The transfer marks on his forearm throbbed, a dull ache beneath the skin. He pulled back his sleeve. The marks were faint, a lattice of pale lines tracing the paths where her years had entered him. In the lamplight they looked like frost on glass. Beautiful. Terrible.

Three months. Perhaps four. The physickers would measure it precisely. They would want to know the donor's identity to calculate the burn-rate, to verify the transfer mechanics, to update the ward-registry's actuarial tables. The Corrin trade thrived on precision. Every year absorbed, every coin exchanged, every contract signed in blood and witnessed by an auditor.

Wards were contracted. Consenting. Paid.

He was none of those things.

The Kindling had taken from him without asking. She had not asked. The mechanism did not allow asking. That was the horror of it, the truth House Corrin buried beneath layers of contract law and commercial language: the transfer was involuntary, absolute, and blind. The nearest body paid. Always.

*Unless a ward stands closer.*

The thought rose unbidden, the Corrin logic he had recited a thousand times in hearings and disputes. Wards existed to control the uncontrollable. To place a chosen body between the wielder and the innocent. To make the cost a transaction instead of a theft.

House Vane had no ward. House Vane had never had a ward. The Corrin trade priced wards beyond the reach of fallen houses, and the Accord did not mandate provision. So when a Vane manifested, everyone nearby became an involuntary ward. Everyone paid.

Including auditors conducting routine boundary verification.

Including the son of the former High Auditor.

Kael leaned back in his chair. The stone walls of the Spire office seemed to close inward. The chains below groaned again, a deep metallic sound that vibrated through the floor.

He should amend the report. He should write his name in the brackets and accept the inquiry. The law was clear. The protocol was absolute. Auditors did not redact. Auditors did not protect subjects. Auditors recorded, verified, and witnessed.

*An auditor who cannot be trusted to record the truth cannot be trusted to enforce it.*

His father's voice. A lesson delivered in the Spire's training hall, the echo of a dozen failed candidates. Kael had been nineteen, certain of his place, certain of the law's clarity.

The law was not clear tonight.

He thought of the ward-registry in the Corrin district, the great ledgers where every absorption was logged, every contract filed, every coin tallied. The Corrin auditors would see the redaction. They would demand the name. They would cite Article Seven and the public trust and the integrity of the trade. They would not accept *investigation integrity* from a junior auditor of House Ashworth.

They would investigate him instead.

His career, his house's standing, his father's legacy — all balanced against three months of life he had not agreed to lose, absorbed from a woman who had no right to take them, who had taken them anyway because the alternative was his death on the terrace stones.

*You'll have to report this.*

*Yes. I will.*

He had not promised her what he would write.

The quill sat on the desk, the nib darkened with ink. The report waited. The courier would come at first light. The Spire never slept; the archives operated on a cycle as old as the Accord, and the morning shift would log the report before the sun cleared the eastern ridge.

He had until then.

Kael stood. The chair scraped against stone. He crossed to the window, a narrow arrow-slit that looked out over the Reach. The lineage-houses clung to the cliff face like barnacles, tier upon tier of stone and timber, connected by bridges and stairways carved into the rock. House Vane occupied the lower terraces, the shadowed levels where the wind hit hardest and the sun reached latest. House Ashworth rose above them, pale limestone catching the last light. House Corrin crowned the summit, gilded domes and spires that glittered in the lamplight of a thousand windows.

Somewhere in the shadows of the lower terraces, a woman climbed. She moved through the vertical dark with the certainty of someone who had never known level ground. Her years burned in his blood now, a debt he could not repay, a theft he had agreed to conceal.

The wind carried the scent of pine and cold stone. Below, the river cut a silver line through the valley floor, indifferent to the houses that clung above it.

*Protection of ongoing investigation integrity.*

The lie settled in his chest alongside the transferred years. He could feel them both, twin weights. The investigation did not exist. The integrity was compromised the moment he chose the brackets over the name.

But the alternative — the truth — would bring Corrin auditors to House Vane's door within days. They would examine her. They would measure her Kindling, test her control, calculate her burn-rate and her market value. They would offer a ward contract or a dissolution order. They would strip her of the only thing she owned: the right to decide how her gift was used.

House Corrin owned the ward trade. They owned the contracts. They owned the physickers who certified the absorptions and the auditors who witnessed them. A Vane with uncontrolled Kindling was an asset waiting to be acquired.

His father had taught him that the law protected the vulnerable. The Accord had been written to end the blood-feuds that carved the cliff face into graveyards. The Reckoning existed to test claimants fairly, to measure Kindling against cost, to ensure no house fell through corruption or coercion.

The Reckoning was three months away. The date had been fixed since the first chapter of the Accord. Eighty-seven days until Isolde Vane stood before the auditor's bench and proved her house's right to exist.

Eighty-seven days until the transfer marks on Kael's arm became evidence in a hearing he was honor-bound to preside over.

He turned from the window. The report lay on the desk, the redaction stark in the lamplight. The seal waited in its warmer.

He could burn the report. Write a new one. Name himself. Accept the inquiry. Protect his integrity.

He could send the report as written. Invite the challenge. Buy her time with his credibility.

He could — 

A knock at the door. Sharp. Official.

Kael froze. The courier was not due for hours. The night shift did not disturb auditors unless — 

"Auditor Ashworth." The voice was muffled by oak and iron. "Message from the High Auditor's office. Immediate."

Valerius. His father. The former High Auditor, now senior advisor to the Accord Council, a man who had not spoken to his son in six months except through formal channels.

Kael crossed the room. Opened the door.

A junior clerk stood in the corridor, face pale in the lantern-light, a sealed cylinder in his hand. The corridor stretched behind him, a tunnel of stone and shadow where the Spire's great chains vanished into the mountain's heart.

"Auditor." The clerk extended the cylinder. "The High Auditor requests your presence at first light. There is a matter of protocol requiring your attention."

Kael took the cylinder. Broke the wax. The hawk seal. His father's seal.

The note inside was brief, written in Valerius's precise hand.

*Kael. The Corrin registry flagged an anomaly in last night's boundary reports. An unsanctioned transfer with redacted recipient. You are directed to present your notes and testimony at the seventh hour. Bring the original log. Valerius.*

The paper was thin. Expensive. The kind used for communications that might be preserved.

Kael read it twice. The corridor was cold. The clerk waited, patient, unaware.

"Seventh hour," Kael said. His voice was steady. "I will be there."

The clerk bowed. Turned. Walked away down the corridor, footsteps echoing.

Kael stood in the doorway. The cylinder crushed in his fist. The note burned against his palm.

*Anomaly.* *Flagged.* *Redacted recipient.*

They knew. The Corrin registry had flagged it. Which meant the report had already been copied, transmitted, received. Which meant the courier had come in the night while he sat at his desk wrestling with brackets.

Which meant the redaction had not protected her. It had only marked her.

He returned to his desk. The report sat where he had left it. The ink was fully dry now. The redaction stared back at him, neat brackets around a word that did not exist.

*[REDACTED].*

The first tangible evidence that an uncontrolled transfer had occurred. The first crack in the protocol he had sworn to uphold.

He picked up the quill. The nib caught the vellum.

He could add a line. *Recipient identity withheld pending verification.* He could claim procedural caution. He could buy another day.

But the Corrin auditors would see that addition too. They would know he had amended after the fact. They would know he was hiding something.

The transfer marks on his forearm pulsed. Three months. Four. A debt he had not chosen, owed to a woman who had stolen his fall and given him her future in exchange.

*Stay down. Don't move. Let it settle.*

She had known. She had known exactly what would happen. She had done it anyway.

And he had let her.

The quill hovered. The lamplight flickered. Somewhere in the Spire's depths, the great chains shifted, and the sound rose through the stone like a heartbeat.

Kael Ashworth, Auditor of House Ashworth, son of Valerius, bound by oath and seal and the weight of generations, lowered the quill to the vellum and wrote the only truth he could afford.

*Addendum: The recipient's identity is known to this auditor. Disclosure is withheld under Article Nine, Section Three, pending determination of whether the transfer constitutes a violation of the Accord's protections for non-consenting parties. This auditor accepts full responsibility for the redaction and any consequent findings.*

He signed it. Sealed it. Pressed the hawk into hot wax.

The report was complete. The lie was official. The evidence was recorded.

He placed the document in the outgoing cylinder. Sealed it with his own mark. Set it by the door for the morning courier.

Then he sat in the dark office, alone with the marks on his arm and the clock ticking toward the seventh hour, waiting for his father to ask the question he could not answer: why a Vane's uncontrolled Kindling had burned an Ashworth auditor's years, and why that auditor had chosen silence over the law.

The wind screamed against the Spire's windows. The chains groaned. Somewhere in the vertical dark, a woman climbed toward a dawn that would bring auditors to her door, and the only shield she had was a redacted name in a report that had already been copied, transmitted, and flagged.

Kael closed his eyes. Counted the seconds. Waited for the morning to bring the consequences he had chosen.
