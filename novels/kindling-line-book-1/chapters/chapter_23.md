The page weighed nothing in Kael's palm. A single sheet of vellum, folded twice, its edges trimmed clean by a blade he had sharpened himself. The ink on it was months old, the hand precise and clinical. *Valerius Ashworth, Ruling Auditor.* The signature at the bottom of the ledger extract was his father's. The notation beside it, in a narrower script, bore the seal of House Corrin's chief factor.

Kael had carried the folded page for six days. Six days of moving through the Reckoning Hall's corridors with his auditor's badge visible, his expression calibrated to the bland neutrality the role demanded. Six days of watching the scribes at their desks in the central archive, noting which ones took tea with which factor's clerks, which ones left the building at the same hour each evening.

He had chosen Mara Vell. Mid-thirties, no house affiliation visible on her wrist cuffs, a reputation for discretion that had survived three changes in the archivist's seat. She worked the late shift in the west annex, where the light failed early and the heating pipes clanked through the walls like dying things. Her desk sat farthest from the door, a deliberate choice he had watched her make on three separate evenings.

Tonight the snow had begun to fall in earnest, thick flakes muffling the city's sounds against the cliff face. The Reckoning Hall's lamps burned low behind frosted glass. Kael approached the annex from the interior passage, his boots silent on stone worn smooth by generations of auditors. He carried no lantern. The page rested in the inner pocket of his coat, against his chest, where his hand could reach it without visible movement.

Mara Vell was bent over a ledger when he entered. She did not look up. "Auditor Ashworth. The Corrin account reconciliations are not due until the tenth."

"I am not here for Corrin accounts." He stopped at the edge of her desk. The air smelled of ink and cold stone and the faint sweetness of the oil she used on her joints. "I have a matter for the neutral record."

Her pen paused. She set it down with care, capping the nib. "The neutral record accepts testimony only during designated hours. You know this."

"This cannot wait for designated hours." He kept his voice flat. "It concerns the Vane Reckoning. The mother's hearing. Year 1."

Her fingers tightened on the pen cap. The only tell. "The Vane matter is closed. The ruling stands."

"The ruling was falsified." He withdrew the folded page. "This is an extract from the original ledger. One page. It shows the transfer of funds from Corrin's factor to the ward contractor who failed to appear at Sol Vane's mother's hearing. It bears my father's signature acknowledging receipt. It bears Corrin's seal on the disbursement order."

Mara Vell's face did not change. She looked at the page in his hand, then at his face. "You would have me copy this into the neutral record. An auditor's son accusing his own father of corruption. House Corrin of bribery. On a single page with no chain of custody."

"I would have you verify it. The ledger still exists in the sealed archive. The page numbers match. The ink composition can be tested. I am not asking you to believe me. I am asking you to do your duty."

"Auditor Ashworth." Her voice softened. "My duty is to record what is presented to me during designated hours with proper witness and seal. What you hold is hearsay. Dangerous hearsay. If I take it, I am not a scribe. I am a conspirator."

"You are a scribe who has seen the Corrin factor's clerk slip coins into the archivist's drawer. You are a scribe who has recorded the ward contracts that pay pennies to the dying and fortunes to the traders. You know what the neutral record is meant to be."

She was silent for a long moment. The heating pipes groaned. Somewhere above, a door slammed.

"Go," she said. "Take your page. I did not see you. I will not see you. If you have evidence, take it to the Reckoning Council. Take it to the public record. Do not ask me to be the knife in the dark."

Kael's jaw tightened. "The Council is stacked. The public record is sealed until the Reckoning. You know this."

"Then wait for the Reckoning. Like everyone else."

He placed the page on the desk between them. "If you change your mind, I will be in the auditor's gallery tomorrow at the midday recess. Ask for me at the gate. The guard knows my face."

He turned and walked out before she could respond. The cold hit him like a blow, the snow swirling around the annex's narrow windows. He did not look back. He had not expected her to agree. He had hoped.

The interception happened at the interior gate.

Two men in the gray cloaks of Corrin's household guard stepped from the shadow of the gatehouse arch. They did not draw weapons. They did not need to. The gate guard, a young auditor's clerk Kael had shared tea with three weeks past, stepped aside without a word.

"Auditor Ashworth." The taller of the two men spoke quietly. His face was forgettable, the kind that vanished from memory the moment you looked away. "Lord Corrin requests your presence. A matter of some urgency."

"Lord Corrin is not my superior." Kael stopped, his hands loose at his sides. "I am a Reckoning auditor. My business is with the Hall."

"Lord Corrin is the Hall's primary creditor. His request carries weight." The man gestured toward the carriage waiting in the courtyard beyond, its horses stamping in the snow. "Shall we walk, or shall we make a scene? The Reckoning approaches. Scenes are... unhelpful."

Kael looked at the clerk. The young man stared at his boots.

"Walk," Kael said.

The carriage ride was short. The Corrin townhouse clung to the cliff face three levels below the Reckoning Hall, its windows dark behind heavy shutters. They took him not to the receiving room but to a small study lined with shelves of bound ledgers. A fire burned low in the hearth. Lord Corrin sat behind a desk of black oak, his fingers steepled. He did not rise.

"Auditor Ashworth." The voice was smooth, unhurried. "You have been busy. A visit to the west annex. A conversation with Scribe Vell. A page offered, then withdrawn."

Kael said nothing. He stood with his weight balanced, his coat dripping on the rush mat.

"Mara Vell is a loyal servant of the record." Lord Corrin lifted a hand. "She reported your approach immediately, of course. She understands her position. As do we all."

"She refused me."

"She did. Wisely." Lord Corrin leaned back. "The page you carry is a forgery. A clever one. The ink matches the period. The hand mimics my factor's clerk. The seal is a passable impression. But the ledger it claims to come from was destroyed in the archive fire of Year 1. A tragedy. No copies survive."

"The ledger was not destroyed. It was sealed."

"Sealed by my father's order. As Ruling Auditor." A pause. "Your father's order."

The word landed. Kael's breath caught, once, silent in the cold room.

"You have been digging," Lord Corrin continued. "In places that do not concern an auditor of your standing. The Vane Reckoning is settled. The claim is denied. The lineage-house passes to Corrin stewardship at the spring equinox. These are facts. They will not change because a son dislikes his father's legacy."

"My father signed the disbursement. Your factor countersigned. The ward contractor was paid to fail."

"Your father signed many things. He was a busy man. He is dead. The dead cannot defend themselves. The living, however, must be careful." Lord Corrin rose, moving to the fire. "You have a choice, Auditor Ashworth. You return to your duties. You forget the page. You forget the scribe. You preside over the Vane Reckoning with the neutrality your badge requires. In return, House Ashworth retains its seat on the Reckoning Council. Your brother's betrothal to the Corrin daughter proceeds. Your position is secure. Your life continues as it has."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the forgery is discovered. A page planted in the archive by a disgruntled auditor seeking to sabotage the Reckoning. The neutral record will reflect your misconduct. House Ashworth will disavow you. The Council will strip your badge. And you will find yourself very alone, very far from the cliff, with no house to claim you and no law to protect you." Lord Corrin turned, his face pale in the firelight. "The ward trade is not a game, Kael. It is the foundation of this city. Of every city. You cannot break it. You can only break yourself against it."

The carriage returned him to the Reckoning Hall. The snow fell heavier now, erasing tracks almost as they were made. Kael walked through the interior passages to his quarters, the stone cold through his boot soles. The folded page burned against his chest, heavier than lead.

His quarters were sparse. A desk, a bed, a washstand, a chair. The lamp on the desk had guttered out. He lit it with trembling fingers, the flare casting shadows against the walls.

He set the page down. Unfolded it. The ink was black, the lines sharp. *Disbursement: Ward Contract 447-Vane. Contractor: Heslin & Sons. Status: Defaulted. Payment rendered per Corrin Factor authorization. Ruling Auditor acknowledgment: V. Ashworth.*

His father's initials. The Corrin seal. The date: three days before Sol Vane's mother's Reckoning hearing. Three days before the ward who should have stood beside her failed to appear. Three days before the Kindling consumed her in a single uncontrolled flare, burning twenty-three years in forty seconds while her daughter watched from the gallery.

Kael's hand closed around the inkwell. Glass and iron, heavy in his grip. The ink inside was thick, black, the scent of gall nuts and iron sulfate sharp in the closed room.

He threw it at the wall.

It shattered. Ink sprayed across the stone, across the desk, across his coat sleeves, his fingers, the page. Black droplets spattered the vellum, obscuring the text, eating into the fibers. The sound of breaking glass was loud in the quiet. Loud in his skull.

He stood breathing hard, his knuckles white on the desk edge. His fingers were stained black to the second knuckle. The ink seeped under his nails, into the creases of his palms. He stared at his hands, at the darkness spreading across his skin.

*Evidence. Proof. Truth.*

The words circled in his mind, hollow.

He could take the page to the Council. He could demand a hearing. He could present the ledger extract, call for the sealed archive to be opened, force the ink test, the handwriting analysis. He could name his father. He could name Corrin. He could blow the Vane Reckoning wide open three months before the date set in stone.

The calculation unfolded, cold and precise.

If he acted now: The Council would convene an emergency session. Valerius Ashworth's name would be entered into the record as a corrupt auditor. House Ashworth would be censured. Kael's badge would be suspended pending investigation. His brother's betrothal would collapse. The Corrin alliance would fracture. House Corrin would retaliate -- legally, financially, physically. The ward trade records would be sealed tighter. The Vane claim would be delayed, not reopened. Sol Vane would face her Reckoning with no new evidence, no ward, no hope.

If he waited: The Reckoning proceeded as scheduled. He presided. He ruled against Vane, as Ashworth had offered. As Corrin demanded. The lineage-house passed to Corrin stewardship. Sol Vane lost everything. Her mother's death remained a tragedy, not a crime. Kael kept his position. His house kept its favor. His brother married. The ward trade continued. The system held.

The cost of action: his career, his house, his brother's future, possibly his life.

The cost of inaction: Sol Vane.

He had known the calculation in chapter twenty. He had made it then. He had hidden the proof. He had accepted the Ashworth offer. He had told himself he would find another way. A cleaner way. A way that did not require burning everything down.

There was no cleaner way. There never had been.

The ink on his fingers was drying, tacky and tight. He flexed his hand, feeling the skin pull. The page on the desk was ruined, the text blurred into illegibility. The only copy. The only physical proof he had dared remove from the sealed archive.

He could return. He could photograph the original ledger. He could memorize the entries. He could find another scribe, another angle, another lever.

But the Reckoning was fixed. The date was carved in the Hall's foundation stone. Forty-seven days. Forty-seven days until Sol Vane stood on the platform and the Kindling took her.

Forty-seven days to destroy himself for a chance that might not exist.

He picked up the ruined page. The vellum was soft with ink, the fibers breaking down. He held it over the lamp flame. The edge caught. Fire crawled across the blackened text, consuming the forgery, the evidence, the hope. Ash fell to the desk, gray and weightless.

He watched it burn. The flame licked his fingertips. He did not drop it until the last corner curled to ash.

The open question was not whether he would act. The question was whether the ash on his fingers would ever wash clean.
