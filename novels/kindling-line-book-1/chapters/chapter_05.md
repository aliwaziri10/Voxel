The inspection warrant had arrived three days before the date stamped on its wax seal. Kael had read the schedule twice, noted the required personnel, and still felt the weight of it settle in his shoulders like a second cloak. The Vane lineage-house clung to the western face of Thornmere Reach, its terraced gardens and sleeping quarters carved into limestone that had weathered three centuries of wind and salt. House Vane had once held the highest aerie on the cliff. Now their claim extended only to the middle terraces, and even those were contested.

Sol stood at the edge of the lower garden wall, her boots dusted with pale grit. She wore the muted gray of a house that had sold its colors to pay debts, her hair pulled back in a practical knot. The Kindling marks on her wrists were hidden beneath leather cuffs, but Kael had seen them in the file. He had seen everything in the file.

"The shear zone runs along the north face," she said without looking at him. Her voice carried over the wind. "The surveyor's report from last autumn marked it stable. The surveyor was paid by House Corrin."

Kael unfolded the map clipped to his board. "The report bears the Reckoning seal."

"The Reckoning seal means the auditor who signed it wasn't looking at the rock." She turned. Her eyes were the color of storm water. "It means he was looking at the ledger."

He didn't flinch. He had expected this. The antagonism was ritual now, a dance they had perfected over four previous inspections. "Then we'll look at the rock together. That's why I'm here."

They began at the western stair, descending into the cool shadow of the overhang. The air smelled of mineral water and old moss. Iron rings driven into the cliff face marked the old ascent routes, their rust staining the stone in orange tears. Sol moved ahead of him, sure-footed on the narrow ledge, her hand brushing the wall as if reading braille.

"Microfractures at the third tier," she murmured. "Hairline. They've widened since the autumn storms."

Kael noted it on his map. "Depth?"

"Thumb-deep at the widest. The binder mortar is crumbling where the water seeps." She scraped a fingernail along a fissure. Granules fell. "House Corrin's surveyor called it surface weathering. He didn't tap the face."

Kael tapped it himself. The sound was wrong — hollow, like striking a drum skin stretched over rot. He marked the map again. "We'll need a core sample."

"Which requires a drill, which requires a permit, which requires Corrin's signature on the requisition." She didn't look back. "The circle closes."

They reached the shear zone at mid-morning. The ledge here widened into a natural platform, perhaps twenty feet across, where the cliff face bowed outward in a gentle curve. Above them, the rock ceiling dripped with slow persistence. Below, the Reach fell away into mist.

"This is where the autumn surveyor stood," Sol said. She crouched, pressing her palm flat against a vertical crack that ran from the ledge to the ceiling. "He marked it stable from here. He never climbed the chimney."

"Because the chimney was deemed unstable."

"Because the chimney leads to the upper terraces. The ones Corrin wants."

Kael watched her. The wind tugged at the loose strands at her temples. She looked smaller than he remembered, or perhaps he had simply forgotten how much space she took up in a room. The file had listed her height, her weight, the exact measurement of her Kindling resonance. It hadn't listed the way she held herself when she thought no auditor was watching — like a coiled spring, like something about to become airborne.

"Show me the chimney," he said.

She rose in a single fluid motion. "It's a tight squeeze. You'll need to leave the board."

"I'll manage."

The chimney was a vertical slit in the rock, barely wide enough for shoulders. They climbed in silence, Kael following Sol's boots, his fingers finding the same holds she had used. The stone was cold, damp. Thirty feet up, the slit widened into a bell-shaped cavity where a centuries-old rope anchor still hung, its fibers gray and fragile.

Sol braced her back against one wall, her feet against the other. "The shear plane runs horizontal here. The upper block is shifting. Millimeters per year, but the acceleration changed after the winter rains."

Kael wedged himself opposite her, his notebook balanced on one knee. "Acceleration data?"

"Measured. Not reported." She tilted her head back, examining the ceiling. "The anchor point for the binder rods is compromised. If the block slips another centimeter, the whole face goes."

"Then the binder rods need replacement."

"Which requires drilling. Which requires — "

"I know the circle." He wrote: *Shear zone active. Acceleration observed. Binder anchor compromised. Immediate remediation required.* "I'll file the emergency directive today."

"Corrin will appeal."

"Let them. The directive holds pending appeal. That buys us months."

"Months the cliff doesn't have."

He looked up at her. "Then what do you propose?"

She didn't answer. Her gaze had fixed on something above them — a hairline fracture in the ceiling, barely visible in the dim light. Her body went still in a way that had nothing to do with balance.

"Sol?"

"Quiet."

The word was barely a breath. Then the mountain moved.

It started as a vibration in the soles of Kael's boots, a low subsonic hum that traveled up through bone. Dust sifted from the ceiling. A pebble dropped, then another, then a cascade of gravel. The fracture in the ceiling widened with a sound like tearing canvas.

Sol moved before Kael could process the motion. She didn't scramble. She didn't shout. Her hands came up, palms outward, and the air around her shimmered — heat rising off summer pavement, light bending wrong. The Kindling.

Kael had read the theory. He had seen the diagrams. He had never seen it live.

Golden threads erupted from her fingertips, not fire but something stranger — luminous filaments that sought the rock like roots seeking water. They sank into the fracture, into the crumbling binder, into the microfractures spiderwebbing through the ceiling. The glow intensified, shifting from gold to white-blue, and the temperature in the chimney spiked. Kael's skin prickled. His breath caught in a throat suddenly dry.

"Hold," Sol whispered. The word vibrated in the confined space. "Hold hold hold."

The rock groaned. Dust became chunks. A slab the size of a dinner plate broke free and fell past Kael's shoulder, missing his head by inches. He flinched, instinct overriding training, and his shoulder pressed against Sol's arm.

The transfer hit him like a hammer blow to the funny bone — sharp, electric, wrong. His vision whited out at the edges. His right forearm erupted in cold fire, a line of sensation tracing from wrist to elbow in the span of a heartbeat. He gasped, his fingers spasming on the rock, and through the ringing in his ears he heard Sol make a sound — not a scream, something smaller, a broken inhale.

The Kindling flare died as suddenly as it had ignited. The threads retracted into her fingertips. The rock settled with a final heavy sigh.

Kael hung in the chimney, panting, his heart a trapped bird. His right arm felt foreign. He forced his eyes to focus on his forearm.

A faint luminescent line etched itself across the skin, glowing with the same white-blue as the Kindling. It pulsed once, twice, then began to fade, sinking beneath the surface like ink absorbing into parchment. The cold fire resolved into a dull ache, deep in the bone.

"Gods," he breathed.

Sol had gone utterly still. Her face was pale, sweat beading at her hairline. Her eyes were fixed on his arm, on the spot where the mark had been. Her chest rose and fell in shallow bursts.

"Sol." His voice came out rough. "What — "

"I didn't choose you."

The words fell into the chimney like stones into a well.

Kael stared at her. "Choose me?"

"The cost." She swallowed. Her voice cracked. "It transfers to whoever is nearest. I can't direct it. I can only — " She stopped. Her jaw worked. "I can only reduce how much burns. Not who pays."

The chimney felt smaller. The air thicker. Kael looked at his forearm again, at the ordinary skin that had moments ago borne witness to something that should not exist. The Kindling burned years from the wielder. That was the law, the science, the bedrock of the Reckoning. When forced to use it near another, the cost transferred involuntarily. That was the theory, written in the auditor's manuals with clinical detachment.

He had not expected to feel it.

"Years," he said. "How many?"

Her throat moved. "Less than it would have taken from me. I've trained to reduce the magnitude. But I cannot choose the recipient."

"Training." The word tasted of ash. "How long have you been training?"

"Since I was fourteen."

"And your mother?"

"Had no ward. Could not afford one." The words were flat, stripped of defense. "The Reckoning took her because she had no buffer. Because House Corrin prices wards beyond the reach of fallen houses."

Kael's mind raced. The auditor's manual. The Reckoning statutes. The ward trade regulations, all controlled by House Corrin. *Cost transfer is involuntary. Wards are contracted, consenting absorbers. The system provides sanctioned channels for — *

"This isn't sanctioned," he said.

"No."

"You're not a ward."

"No."

"I'm not a ward."

"No."

The wind howled above them, indifferent. Somewhere below, a bird cried.

Kael looked at his arm. The mark was gone, sunk deep. But he felt it now, a phantom weight in the bone, a debt incurred without contract. He was an auditor of the Reckoning. His duty was to observe, to record, to enforce the law. The law said cost transfer without ward contract was illegal. The law said he must report it.

The law also said House Vane's claim was fraudulent, their Kindling unregistered, their lineage-house forfeit.

He had come here to verify the shear zone. He had found something that would destroy them both if it reached the wrong ears.

"Can you climb?" Sol asked quietly.

He realized she had been watching his face. "Yes."

"Then climb. The ledge above is stable. We'll reassess from there."

She moved first, ascending with the same fluid certainty. Kael followed, his right arm weak, his fingers clumsy on the holds. Every movement sent the phantom ache spiraling up his shoulder. He focused on the rhythm: hand, foot, hand, foot. The rock was solid. The rock was real.

They emerged onto the upper terrace where the Vane gardens had once bloomed. Now only hardy weeds grew in the cracked planters. Sol walked to the balustrade and leaned on it, her back to the Reach, facing the cliff face they had just climbed.

Kael joined her. His board was somewhere below, abandoned in the chimney. He would need to retrieve it. He would need to file the emergency directive. He would need to decide what to write in the report about the Kindling flare, about the transfer, about the mark on his arm that was not a ward mark and never would be.

"The shear is worse than the survey," she said. "The upper block has shifted three millimeters since autumn. At this rate, the face fails before the spring equinox."

"Then the emergency directive stands."

"Corrin will appeal. They'll drag it through the courts until the block falls. Then they'll claim the terraces as salvage."

"Salvage law requires abandonment."

"House Vane hasn't abandoned anything. But we can't prove the acceleration without the core sample. Which requires the drill. Which requires the permit."

The circle. It closed every time.

Kael pulled his stylus from his belt. He had a spare notebook in his pack. He wrote: *Shear zone critical. Kindling intervention by subject Sol Vane. Involuntary cost transfer to auditor Kael Ashworth. Magnitude reduced by subject's training. Recipient not a contracted ward. Illegal under Reckoning Statute 47.1.*

His hand shook. The letters wavered.

"What are you writing?" Sol asked.

"The truth."

"All of it?"

He paused. The wind tugged at the page. "The shear zone. The acceleration. The binder failure. The emergency directive."

"And the transfer?"

He looked at her. Her face was expressionless, but her hands gripped the balustrade until the knuckles whitened. "That goes in my private log. Not the official report."

"Why?"

"Because if it goes in the official report, House Corrin gets the legal pretext to seize the lineage-house tomorrow. They'll claim Kindling misuse. They'll claim Vane fraud. They'll install a Corrin ward to 'monitor' you, and that ward will bleed you dry inside a year."

"And if you don't report it?"

"Then I'm complicit in a statutory violation. My commission is forfeit. My house pays the penalty."

She turned. The wind whipped her hair loose from its knot. "So you'll sit on it. Like you sat on the proof about my mother."

Kael went cold. "What proof?"

"Don't." Her voice sharpened. "Don't play the neutral auditor with me. You've had the Corrin ledgers for weeks. The payments to the autumn surveyor. The wire transfers from Corrin's ward-trade account to the Reckoning clerk who filed the stability certification. You know they engineered the failure. You know my mother didn't fail because she was weak. She failed because the system was rigged."

He said nothing. The silence stretched, filled only by wind and the distant cry of gulls.

"When were you going to tell me?" she asked. "At the Reckoning? After the verdict? Never?"

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Then you're a worse liar than I thought." She pushed off the balustrade. "The inspection is done. The directive will be filed. You'll write your private log. And we'll both pretend this didn't happen."

"Sol — "

"Don't." She walked toward the stair, her boots loud on the stone. "Just don't."

He watched her go. The phantom ache in his arm pulsed in time with his heartbeat. In his pocket, the private log pressed against his thigh, its pages waiting for ink that would condemn him either way.

Above them, the cliff face held. For now.

But the shear zone breathed, and the Kindling slept in Sol's wrists, and somewhere in the Corrin aerie, a ledger balanced on the edge of exposure. Kael touched his forearm, feeling for a mark that no longer showed, and wondered how many years he had just lost to a woman he was sworn to ruin.
