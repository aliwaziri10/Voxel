The moon hung low over Thornmere Reach, a pale coin dropped into dark water. Sol crouched on the outer ledge of House Vane's crumbling facade, her fingers finding holds in mortar that had not felt a hand in generations. The wind tasted of salt and iron. Somewhere below, the tide slapped against the cliff base, a steady rhythm that matched the pulse in her ears.

She had not come out for the view.

The ledger sat in her pack, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. Three months of Kindling drills recorded in her own cipher. Three months of waking with her skin cold and her joints stiff, the years burning clean off her bones in increments she could almost measure. She had gotten the cost down to six days per flight. Six days. Her mother had never managed less than twelve.

A loose stone shifted under her boot. She froze, weight settling into the balls of her feet, the Kindling rising in her chest like heat behind a dam.

The enforcers moved like shadows given weight. Three of them, cloaked in the matte gray of House Corrin's private guard. No house sigils. No declared writ. They came from the stairwell that serviced the abandoned watchtower two hundred feet north, crossing the connecting bridge with the silence of men who had done this before.

Sol did not reach for her blade. She reached for the wind.

The Kindling surged. Her shoulders unfolded, not wings — never wings, the gift did not work that way — but something older, a membrane of force that caught the updraft and snapped her upward. The ledge fell away. The enforcers shouted. Gray cloaks billowed as they leapt after her, grappling hooks biting into stone.

She banked hard toward the Spine, the central arterial ledge that stitched the city's vertical neighborhoods together. Her lungs burned. The drain hummed in her veins, a familiar violin string pulled tight. Six days. Maybe seven. The count began the moment her feet left stone.

Behind her, the enforcers gained. Corrin bought the best hooks. The best rope. The best men who asked no questions.

Ahead, the Spine yawned — a thoroughfare wide enough for four carts abreast, carved from the cliff face itself. Lanterns guttered in their brackets. Late pedestrians scattered as she dropped onto the flagstones, boots skidding on moss-slick stone. She ran. The Kindling folded back into her ribs, the cost settling into her marrow like frost.

"Stop! In the name of the Accord!"

The voice came from above. Not behind. *Above.*

Sol skidded, pivoting. Kael Ashworth stood on the balcony of House Corrin's southern wing, his auditor's badge catching moonlight. His coat whipped in the wind. His face was a mask she had learned to read in fragments — jaw tight, eyes tracking the enforcers behind her, one hand resting on the hilt of his sword.

He was not supposed to be here. His rounds took him to the Lower Spiral this week. She knew because she had memorized his schedule.

The enforcers did not slow. They saw the badge. They saw the sword. They came on.

Kael moved. Not away. *Down.*

He vaulted the balcony rail, dropping twenty feet to a decorative corbel, then another fifteen to the Spine's upper tier. He landed in a crouch, sword already drawn, and the enforcers' lead runner impaled himself on the blade with a sound like wet canvas tearing.

The other two did not hesitate. They split, flanking.

Sol's breath came hard. The Kindling still sang in her blood, the afterburn of the flight eating at her. She drew her knife, the one with the serrated edge for rope and the straight edge for flesh. Her back found the cliff wall. Kael stood three paces to her left, blade high, weight balanced on the balls of his feet.

"Vane," he said. His voice cut the wind. "You were seen leaving your ledge at midnight. Care to explain?"

"Corrin enforcers," she said. "No writ. No sigil. You know what that means."

"I know what I see." His eyes flicked to her pack, then back to her face. "Three graycloaks. One down. Two armed with hooked axes. You have a knife and a gift you're not supposed to use."

"I have a knife and a gift I *do* use. The distinction matters."

The enforcers attacked together.

Steel rang. Sol ducked a sweeping hook, drove her knife into the gap between greave and cuirass. The man grunted, stumbled. Kael parried the other's overhead chop, riposted with a thrust that took the enforcer through the throat. The body collapsed. Silence rushed back in, broken only by ragged breathing and the distant tide.

Kael wiped his blade on the dead man's cloak. "Two. Clean."

"Three," Sol said. She pointed up.

The third enforcer had not fallen. He clung to the Spine's upper retaining wall, one-handed, the other hand raising a crossbow. The bolt flew.

Sol moved. Not with the Kindling — no time, no space, the drain too close already. She moved with muscle and desperation, throwing herself sideways into Kael's space.

The bolt struck the stone where his throat had been. A shard of rock exploded upward, jagged and fast.

It caught them both.

Sol felt the line of fire across her cheekbone, hot and sudden. Kael made a sound, short and sharp, and his hand came up to his forearm. Blood welled between his fingers, bright in the moonlight.

They stared at each other. The same shard. The same stone. Blood mingling on the flagstones between them.

"Shallow," Kael said. He let out a breath. "Just shallow."

Sol touched her cheek. Her fingers came away red. "Yes."

The Kindling's hum changed pitch. Deeper. Lower. A second heartbeat beneath her own, slow and deliberate, counting down days she could not spare. She felt it in her fingertips, in the hollows of her collarbones, in the roots of her teeth. Six days. Seven. The cost of the flight, settling.

Kael swayed. Just slightly. His free hand pressed against the cliff wall, fingers spreading on cold stone. His pupils dilated. His breathing slowed, then quickened, out of rhythm with his exertion.

*He feels it,* she realized. *The echo. The transfer.*

The enforcers' bodies lay cooling. The crossbowman had fallen when his shot missed, a broken heap on the lower tier. No more threats. No more witnesses.

Kael straightened. His face had gone pale, the freckles across his nose standing stark. He looked at his forearm, at the cut already clotting, then at Sol's cheek.

"The transfer," he said. Quiet. "It's not just years. It's... sensation. Memory. You feel it too."

Sol said nothing. Her throat worked. The Kindling drained her, a slow tide pulling at the shore of her life. She had learned to measure it in heartbeats. One hundred and forty beats per day. She had counted.

"Your turn," Kael said. "Explain. Now. Why Corrin enforcers with no writ. Why midnight flight. Why you have a ledger in your pack that I know contains Kindling data because I watched you write in it three nights ago from the tower across the way."

Sol's hand went to her pack. "You watched me."

"I watch many things. Auditor's privilege." His voice hardened. "Sol. *Isolde.* You are under active investigation. Your house's claim hangs on the Reckoning. Every use of the Kindling without a contracted ward is a violation. Every day you burn brings you closer to the same end as your mother."

The words struck like a physical blow. Sol's vision tunneled. The Kindling's hum spiked, a sudden crescendo that made her knees weak.

"My mother *had* a ward," she said. The words came out strangled. "House Corrin *sold* her a ward. Then they recalled him three days before the Reckoning. They *knew*."

Kael went still. The wind tugged at his coat. His hand on the cliff wall tightened, knuckles white.

"That is a serious accusation," he said. "Without evidence, it is treasonous speech."

"The evidence is in the ward contracts. The ones Corrin holds. The ones no one else sees." She stepped closer, invading his space, ignoring the pain in her cheek and the fire in her veins. "You're an auditor. You have the right to request those contracts. You have the *duty*."

"I have requested them. Three times. Corrin claims clerical delay."

"Then go again. Go with me. I know where they're kept."

Kael looked at her. Really looked. The mask cracked, just at the edges — exhaustion, something raw and old, a grief he carried like a second badge.

"You are asking me to commit professional suicide," he said. "To breach the neutrality that protects your house's claim. If I am compromised, the Reckoning defaults to Corrin's petition. Vane falls."

"Vane falls *anyway* if you do nothing."

"Then I will find another way."

"There is no other way." The Kindling's drain pulsed, a reminder. *Six days. Seven. Count them.* "You felt the transfer. You know what it means. I cannot control who pays. But I *can* control how much. And every day I burn is a day I lose. My mother lost thirty years in her final flight. She was forty-two. She looked seventy."

Kael's jaw worked. "I know the records. I read the file."

"Then you know I'm telling the truth."

A long silence. The tide turned below, a new rhythm. Somewhere in the city, a bell tolled the hour — two past midnight.

Kael sheathed his sword. The sound was final. "I will request the contracts again tomorrow. Formally. In writing. With the Reckoning Council as witness."

"And if they refuse?"

"Then I will do what I must." His eyes met hers, steady now. "But you — you will not fly again until this is resolved. Do you understand? No more midnight runs. No more burning years on ledgers. The next time you leave the ground, it will be with a contracted ward or not at all."

Sol's laugh was brittle. "And if Corrin refuses every ward? If the trade is closed to House Vane?"

"Then we find a way." He reached out, stopped, let his hand fall. "Go inside. Clean the cut. Record the cost. Honestly."

"I always record honestly."

"Then you know what six days looks like on the page. You know what it means for the Reckoning."

She did. Six days was nothing. Six days was everything. The Reckoning was forty-three days away. She had maybe two hundred left in her, if she was careful. If she didn't fly. If she didn't fight.

If she didn't *live.*

"Tomorrow," she said. "You request the contracts. I'll be watching."

"I know."

He turned. Walked toward the stairwell that led to the auditor's lodge. His step was even. His shoulders were square. But she saw the way his left hand pressed against his right forearm, covering the shallow cut. Saw the way his breathing hitched, just once, when the wind shifted.

The echo. The second heartbeat. He carried her cost now, a fragment of it, lodged in his flesh like a splinter of glass.

Sol watched until he disappeared into the stairwell. Then she touched her cheek again. The cut had stopped bleeding. The Kindling's hum settled into a low drone, patient and inevitable.

She opened her pack. Pulled out the ledger. Found a fresh page.

*19 Emberfall. Flight: 4 minutes. Cost: 6 days. Transfer event: 1 (auditor K. Ashworth, proximal). Notes: Corrin enforcers, no writ. Kael Ashworth intervened. Crossbow bolt deflected. Shared shrapnel wound. Transfer confirmed.*

She wrote it in her cipher. Closed the book. Stood.

The wind had shifted. It carried a new scent — smoke, and something sweet, and the unmistakable ozone tang of Kindling use on a scale she had never felt before.

Not from her.

From somewhere deep in the city, below the Spine, below the Lower Spiral, a bloom of pale light erupted. It rose like a pillar, silent and terrible, painting the cliff face in shades of ghosts.

Sol's blood ran cold.

That was not a single flight. That was not a ward's controlled burn.

That was a *lot* of years burning at once. And the only thing that could cause that —

The Reckoning grounds. The testing spire.

But the Reckoning was forty-three days away.

Unless it wasn't.

Unless someone had moved the date.

Sol's fingers tightened on the ledger. The Kindling screamed in her veins, a sudden wild thing demanding release, demanding *flight*, demanding she reach that light before it consumed whoever stood in it.

She took one step toward the stairwell. Then another.

The open question hung in the air behind her, unspoken but loud as the bell that had tolled midnight: *Who just used the Kindling on the testing spire, and why did the Reckoning come early?*
