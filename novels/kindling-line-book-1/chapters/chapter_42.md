The courtroom in the Spire's heart smelled of old ink and cooler stone. Sol had not expected the scent to ground her, but it did. She stood at the rail where defendants had stood for generations, her fingers curled around the worn wood, and watched the senior auditors file in. Their robes caught the light from the high windows. Dust motes drifted through beams that had illuminated a hundred judgments before this one.

Kael entered last. He wore the plain gray of an independent auditor, no house sigil at his throat, no gold braid on his cuffs. The badge on his chest caught the light differently than the old one had. Same metal, the smith had told her. Melted down and cast again. She had watched the pouring in the yard behind the guildhall, the way the silver-gold alloy swallowed the fire and gave it back as something new.

"House Vane," the presiding auditor said. Her voice carried without amplification. "The claim of lineage-right and territorial autonomy has been reviewed under the Reckoning Accord. The evidence presented by House Corrin regarding the failure of the previous Reckoning has been found to consist of falsified ledger entries, coerced ward testimony, and a deliberate concealment of the Kindling mechanics governing cost-transfer thresholds."

Sol's breath caught. Beside her, her uncle made a small sound, half cough and half laugh.

"Accordingly," the presiding auditor continued, "the claim is upheld. House Vane retains its lineage-house on the western face. Autonomy is restored subject to a provisional oversight period of five years, during which an independent auditor will review all Kindling use, ward contracts, and financial records quarterly. The auditor assigned to this duty will be Kael Ashworth, reinstated to independent status effective today."

Kael did not look at her. He stood with his hands clasped behind his back, his face the mask he had worn for months. Only the faint tension at his jaw suggested the cost of stillness.

"The ward trade," the presiding auditor said, and the chamber shifted. Chairs scraped. Breath held. "Will be placed under the authority of a provisional oversight board. Its charter has been drafted by representatives of House Vane, House Ashworth, House Corrin, and the Guild of Auditors. Signatories will affix their seals before this session adjourns."

A murmur rose. The presiding auditor waited. When silence returned, she nodded to the clerk.

The charter was read in sections. Sol tracked each clause. Ward contracts must be voluntary, witnessed, and registered within forty-eight hours. No contract may exceed three years without renewal. Cost-transfer thresholds must be disclosed to both parties before signing. A ward may terminate at any time with thirty days' notice, and the house holding the contract must provide transition support. Penalties for coercion or falsification include forfeiture of lineage-rights and criminal referral.

House Corrin's representative signed with a flourish that tried to disguise reluctance. His seal pressed deep into the wax. Sol watched the impression form, the crest of the climbing ivy distorted by pressure.

When the clerk turned to her, Sol rose. Her legs held. She walked to the table, took the pen, and signed *Isolde Vane* in the space beneath the final clause. The ink was dark. Her hand did not shake.

Kael signed last. *Kael Ashworth, Independent Auditor.* The badge on his chest caught the light as he bent over the page. The new casting. The same metal.

Afterward, they stood together on the balcony overlooking the Reach. The wind came up the cliff face, cold and clean. Below, the lineage-houses clung to the rock like barnacles, their lights kindling one by one as the day faded.

"Five years," Kael said. "Quarterly reviews. I'll be the one climbing your stairs every three months."

"Could be worse," Sol said. "Could be my uncle."

He smiled then, a flash of teeth in the gathering dark. "Your uncle would miss half the infractions. He doesn't know the ledger codes."

"Neither do you, technically. You're independent now. No house codes to fall back on."

"I'll learn them. Or I'll make new ones." He turned toward her. The wind tugged at his collar. "The badge was forged from the one I surrendered when I was suspended. The smith said the impurities burned out in the melt. What came back was purer."

"Is that a metaphor?"

"Maybe. The guildmaster handed it to me herself. She said the metal remembers its shape even when you think it's lost." He touched the badge. "I've been thinking about what you said. About the cost transfer. About how much burns and who pays."

Sol looked at the ridge of his jaw, the line of his throat. The marks were hidden beneath his collar, but she knew their map. She had watched them form during the Reckoning, the silver threads spreading across his skin where the Kindling had taken years he had not agreed to give. She had not chosen him. The mechanic had. She had only managed to limit the bleed.

"The oversight board," she said. "It's provisional. Five years. After that?"

"After that it becomes permanent, or it dissolves, or the houses rewrite it again. That's how law works. It's not a solution. It's a framework for the next fight."

"Corrin signed."

"Corrin signed because the alternative was having his ward contracts audited by the Guild before the ink dried on the Reckoning verdict. He bought time. He didn't surrender."

"Did we win?"

Kael was quiet long enough that she thought he might not answer. The wind carried the distant sound of a bell from the lower tier, marking the hour.

"We survived," he said. "The claim stands. The house stands. You're still here. I'm still here. That's not nothing."

"It's not everything either."

"No. But it's a place to start."

He reached out. His fingers brushed her wrist, light as the dust motes in the courtroom. Contact. She held her breath, waiting for the familiar heat, the drain, the involuntary pull that had marked every touch since the first time she had burned near him.

Nothing came. No heat. No silver thread. No years slipping away into the space between them.

She looked at his hand on her wrist. At her own fingers, pale against his cuff. The Kindling was quiet. Her control, earned in the crucible of the Reckoning, held.

"Five years," she said. "Quarterly reviews. You'll have to write reports."

"Detailed ones. With footnotes."

"And you'll have to climb the western face. The stairs are treacherous in winter."

"I've climbed worse."

"Have you."

"Once. In the dark. With a broken rib." His thumb traced the pulse at her wrist. "I'd do it again."

The bell struck again. Somewhere in the Reach, a door slammed. A ward's lantern flickered in a window three houses down, a small gold spark against the stone.

"The board meets for the first time next month," Kael said. "Corrin's representative will try to gut the disclosure clause. Ashworth's will push for stricter termination penalties. Yours will argue for transition support funding. Mine will try to keep the peace."

"Yours?"

"I'm the independent auditor. I don't get a representative. I *am* the representative. For the wards."

Sol turned her hand, palm up. His fingers curled around hers. Still no heat. Still no cost.

"Then you'll be busy," she said.

"Busy is good. Busy means I'm not thinking about other things."

"Like what?"

"Like whether the metal really does remember its shape. Or whether we just tell ourselves it does because the alternative is admitting we've been changed into something we don't recognize."

The wind shifted. It carried the smell of rain, and below them the Reach seemed to breathe, houses expanding and contracting with the lives inside them. Wards and holders. Auditors and accused. People who had signed contracts and people who had never been given the choice.

"Kael," she said.

"Yeah."

"If the board works. If the contracts change. If the wards get real protection." She swallowed. "What happens to us?"

He was quiet. The darkness deepened around them. The first drops of rain struck the balcony stones, dark spots spreading on gray.

"I don't know," he said. "But I'll be at your stairs in three months. With a ledger. And a pen. And whatever the metal remembers."

She squeezed his hand. He squeezed back. The rain fell harder, washing the dust from the railings, from the stones, from the air between them.

The charter lay signed in the courtroom below. The badge rested on his chest, new-forged from old metal. The Kindling slept in her veins, held by control she had bled to earn.

Above them, the Spire's light swept the cliff face, steady as a heartbeat.
