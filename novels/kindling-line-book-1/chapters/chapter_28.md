The frost on the council chamber windows had formed feathers overnight, each barb a perfect crystal spine. Kael traced one with his fingertip, the cold biting through the thin skin of his knuckle. The antechamber smelled of ink and old parchment and the sharp mineral tang of the heating pipes that ran behind the wainscoting. Three days since the Ember Festival. Three days since the ward named Mara had died on the Obsidian Terrace in front of three thousand witnesses, while Kael stood six feet away with the proof of Corrin's engineering folded in his coat pocket.

The door opened behind him. He did not turn.

"The emergency session begins in ten minutes." Valerius Ashworth's voice carried a flatness that told Kael he had rehearsed this conversation. "The gallery is full. Corrin has brought witnesses up from the lower tiers."

Kael's hand dropped from the window. "I'll be there."

"You've read the order they mean to move?"

"Twice."

"And?"

"It's what we knew it would be." Kael turned. His father stood in the doorway, the silver thread in his dark coat catching the weak morning light, the Ashworth hawk pinned at his collar. "Corrin is calling it the Emergency Safety Levy. Every ward contract in the Reach, repriced twenty percent, effective the day the Festival went wrong. The name alone tells you everything."

Valerius stepped inside and closed the door. The silence that followed was heavier than the frost. "What happened on the terrace was regrettable. No one disputes that. But Corrin has the votes. They had them before the Festival. This session only makes it lawful."

"The price was not inevitable. It was posted nine days ago, on three houses, with an arrears clock that started before anyone had seen a bill. You saw the ledgers." Kael kept his voice level. "A ward died because the line was strained past its rating, and the line was strained because Corrin cut the rotation to save coin. Now they will charge everyone for the funeral."

"House Vane's finances are not the auditor's concern."

"They are when the auditor's delay costs a ward her life."

The word hung between them. Valerius's jaw tightened. "You are the auditor, Kael. You are also my son. If you speak in that chamber today, you speak as an Ashworth challenging the ruling coalition, and the consequences will not stop at your career."

"I know the consequences."

"Do you? The ledgers you keep mentioning show Corrin's hand in the rotation. They also show Ashworth loans behind Corrin's eastern contracts. Loans your grandfather authorized. Loans I renewed." Valerius moved closer and lowered his voice. "If the Levy falls, the loans are called. Ashworth liquidity collapses. The house falls. Is that what you want? Is that what the ward's death is worth to you?"

Kael looked at his father. This was the man who had taught him to read a ledger before he could read poetry. The man who had sat the dais at Sol's mother's Reckoning while Kael, ten years old, watched from the gallery. "Her death isn't worth anything. It's a fact. The Levy is a lie. And you are asking me to choose between the house and the truth."

"I am asking you to understand that truth without power changes nothing. The Reckoning is eighteen days away. House Vane's claim will be heard. Sol Vane will stand on that platform and either fly or fall. Your job, your only job, is to make sure the process is fair. Not to burn the process down."

A gavel sounded somewhere below and echoed up through the stone. A summons.

"I'll be there," Kael said again. He brushed past his father, the wool of his coat catching on the doorframe. In his pocket the folded proof pressed against his thigh: the ledger page showing Corrin's bribe to the previous auditor, the memorandum with Valerius's own signature approving the falsified Reckoning. He had carried them for three weeks. He had carried them while Mara died.

The gallery was full. Representatives from every house crowded the stone benches, their sigils a blur of color against the grey walls. Corrin's green and gold dominated the front rows. Ashworth grey clustered near the back. Vane black was absent entirely. Sol would not come to this, not while the wound was fresh.

At the dais, Lord Corrin rose. He was a large man, his face smoothed by wealth and the kind of power that never needed to raise its voice. A ward stood beside him, a young woman with the faint shimmer at her temples, her contract plain at her wrist.

"Esteemed auditors, honored representatives," Corrin began, and his voice carried without effort. "Three days ago we watched a tragedy on the Obsidian Terrace. A ward, a contracted and consenting servant of the system, gave her life because the line could not hold. The Safety Levy before you does not assign blame. It funds the repair. It raises every contract by a fifth, so that no house will again be tempted to run a line thin. It also creates a standing deposit, payable by the holding house, against any ward lost in a public ceremony."

Murmurs rippled through the gallery. Kael gripped the railing.

"The deposit falls due within fourteen days," Corrin went on, with a slight smile. "Houses unable to pay will be assessed against their Kindling claim."

The gallery did not protest. It calculated. Kael watched faces move from grief to arithmetic in the space of a heartbeat. A house that could not pay the deposit lost its claim. Three houses were already behind. One of them was Vane.

Beside him a Corrin representative leaned toward an Ashworth cousin. "The deposit alone doubles our western portfolio. If we move the contracts before the Reckoning..."

"Quiet," the Ashworth hissed. "The auditor is two seats down."

Kael did not turn. On the far wall the clerks had projected the order's text in precise script. *Section 4: Standing Deposit. Section 7: Retroactive Assessment. Section 12: Auditor Certification.*

Section 12. The auditor's signature required on every assessment. Kael's signature, in time. Valerius's signature, now, as senior advisor to the Accord.

The posted price. The cut rotation. The loan ledgers. Every step deliberate, every step building toward the moment the Festival's dead became a line item.

Valerius rose at the advisors' bench. The chamber stilled.

"House Ashworth supports the principle of ward protection," he said, in the calm, resonant voice of a man who had practiced the speech. "We support a repair fund. We do not support an assessment that runs backward from the day of the tragedy. It punishes houses for a failure that was not theirs. We propose an amendment: the arrears clock is extended from fourteen days to thirty."

Corrin's smile did not waver. "A reasonable amendment. The Council has the advisor's thanks for his diligence. The amendment is accepted. The order proceeds to a vote."

The gallery exhaled. Kael's hands were white on the railing. The amendment changed nothing. Thirty days from the Festival ran out nine days after the Reckoning. Vane would be assessed the moment its claim was decided, win or lose. The machinery had just been greased.

The vote was unanimous.

***

Sol stood on the Vane ledge, three hundred feet above the canyon floor, the wind tearing at her coat. The Kindling sat cold in her chest, a dormant weight she had learned to measure in heartbeats and breath. Below her, the Reckoning platform was going up on the central spire, scaffolding rising like bones against the sky. Eighteen days.

She had not slept in thirty hours. Not since the terrace. Not since she had crossed the stone in one stride and landed beside a dying woman, close enough to see the ward's face twist as the cost went looking for a body to land in.

The ledge door opened behind her. She did not turn.

"Sol." Pell's voice was rough with exhaustion. "The order passed. Unanimous. Ashworth proposed an amendment and Corrin took it like a gift."

Her fingers curled around the cold iron railing. "What did the amendment do?"

"Stretched the arrears clock from fourteen days to thirty." A pause. "It's theater. You know it's theater."

"I know." Her voice was flat. "Did you read Section 4?"

"The deposit. Yes." The old steward came to stand beside her, his shoulder brushing hers. "They have been building toward this since before the Festival. The price posting wasn't about profit, not only profit. It was leverage. Push the small houses behind, then offer to take their claims in lieu."

"We couldn't afford a ward at the old prices. We can't afford the deposit." Sol's breath clouded. "My mother was sold a signature and told it was a ward. The cost landed on her and there was nothing behind the paper to catch it. And now Corrin has made that a rule for everyone."

"Sol..."

"Don't." She turned, her face pale in the frost light. "Don't tell me it isn't personal. Don't tell me the system is broken. It is working exactly as built. The wards burn so the houses don't have to. The auditors sign the papers. Corrin counts the money. And Kael Ashworth sits in that gallery with the proof in his pocket and does nothing."

Pell went still. "You know about the proof."

"I know there is proof. I know he has had it for weeks. I know he has not given it to me." She laughed, a short sharp sound. "I also know what I saw on that terrace. The cost went into him. He has the marks on both arms and he stood in front of the Council yesterday and called it physics."

The wind howled between them, carrying the distant sound of hammers on the Reckoning platform.

"The transfer is involuntary," Pell said carefully. "You've told me a hundred times. If he was the nearest..."

"Then he paid for something he did not choose. Which makes it worse." Her hand pressed against her chest, over the Kindling's cold weight. "He took the cost that should have been spread across twelve wards, because someone built a line to fail, and he knew it, and he let me walk out there anyway."

Pell was quiet for a long moment. "The Reckoning is eighteen days away. You need to train. You need to eat. You need to decide what you are going to do about Kael Ashworth, because whatever he is and whatever he knows, he is the auditor of record for Vane's claim. He will stand on that platform with you. You cannot change that by staring at scaffolding."

Sol looked at him. Really looked. The exhaustion carved into his face. The tremor in his hands he thought she couldn't see. He had been running Vane's accounts and her training schedule and the fight with the arrears since the terrace.

"You're right," she said. "I'm sorry. You're carrying everything and I am..."

"Standing on a ledge. Which is your right, and the only one any of us has left." He managed something close to a smile. "Come inside. There's tea. It's terrible. I kept back a bottle of the good whiskey from your father's cellar. We can pretend it is medicine."

Sol almost laughed. "Does the whiskey help with the Kindling?"

"Nothing helps with the Kindling. The whiskey just makes the pretending easier."

She followed him inside, the ledge door shutting behind them with a heavy thud. The Vane common room was cold, the fire down to embers. Pell poured tea into chipped mugs and produced the whiskey from a cabinet behind a stack of account books. The amber caught the lamplight.

"To the ward," Pell said, raising his mug.

"To Mara," Sol said. They drank. The whiskey burned clean and hot, a different fire entirely.

"The posting that started all this," Pell said after a moment. "The letters from Corrin's factor to the eastern supply houses. They are dated. The first one went out the week Kael Ashworth filed his opening audit of our books."

Sol's mug stopped halfway to her lips. "He filed without a word about it."

"He filed what the Accord required him to file. I am not saying he is Corrin's. I am saying the dates are inconvenient. And I am saying that if he has real proof, the kind that brings down a levy, he has had weeks to use it. He has not."

Sol set the mug down. Her hands were steady. "He is an Ashworth. His father runs the house. His father signed the loans."

"His father is also the advisor who sat the dais at your mother's Reckoning." Pell met her eyes. "The one who ruled against her."

The room seemed to contract. The fire popped, a spark spiraling up the chimney. Her Kindling stirred, a slow heat behind her ribs.

"Valerius Ashworth ruled against my mother," she said. "Kael's father. The man who taught Kael to read ledgers. And Kael has the proof and hasn't used it."

"Sol..."

"Find out what he has." Her voice was strange in her own ears. Calm. Cold. "Pell. The posting letters. The loan ledgers. The opening audit notes. Everything we can lay our hands on. Put it in a packet. Seal it. Give it to me."

"If this breaks..."

"If it breaks, it breaks. The Levy passed today. Vane will be assessed nine days after the Reckoning, win or lose. And the auditor for our claim is Kael Ashworth. He will stand on that platform. He will sign the papers. If he has known since before the posting, then he chose the house over Mara. He chose the order over the truth. He chose his father over..."

She stopped. The Kindling pulsed, a sudden sharp heat that made her vision swim.

"Over what?" Pell asked softly.

Over me, she thought. Over the one person who might have mattered.

But she did not say it. She never said it. The words burned in her throat like the Kindling itself, like a cost she could not control and could not direct and could only watch consume whoever stood closest when the fire came.

"Get the packet," she said again. "Tonight, before the courier leaves for the lower tiers. I will read it into the record before the first witness is sworn if I have to."

Pell studied her face. The firelight caught the exhaustion, the determination, the fear she could not quite hide. "And if the proof brings down the Ashworth house? If Kael loses everything?"

Sol's hand found the mug again. The whiskey was gone. She had drunk it without noticing.

"Then he loses everything," she said. "The way Mara lost everything. The way my mother lost everything. The way I will lose everything in eighteen days if I can't control the burn."

She stood, the mug clicking against the table. The Kindling settled into a slow banked heat.

"Eighteen days," she said. "Train me. Every hour. Every technique. Push me until I break, and then push me again."

Pell rose too, slower, his joints popping. "I will get the packet. You get some sleep. Real sleep. That is an order, Sol."

"Since when do you give orders?"

"Since you stopped giving them to yourself."

She walked toward the sleeping alcove, the stone floor cold through her boots. Behind her Pell moved toward the desk where the account books waited, the bottle glinting in the lamplight. The ledge door opened and closed. The wind howled.

Sol lay down on the narrow bed and pulled the wool blankets to her chin. The Kindling beat in her chest, a second heart. Eighteen days. The packet. The Levy. The auditor who stood nearest while a ward died.

She closed her eyes. The frost feathers on the window grew longer in the dark.
