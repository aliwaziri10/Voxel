<!-- chapter_date: 26 Frostveil, Year 3 of the Reckoning Accord -->

The wind came off the Grey Spine at dawn, cold enough to crack the skin on Sol's knuckles. She stood on the western ledge of House Vane's aerie, the Reckoning crystal cold against her palm where she had wedged it into the mortar crack. Three days. Seventy-two hours before the auditor's bench would fill, before the lineage-houses would send their witnesses to the central plaza, before Corrin's law would either break or harden into something permanent.

Kael approached from the stairwell, his breath visible in the air. He carried a satchel slung across his chest, the leather worn at the corners. His auditor's sigil had been scratched from the clasp. A deliberate choice, or maybe just the only leather he had left after House Ashworth froze his accounts.

"You're up early," he said.

"Couldn't sleep." She didn't turn. The crystal caught the first light, throwing fractured rainbows across the stone. "The projection geometry doesn't work from this angle. The crystal's facet structure scatters the light sideways. We need a prism array."

"We have three shards from the old chandelier in the great hall. My mother's. She had them taken down when the ward trade prices spiked." He set the satchel down, unbuckled it with fingers that moved carefully. "They're flawed. Air bubbles in the glass. But they'll refract if we angle them right."

Sol turned then. The dark circles under his eyes had deepened since the hearing at the Hall of Contracts. Since he had stood before the Conclave and named his father's name alongside Corrin's. Since House Ashworth had declared him cast off, the words formal and final, delivered by a clerk who would not meet his gaze.

"Show me," she said.

He withdrew the shards, each wrapped in oilcloth. The largest was the length of her thumb, milky with trapped air. The second, a sliver no wider than a fingernail. The third, a jagged wedge that caught the light and held it.

"The central crystal sits on a rotating base," Kael said, arranging them on the ledge stone. "The auditor turns it to face each house in sequence. If we mount the array on the base's underside, the projection will sweep with it. Every house sees the ledger. No one can claim they weren't shown."

"The rotation speed is fixed?"

"One revolution per minute. The mechanism is geared to the water clock in the tower. We can't change it without triggering the tamper wards."

"Then we time the activation." Sol picked up the largest shard, turning it in her fingers. "The ledger projection needs to hold for at least three full rotations. Long enough for the scribes to copy. Long enough that denying it becomes useless."

"Three minutes." Kael's voice was flat. "The crystal's charge will last four, maybe five, if the shards don't bleed off too much luminance. I calculated the decay rate last night. The air bubbles scatter twelve percent per pass."

"Can you improve it?"

"I can try." He didn't meet her eyes. "If I recut the facets. But I'd need a lapidary's tools, and the only set in Thornmere belongs to House Corrin."

A silence stretched between them, filled only by the wind and the distant cry of a cliff-hawk. The stone beneath their feet was pitted with centuries of weather, its surface darkened in a crescent near the mortar crack where Sol had set the crystal. She knew that stain. Everyone in House Vane knew it. The ward's blood had soaked deep before the rain could wash it clean.

"The ward from the hearing," she said quietly. "The one who died in the plaza. Her name was Mira."

Kael went still. "I know her name."

"She was twenty-three. She had a sister in the lower terraces. A mother who takes in mending." Sol's thumb traced the dark crescent. "Her contract was held by Corrin's factor house. Three years remaining. She absorbed a Kindling surge meant for a Corrin scion during the demonstration. The transfer took forty years from her in seconds. She didn't scream. She just... stopped breathing."

The words hung in the cold air. Kael's hands, resting on the ledge near the shards, clenched into fists.

"I didn't know her name," he said. "At the hearing, I only knew the case number. V-447. The auditor's log doesn't record names. Only costs transferred and contracts fulfilled."

"She had a name." Sol's voice hardened. "She had a life. And she died because I used the Kindling near her, and you knew the transfer was coming, and neither of us could stop it."

"Sol."

"No." She faced him fully now, the crystal forgotten in her grip. "Don't soften it. That's what we're doing here. We're standing on the stone where she died, planning to project a ledger that proves what killed her was engineered. The timing has to be perfect because if it isn't, her death means nothing. Her sister's grief means nothing. Every ward who ever signed a contract in desperation means nothing."

Kael was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "You're right. I knew the mechanic. I knew the transfer was involuntary. I knew Corrin's factors positioned wards in the front ranks of every public Kindling use. And I delayed. I hid the proof. I let the hearing proceed." He exhaled, a cloud of white in the grey light. "I can't undo that. I can only make sure it counts for something now."

The admission hung between them, raw and unvarnished. No excuses. No deflections. This was the repair, she realized. Not forgiveness. Not forgetting. Just the willingness to stand in the truth of it, together, and move forward anyway.

"Three days," she said. "The Reckoning begins at noon on the twenty-ninth. The crystal will be brought to the plaza at first light. The auditor's bench is assembled the night before."

"Which means we have tonight and tomorrow night to mount the array." Kael picked up the largest shard, turning it toward the dawn. "The base is stored in the Hall of Contracts' undercroft. Under guard. Corrin's men."

"Then we don't go through the guard."

"There's a maintenance shaft. Runs from the undercroft to the plaza's drainage culvert. Narrow. Damp. The grating at the plaza end is rusted shut, but the hinges are iron. Iron yields to heat."

Sol's fingers tightened on the crystal. "You want me to melt the grating."

"I want you to consider it. The Kindling can cut iron. You've done it before. In the chase across the ledges." He finally met her eyes. "But the cost. The transfer. If someone is near you when you do it..."

"I'll go alone."

"Sol."

"I'll go alone." She said it flat, final. "There's no one else to send. You're the auditor. You have to be on the bench. If you're not there, Corrin's replacement reads the verdict before we can project anything."

"Then I'll be on the bench. And you'll be in a drainage culvert melting iron with your life force." Something flickered across his face. "This is insane."

"Insane is letting Corrin win because we were too careful." She set the crystal down carefully, aligning it with the mortar crack. "The array goes on tonight. We test the projection tomorrow. The day after, we rehearse the activation sequence. Three minutes. Three rotations. That's all we get."

Kael studied her face. The wind tugged at his hair, dark strands escaping the tie at his nape. He looked exhausted, and frightened, and determined in equal measure. She knew the look. She saw it in her own reflection every morning.

"Tonight, then," he said. "I'll bring the tools from the workshop. The lapidary's files are in my quarters. Corrin's men didn't think to seize them."

"They didn't think you'd need them."

"They didn't think I'd fight." A ghost of a smile, bitter and brief. "They were wrong about that."

He began wrapping the shards again, movements precise. Sol watched his hands, the way his fingers found the edges of the oilcloth without looking. The same hands that had held the proof in the Hall of Contracts, that had signed the accusation against his own father, that had brushed hers on the stairwell three nights ago when neither of them could sleep.

The repair wasn't grand gestures. It was this. The quiet work of building something together from the pieces they had left.

"Kael."

He paused, the satchel half-buckled.

"The stone. The stain." She gestured to the dark crescent. "When we rehearse the timing. We'll be standing here. Our hands will touch this stone. The same stone."

He looked down at the ledge, at the pitted surface where the wind had worn away everything but the deepest shadow. "I know."

"It matters," she said. "That we acknowledge it. That we don't pretend the cost wasn't paid."

A long breath. His shoulders dropped, just slightly. "Yes. It matters."

He finished with the satchel, rose in a single fluid motion. The auditor's grace, she thought. The training that never fully left, even cast off and alone.

"Tonight," he repeated. "Midnight. The maintenance shaft access is behind the water clock tower. I'll meet you there."

"Midnight."

He nodded once, sharp, and turned toward the stairwell. His footsteps echoed on the stone, fading downward into the house's warm dark.

Sol stood alone on the ledge. The crystal caught the strengthening light, throwing rainbows across the stain. Three days. Seventy-two hours. The Reckoning would come, and the ledger would be shown, and Corrin's law would break or it wouldn't. But tonight, they would mount the array. Tomorrow, they would test the light. And the day after, they would stand on this stone together, hands brushing over the same dark crescent, and activate the crystal that would tell the truth.

She breathed in the cold wind, tasting iron and stone and the sharp edge of possibility.

The Kindling stirred beneath her ribs, a quiet heat that had nothing to do with the sun rising over the Grey Spine. Three days. She would make them count.
