The platform had been built out from House Vane's lowest ledge, a scaffolding of ironwood and rope that shuddered when the wind hit it crosswise. Frostveil morning light caught the salt crust on the railings. Below, the Reach fell away in vertical streets, the lineage-houses stacked like shattered teeth against the cliff face. Smoke from cooking fires drifted upward in lazy ribbons.

Sol stood at the platform's center, her boots braced on the worn planks. She wore the Vane flight-suit, charcoal wool reinforced at the shoulders and wrists, the house sigil stitched in faded silver at her collar. The cold bit at her exposed face, but she had stopped feeling it hours ago. The Kindling sat warm behind her sternum, a coal banked in ash.

"Lady Vane." The voice came from the platform's edge, where the official party stood. "The council is ready."

She turned. Magistrate Harrow climbed the last rung of the access ladder, his robes brushing the rungs. Behind him came two wardens in the gray-and-blue of the Reckoning Office, their badges catching the light. And behind them, Kael Ashworth.

He wore the auditor's coat, charcoal with the Ashworth crest at the cuff. His face was neutral, the mask he wore for official business. Only the tension at his jaw betrayed anything. He had not looked at her since the ladder. He was looking at the crowd.

The platform faced a natural amphitheater carved into the opposing cliff wall. Tiered ledges held perhaps two hundred people. House representatives in their colors. Ward contractors in muted grays. Citizens who had climbed up before dawn for a view. Children perched on parents' shoulders. The air smelled of salt and coal smoke and the sharp ozone that always preceded a Kindling use.

Sol inhaled. The Kindling responded, a flare of heat in her chest. She had trained for this. Three months of dawn sessions on the high ledges, of measured breaths and counted heartbeats, of learning to dam the flood to a trickle. She could hold the burn to a single year now. She had done it six times in private. Seven, if she counted the night on the ledges when Kael had been the one standing close.

She did not count that one. The cost had not been measured. The cost had been him.

"Lady Vane." Harrow's voice carried without shouting. "The Reckoning Office acknowledges your petition for public demonstration of Kindling control. The record shows House Vane's claim rests on your ability to manage the transfer cost within sanctioned limits."

"The record shows that," Sol said. Her voice carried too. She had practiced that as well.

"And the ward?" Harrow gestured to the figure standing at the platform's rear, half-hidden by the warden's bulk.

The ward stepped forward.

She was young. Sol's age, perhaps a year older. Her contractor's coat was the pale gray of House Corrin's ward trade, the stitching precise at the cuffs. A silver band circled her left wrist, the mark of a bonded absorber. Her face was pale, but her chin was lifted. She had volunteered for this. She had been paid for this. The contract specified one year of life, transferred cleanly, witnessed and recorded.

"I am Mareth of House Corrin," the ward said. Her voice did not shake. "I am contracted to absorb the Kindling transfer for Lady Vane's demonstration. The terms are one year, measured and verified."

Sol's fingers curled at her sides. The Kindling pulsed, hungry. It knew a vessel stood near.

"Approach," Harrow said.

Mareth walked to the platform's center. She stopped three paces from Sol. The distance was measured. The wardens had paced it yesterday. Three paces was the standard proximity for a controlled transfer. Close enough for the Kindling to reach. Far enough that the auditor could intervene if the burn exceeded parameters.

Kael had paced it himself. Sol remembered watching him from her window, his stride even, his notebook open. He had not looked up.

"Lady Vane," Harrow said. "Initiate."

Sol closed her eyes. She found the breath pattern. In for four counts. Hold for two. Out for six. The Kindling rose, a tide responding to the moon. She visualized the dam. Stone walls. Iron gates. The years of her life held behind them, and she would release only one. A single year. Measured. Clean.

She opened her eyes.

The Kindling flared.

It was not fire. It was not light. It was a pressure behind her ribs, a gravity that pulled at the air around her. The platform's ropes sang. Frost on the railings sublimated in a hiss. The crowd went silent, the collective held breath of two hundred people.

Mareth stood straight. Her hand rested on the silver band at her wrist.

Sol pushed. *One year. Only one.*

The transfer snapped into existence.

It was not a flow. It was a seizure. The Kindling did not trickle. It *tore*. Sol felt three years ripped from her chest in the space of a heartbeat, three years of memories and mornings and the taste of salt wind on her tongue, and they did not vanish. They slammed across the three paces into the woman standing before her.

Mareth screamed.

The sound was not human. It was the sound of something breaking inside a living body, the sound of joints popping and skin stretching and organs shifting to accommodate a sudden absence. The ward's back arched. Her hands clawed at her chest. The silver band at her wrist flared white-hot, then went dead gray.

Sol staggered. The dam had not held. The Kindling had taken *three*. She had felt it go. She had felt the years vanish from her own span, but the transfer had taken *three* from the ward as well. The mechanics did not work that way. The cost was supposed to be shared. One year from the wielder, one year to the absorber. A clean exchange.

But the Kindling had taken three from Sol and burned three into Mareth.

The ward collapsed to her knees. Her breath came in ragged gasps. Her hair, loosened from its pins, fell across a face suddenly lined at the corners of the eyes, at the mouth, at the forehead. Three years written in flesh in the span of a breath.

The silence shattered.

"Three years," someone whispered from the crowd. "She took three."

"Impossible. The contract specifies one."

"Did you see her face? She aged."

"Another failure. House Vane cannot control it."

"Corrin's wards are supposed to be calibrated."

"Calibrated for what? For murder?"

The whispers rose, a tide of voices. Harrow raised a hand. "Order. The demonstration is not concluded. Wardens, attend the ward."

The two wardens moved forward. One knelt beside Mareth, pressing fingers to her wrist. The other lifted the dead silver band, examining it.

Sol stood frozen. The Kindling settled in her chest, a dormant beast. Three years. She had lost three years. She had *given* three years. The training had failed. The control had shattered. She had not chosen the amount. She had not chosen the target. But the ward had taken three, and the ward was Corrin's, and the crowd was watching.

Kael had not moved. He stood at the platform's edge, his notebook closed in his hand. His face was the auditor's mask. But his eyes were fixed on Mareth's crumpled form, and something in them cracked.

The warden beside the ward spoke. "Pulse rapid. Breathing shallow. The transfer exceeded parameters by a factor of three. She has absorbed three years of life-cost in a single event."

"Record it," Harrow said. His voice was flat. "The demonstration is failed. Lady Vane, you will remain on the platform pending review."

Sol's throat tightened. "The control held in private trials. Seven consecutive demonstrations. One year each. Measured."

"Private trials are not the record," Harrow said. "The public demonstration is the record. And the public demonstration shows a three-year transfer to a contracted ward. The Kindling cannot be safely managed."

The phrase hung in the air. *Cannot be safely managed.* The words that condemned House Vane's claim. The words that would end them.

From the crowd, a woman's voice rose. "My husband was a ward. He absorbed two years in a routine transfer. He died three months later. They said it was natural causes."

Another voice. "My sister was bonded to House Corrin. She came back wrong. Her eyes didn't track. She forgot my name."

"Wards are volunteers. They're paid."

"Paid in coin they don't live to spend."

The murmur swelled. Harrow's face hardened. "Clear the platform. The ward will be transported to the Corrin infirmary. Lady Vane, you are confined to House Vane pending the auditor's report."

Kael moved then. He stepped forward, his boots loud on the planks. "Magistrate. The auditor's report requires the auditor's observation. I have not completed my observation."

"You have seen the transfer exceed parameters," Harrow said. "What further observation is required?"

"The cause." Kael's voice was level. "The Kindling has not previously shown this degree of variance in controlled proximity. The ward's contractor is House Corrin. The calibration of the absorption band is Corrin's responsibility. I require access to the band's specifications and the ward's medical history before filing."

Harrow's eyes narrowed. "You are accusing House Corrin of equipment failure."

"I am noting an anomaly," Kael said. "An auditor notes anomalies. The Reckoning Office demands full data."

A murmur went through the official party. The wardens exchanged a look. The Corrin contractor's band had been certified. The ward's health had been certified. But Kael Ashworth, neutral auditor, son of the late High Auditor Valerius, was demanding the certifications be produced.

Sol watched him. The cold wind whipped his hair across his forehead. He did not look at her. He looked at Harrow. He looked at the wardens. He looked at the silver band lying dead on the platform planks.

And for a moment, she saw the night on the ledges again. The chase across the ice-slick stone. The Kindling flaring in her chest as she leapt the gap between House Vane and House Ashworth's lower terrace. Kael catching her wrist. The transfer snapping across the contact, burning a year from her into him. His scream, swallowed by the wind. The way he had fallen against the rock, gasping, his face gone white and lined in the moonlight.

Mareth's scream had been the same sound.

The wardens were lifting Mareth now, carrying her toward the access ladder. Her hand dangled, the dead band catching the light. Her eyes were open, fixed on nothing. The lines at their corners were deep, three years carved in seconds.

Sol's chest ached. Not the Kindling. Something else.

"Lady Vane." Harrow's voice cut through. "You are confined. Do not attempt the ledges. The wardens will escort you."

She nodded once. The motion cost her nothing. The Kindling was quiet now, sated on three stolen years. She had twenty-seven left, by the last private measure. Twenty-four now. Three gone to the demonstration. Three more to the ward.

The math was simple. The Reckoning was in six weeks. She would need every year she had.

She descended the ladder behind the wardens, her hands steady on the rungs. The crowd parted before her, a sea of faces. Fear. Judgment. Calculation. A few faces she knew. House Vane's few remaining allies. House Corrin's agents, clipboards in hand, already writing.

At the base of the ladder, a hand caught her elbow.

She turned. Kael stood there. He had descended the official ladder, the magistrate's ladder. His coat brushed the salt-stained stone. Up close, the mask was thinner. The skin around his eyes was tight. A muscle jumped at his jaw.

"Sol," he said. Low. Not Lady Vane. Not the auditor's tone.

She did not pull away. "The band was dead before the transfer completed. I felt it. The absorption capacity simply... vanished."

"I saw." His voice was rough. "The certification will show it was functional. Corrin's engineers will testify it was functional. The ward's medical history will show she was healthy."

"Then why did it take three?"

"I don't know." He glanced up at the platform, where Harrow was speaking to the wardens, where the crowd was still murmuring. "But I intend to find out. Before the report is filed."

"The report goes to the Reckoning Office. To your father's old seat."

"To the acting High Auditor. Not my father." The correction was sharp. "And I will file what I observe. Not what is convenient."

She searched his face. The wind carried the scent of the sea, of coal, of the ozone that clung to Kindling use. Three years of her life burned into a stranger. Three years of a stranger's life burned into nothing. The crowd's whispers rising like smoke.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

Kael was silent for a long moment. His thumb brushed her elbow through the wool sleeve. A touch that lasted less than a breath.

"Because the night on the ledges," he said, "you asked me to trust you. And I didn't. And Mareth screamed the same sound I made."

He released her. Turned. Walked toward the magistrate's party, his notebook opening, his pen moving.

Sol stood alone at the base of the platform. The wardens had taken Mareth away. The crowd was dispersing, voices rising in argument, in fear, in the grim satisfaction of those who had predicted failure. House Vane's claim was dead. The Kindling could not be safely managed. The Reckoning would confirm it.

She looked at her hands. The wool was worn at the fingertips. The Kindling slept behind her sternum, a coal buried in ash.

Twenty-four years. Six weeks until the Reckoning.

She began the climb back to her house, up the vertical stair carved into the cliff face, her boots finding the holds she had known since childhood. Behind her, the platform stood empty in the wind, the dead silver band glinting on the planks where it had fallen.
