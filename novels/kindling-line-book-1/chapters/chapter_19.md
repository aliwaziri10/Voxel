The envelope had no seal. No return address. It sat on Sol's desk beneath the cracked windowpane where the wind always found a way through, plain as a laundry list and twice as dangerous.

She had found it tucked into the gap between the doorframe and the lintel when she returned from the morning's climb. The climb had been routine. The envelope was not.

Inside, a single sheet of vellum folded twice. The handwriting was cramped, deliberate, the ink brown with age or iron gall.

*Contract 7-44. Ward ledger, third folio, line twelve. Consent signature forged. Witness marks altered post-signing. Check the ink bleed on the thumbprint. You have three days before the next audit cycle.*

No signature. No cipher. Just the facts, stripped bare.

Sol read it three times. The wind rattled the window. Somewhere below, the great chains of Thornmere Reach groaned as a heavy gondola passed.

Contract 7-44. The number meant nothing to her. But the implication — a forged consent signature on a ward contract — that meant everything. Wards were the backbone of the Kindling system. Contracted, consenting cost-absorbers. Paid. Controlled. The Corrin-controlled trade existed because consent could be documented, verified, audited. If consent could be forged, then the entire ledger was rotten.

She stood, the chair legs scraping stone. The vellum burned cold against her fingertips.

She needed Kael. She hated that she needed Kael.

---

Kael Ashworth sat in the auditor's alcove on the seventh tier, surrounded by stacks of ledgers that smelled of dust and old iron. The morning light cut pale across the desk at a slant. He had been reviewing the quarterly Kindling expenditure reports for House Merren since dawn. The numbers did not balance. They rarely did.

A knock at the doorframe.

He did not look up. "Enter."

Sol Vane stepped inside. She wore climbing leathers stained grey at the cuffs, her hair pulled back in a rough knot. A smudge of graphite marked her left cheekbone. She carried a folded sheet of vellum pinched between two fingers like something poisonous.

Kael set down his pen. The scratch of nib on paper ceased. In the silence, the Reach's chains sang their endless low note.

"Auditor Ashworth." Her voice was flat. Professional. The mask she wore when she needed something from him.

"Lady Vane." He kept his tone level. "This is irregular. My office hours for private petitions are third bell."

"This isn't a petition." She crossed the room in three strides and laid the vellum on his desk atop the Merren ledgers. "It was left at my door. Anonymous. Read it."

Kael unfolded the sheet. His eyes scanned the lines once, twice. The muscle at his jaw tightened almost imperceptibly.

"Contract 7-44," he said. "Ward ledger, third folio, line twelve."

"I don't know the contract number," Sol said. "But if the signature is forged — "

"Then the ward's consent was manufactured." Kael refolded the vellum with precise creases. "And if one contract bears a forged signature, the integrity of the entire ledger is suspect."

"Corrin's ledger."

"All ward contracts route through Corrin administration. Yes."

Sol's hands curled into fists at her sides. "Can you verify it? Today?"

Kael picked up his pen again. He did not look at her. "I can request the original document from the Corrin archives. The request will be logged. It will be processed in the standard queue."

"How long is the standard queue?"

"Four to six weeks. The Reckoning audit cycle has priority. Every available clerk is pulled to Reckoning preparation."

"The Reckoning is in six weeks."

"Five weeks and three days." Kael made a notation in the Merren ledger. "The backlog is not my invention, Lady Vane. It is a structural reality."

"You're the auditor. You have authority to expedite."

"I have authority to request expedited review. The Corrin archivists control the physical documents. They control the queue." He finally looked up. His eyes were the colour of flint. "I will submit the request this afternoon. I will mark it urgent. Beyond that, the timeline belongs to House Corrin."

Sol stood motionless. The wind chose that moment to shriek around the building's corner, a sound like tearing canvas.

"Five weeks," she said quietly. "The Reckoning happens in five weeks. If this contract is genuine — if the forgery is real — then Corrin's entire ward trade could be exposed before the Reckoning convenes. Every claim they've backed. Every auditor they've influenced. Every house they've crushed."

"Including yours."

"Especially mine."

Kael capped his pen. The click sounded loud in the alcove. "I understand the stakes, Lady Vane. I will do what is within my authority. I will not do what is outside it."

"Even if what's within your authority isn't enough?"

"Especially then." He rose, the chair legs silent on the stone. "The law is not a weapon you swing at convenience. It is a framework. If I break framework to chase a result, I become what I'm sworn to audit."

Sol studied his face. The hard line of his mouth. The tension she had learned to read in the set of his shoulders. She had climbed the Reach's sheer faces since childhood. She knew how to read rock for fracture lines. Kael had fracture lines today.

"Fine," she said. "Submit the request. Mark it urgent. I'll expect the reference number by evening."

"You'll have it."

She turned toward the door. Her hand rested on the frame.

"Sol."

She paused. Did not turn.

"My father's name is not on the Corrin ward ledger. Whatever you think you know about House Ashworth's involvement — "

"I didn't say your father's name."

"You didn't have to." Her voice stayed level. "The tip didn't name him either. That's what makes it interesting."

She left before he could respond.

---

The reference number arrived by courier at the ninth bell. Sol broke the wax seal with her thumbnail. REQ-7744-URG. Urgent review request for Contract 7-44, ward ledger third folio line twelve. Submitted by Auditor K. Ashworth, seventh tier. Status: queued.

Queued. Not expedited. Not prioritized. Queued.

She stared at the slip of paper until the characters blurred. The wind had died. The Reach hung silent, holding its breath.

Three days. The note had said three days before the next audit cycle. Three days until something changed. Or until the window closed.

She climbed to the roof of House Vane's lineage-house that evening. The stone was cold beneath her palms. The city spread below her, a vertical maze of torchlight and shadow, the great chains disappearing into the mist above and the dark below. Somewhere in that maze, a ward contract numbered 7-44 slept in an archive. Somewhere, a signature had been forged. Someone had paid the price.

Her mother's face rose unbidden. The way the Kindling had burned her from inside, years stolen in great gulps because no ward stood ready to absorb the cost. House Vane had been too poor. Too proud. Too late.

Sol had spent six years training to reduce the burn. Six years of secret climbs, of measured Kindling use, of learning to hold the fire in her veins until it smoked instead of flared. She had reduced the cost. She had not eliminated it.

And she had never, not once, controlled who paid the remainder.

The stars wheeled overhead. Cold. Indifferent.

She thought of Kael in his alcove, surrounded by ledgers that never balanced. She thought of the way he had said *I will do what is within my authority* — not *I will find the truth*, not *I will help you*. Authority. Framework. The words of a man who had built walls around himself and called them duty.

But he had submitted the request. He had marked it urgent. He had given her a reference number.

It was not enough. It was something.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold stone of the parapet. The Kindling stirred in her blood, a low hum at the base of her skull. Not called. Not wanted. Just present, as it always was, waiting for the moment when flight became necessary and the cost came due.

Three days.

She would wait. She would watch. And if the archive produced nothing, she would find another way.

She always did.

Kael remained in the alcove long after the courier delivered the reference slip. The Merren ledger still did not balance. He had stopped pretending it would.

The anonymous tip troubled him. Not the accusation — accusations were the air auditors breathed. The specificity troubled him. Contract 7-44. Third folio. Line twelve. Ink bleed on the thumbprint. That was not a guess. That was knowledge from inside the archive.

Someone in Corrin administration had sent it. Or someone who had bought access to Corrin administration. The distinction mattered less than the implication: the ward ledger had a leak.

He pulled the Corrin ward index from the shelf. Heavy. Bound in black leather stamped with the Corrin sigil — a coin crossed by a quill. The index listed every active ward contract by number, house, ward name, and consent verification status.

Contract 7-44. House Merren. Ward: Toma Vell. Consent verified: Ninth of Highsummer, Year 2. Witnesses: Corrin Archivist Pell, Notary Heskin.

Kael turned to the third folio. Line twelve.

The entry was clean. Neat script. Thumbprint mark beside the signature. No visible bleed. No alteration marks.

He leaned closer. The lamp light caught the vellum at a slant.

There. A faint halo around the thumbprint. Brown ink feathering into the paper fibres in a way that suggested the print had been pressed onto wet ink — not signed, then printed. Printed, then signed over.

His breath caught.

He had seen this before. Once. In a training case during his auditor apprenticeship. A forger's trick. The thumbprint applied first, the signature drawn over it to create the appearance of simultaneous execution.

But that case had been a minor merchant house. A single contract. This was the Corrin ward ledger. The backbone of the Kindling economy.

If Contract 7-44 was forged, the verification signatures of Archivist Pell and Notary Heskin were either complicit or compromised. And if one contract could be compromised —

Kael closed the index. His hands were steady. They were always steady.

He needed to request the physical contract. The original. Not the index copy. The original would show the ink layers under magnification. It would show whether the witness signatures shared the same ink batch. It would show truth.

He needed to request it through channels. Proper channels. The channels that took four to six weeks.

Unless.

He opened his desk drawer. Beneath the blank request forms lay a different form. Plain vellum. No sigil. A direct appeal to the Chief Auditor — his father — for emergency document seizure under Reckoning Authority Provision Twelve.

He had drafted it three days ago. He had not submitted it.

Provision Twelve allowed an auditor to bypass archive queues when Reckoning integrity was at stake. It required the Chief Auditor's sign-off. It created a record. It created witnesses.

It would put the request on his father's desk. Valerius Ashworth would see the contract number. He would see the ward name. He would ask questions Kael could not answer without exposing Sol.

Kael's fingers brushed the form. The vellum was warm from the drawer's darkness.

*I will do what is within my authority.*

He had told her that. He had meant it.

But authority had limits. And the Reckoning was five weeks away.

He pulled the form out. He picked up his pen. He wrote the date: 28 Emberfall, Year 3.

His hand hovered over the signature line.

The chains groaned below. A gondola passed, its lanterns painting the alcove walls in swinging gold.

Kael signed.

He folded the form. Sealed it with his signet ring. Called for the runner.

"To the Chief Auditor's office. Seventh tier to first. Priority."

The runner nodded and departed.

Kael returned to the Merren ledger. The numbers still did not balance. But the ward index sat open on his desk, page marked, the forged thumbprint waiting under the lamp light like a wound.

He had done what was within his authority. He had used Provision Twelve. He had put his name on a request that would land on his father's desk by morning.

He had not told Sol.

He would not tell Sol. Not yet. Not until he knew what the original contract revealed. Not until he knew whether the leak in Corrin administration was a crack or a flood.

Three days. The tip had said three days.

Kael blew out the lamp. The alcove darkened. The chain-song rose from the deep, steady as a heartbeat.

He would know in three days. One way or another.
