The practice chamber beneath House Vane smelled of cold stone and old iron. Sol had scrubbed the floor herself three days ago, working the grime from between the flagstones until her knuckles bled. She had not told anyone she was down here. The servants knew better than to venture into the sub-levels after dark. Her father slept three floors up, drugged into silence by the tincture the apothecary brought in unmarked bottles. Her brother had not come home in six nights.

She stood at the center of the chamber, feet planted on the worn circle she had chalked onto the floor. The circle was not ritual. It was measurement. She had calculated its radius to the millimeter using the surveyor's chain she had stolen from the steward's office.

In her hand she held the flare-stone, a piece of raw kindling-crystal no larger than a thumb joint. It pulsed with a light that had no color, only weight. The Kindling in her blood answered it. She could feel the hum in her marrow, the familiar ache behind her ribs.

Breathe. Hold. Release.

The first flare erupted from her palm, a silent bloom of force that struck the target board she had mounted on the far wall. The board was layered oak backed with lead sheeting. She had scored it with concentric rings, each marked in charcoal.

The impact left a clean dent in the third ring.

Sol did not move. She waited for the burn.

It came as it always did, a slow unspooling of years from somewhere deep in her chest. Not pain exactly. A subtraction. She had learned to count it in heartbeats. Twelve beats for a full year. Seven for half. She had trained herself to feel the boundary between one year and the next, the moment the debt settled into her bones.

This time the count stopped at seven beats and three pauses.

She exhaled and reached for the slate propped against the wall. Chalk dust coated her fingers. She wrote: *Flare 1 — 0.58 years.*

The numbers blurred. She blinked and wrote it again. *0.58.*

She had done thirty flares in the past hour. The first ten had ranged from 0.72 to 0.85. The middle ten had clustered around 0.65. The last five had all fallen between 0.58 and 0.62.

Progress. Measurable. Real.

She picked up the flare-stone again. The crystal had warmed against her palm, drinking the residue of her gift. It would need to cool before the next session. She set it in the iron bowl beside the slate and walked to the water bucket in the corner. The water was freezing. She drank until her stomach cramped.

Her reflection in the bucket's surface showed a face she barely recognized. Cheekbones sharp enough to cut. Eyes that had not slept properly in weeks. The dark circles beneath them were not exhaustion. They were the shadow of what she had already spent.

She was twenty-three. By the old counts she should have had fifty years ahead of her. By the new counts, the ones she kept in the ledger hidden beneath the loose floorboard in her bedroom, she had perhaps twelve. Thirteen if she pushed the technique further. Fourteen if she never used the Kindling again.

But she would use it. The Reckoning was coming. The date had been fixed since the first snow of Year 1. Forty-three days from now. The auditor would stand in the Great Hall of the Spire and read the findings. House Vane would stand or fall on the strength of its claim.

And Sol would be there. She would fly the ancestral route. She would prove the bloodline still held the sky.

She returned to the center of the circle. The flare-stone had cooled to a dull grey. She lifted it, centered her weight, and began the breathing pattern again.

In for four counts. Hold for six. Out for eight. The rhythm was a cage she had built for the fire in her blood. Each cycle tightened the bars.

The second flare caught the second ring. The burn counted to seven beats even.

*Flare 2 — 0.58 years.*

She wrote it down. Her hand did not shake.

The third flare struck the bullseye. The burn was seven beats, one pause.

*Flare 3 — 0.60 years.*

The fourth. Third ring. Seven beats, two pauses.

*Flare 4 — 0.62 years.*

The fifth. Second ring. Six beats, five pauses.

*Flare 5 — 0.54 years.*

She stared at the number. The lowest yet. Her breath came shallow. The chalk snapped between her fingers.

She had not expected to break the six-tenths barrier this soon. The texts she had stolen from the archive — the ones the Corrin ward-traders had not burned — spoke of masters who spent decades reaching 0.5. The Vane line had never produced a master. Her mother had plateaued at 0.83 and stayed there until the day she flew the Reckoning route and never came back.

Sol picked up the broken chalk. She wrote *0.54* again, larger this time. Underlined it twice.

The promise tasted like iron on her tongue. *Not like her. Not like her. Not like her.*

She had been seven when the Reckoning took her mother. Seven and standing in the crowd with her father's hand crushing her shoulder. The auditor had read the findings in a voice that carried no mercy. House Vane, insufficient ward coverage. House Vane, Kindling expenditure exceeding sustainable threshold. House Vane, claim denied.

The ward-traders had descended like vultures. House Corrin had bought the debt for pennies. They had stripped the lineage-house of its flight-rights, its landing platforms, its very name from the registry. Sol's father had drunk himself into the stone floor. Her brother had vanished into the lower reaches, running messages for the smugglers who moved contraband Kindling through the cliff-face tunnels.

Sol had stayed. She had studied. She had stolen the texts. She had built the practice chamber with her own hands.

And now she had 0.54.

She lifted the flare-stone for the sixth time. The crystal flared in response to her intent, hungry and cold.

She breathed. The rhythm held. The fire in her blood pressed against the bars of the cage.

The flare struck the bullseye dead center.

The burn came slower this time. She counted. Eight beats. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

Thirteen.

Fourteen.

Fifteen.

She gasped, knees buckling. The slate caught her as she fell. The chalk skittered across the floor.

Fifteen beats. One and a quarter years. A single flare had cost her more than the previous five combined.

She lay on the cold stone, gasping, and waited for the subtraction to settle. It did not feel like dying. It felt like waking up smaller than you were when you closed your eyes.

The ledger in her bedroom would need a new entry. *Session 17. 5 flares at mean 0.58. 1 flare at 1.25. Net expenditure: 4.15 years. Remaining estimate: 8.3 years at current rate.*

She had done the math a thousand times. At 0.6 years per flare, the ancestral route required forty-seven flares. Twenty-eight years. She did not have twenty-eight years.

At 0.54, the route required forty-seven flares. Twenty-five years. Still not enough.

She needed 0.4. She needed 0.3. She needed a miracle she had not earned.

But 0.54 was real. She had held it for five flares. She could hold it again. She would hold it until her fingers bled and her heart stopped and the stone forgot her name.

She pushed herself upright. Her hands trembled as she retrieved the chalk. She wrote: *Flare 6 — 1.25 years. Anomaly. Fatigue factor. Recalibrate.*

She would not call it failure. She would not give the word space in her ledger.

The flare-stone sat in its iron bowl, dull and patient. She could end the session here. She should end the session here. The texts were clear: overtraining the Kindling accelerated the burn exponentially. The masters who pushed past their limits had simply vanished, their bodies consumed by the very gift they sought to master.

But the Reckoning was forty-three days away.

She stood. She lifted the stone. She breathed.

The seventh flare hit the third ring. The burn counted to eight beats.

*Flare 7 — 0.67 years.*

The eighth. Second ring. Seven beats, four pauses.

*Flare 8 — 0.62 years.*

The ninth. Bullseye. Seven beats, one pause.

*Flare 9 — 0.60 years.*

The tenth. Bullseye. Seven beats even.

*Flare 10 — 0.58 years.*

She was back in the rhythm. The anomaly had been fatigue. The technique held. The cage held.

She wrote the numbers. Each decimal a vow. Each entry a wall between her and the fate that had taken her mother.

The chamber door creaked.

Sol froze. The chalk stopped mid-stroke. Her heart hammered against ribs that had already paid too dearly for this night.

Footsteps. Slow. Deliberate. Not her father's shuffling gait. Not her brother's light tread.

A man's stride. Heavy on the heel. Confident in the dark.

She did not reach for a weapon. She had none. She had only the flare-stone in her left hand and the Kindling in her blood and the certain knowledge that whoever stood at the top of the stairs was about to become the nearest soul.

The transfer was involuntary. It always had been. It always would be. No technique could redirect it. No ward contract could shield against it unless the ward was bound and consenting and paid. House Vane had never been able to afford a ward. Her mother had flown the Reckoning route with no one to catch the cost but the empty air.

And now someone was descending the stairs into her secret chamber, and if she flared — if she even breathed wrong — the cost would land on them.

The footsteps stopped at the bottom of the stairs. A silhouette filled the doorway, broad-shouldered and still.

"Vane," said a voice she knew from the Great Hall, from the audit hearings, from the nightmares she had not allowed herself to name. "You're burning the midnight oil."

Kael Ashworth stepped into the circle of lantern-light she had hung from the ceiling hooks. His auditor's coat caught the glow, the silver thread of his house insignia catching fire. His eyes found the target board, the slate, the flare-stone in her hand.

They found the numbers written in chalk.

"Point five eight," he said. "Point six. Point five four."

He did not sound surprised. He sounded like a man reading a ledger he had already memorized.

Sol's throat closed. The Kindling in her blood surged, reactive and wild. She felt the flare-stone pulse against her palm, hungry for release.

She did not breathe. She did not move. She stood in the center of her chalk circle with a year and a quarter already stolen from her tonight and the auditor of House Ashworth standing twelve feet away, the nearest soul in the dark.

"Get out," she said. Her voice cracked. "This is private property. You have no warrant."

"I have the Reckoning Accord," he said. "Article Seven, Section Three. Any Kindling use by a claimant house is subject to audit at any hour. You knew that when you stole the texts. You knew that when you built this chamber."

He took a step closer. Eleven feet.

The flare-stone burned cold in her grip. The Kindling pressed against the cage. She could feel the transfer waiting, coiled and patient. If she flared now, he would pay. 0.6 years. 0.58. Whatever her control allowed.

He would pay because he was nearest. Because the Kindling did not ask permission. Because the system had been built this way on purpose, by the ward-traders who profited from the only sanctioned alternative.

Kael Ashworth stood in her practice chamber and looked at her numbers and did not reach for his seal-stamp. Did not call for witnesses. Did not pronounce judgment.

He looked at her. Really looked. His face was pale in the lantern-light, the sharp bones of it shadowed by exhaustion she recognized. He had not been sleeping either.

"You're going to kill yourself," he said quietly. "Before the Reckoning even begins."

Sol's laugh came out jagged. "That's the plan. Fly the route. Prove the claim. Die with the name restored."

"You won't restore the name if you're ash."

"Then I'll be ash with wings. My mother didn't have wings. She had 0.83 and a corrupted ward contract and a Corrin auditor who pocketed the difference."

The silence stretched. The flare-stone pulsed. The Kindling waited.

Kael's jaw tightened. "My father was not the auditor on your mother's case."

The words hit her harder than any flare. She had researched the records. The auditor's name had been redacted. House Corrin had sealed the file. She had assumed — everyone had assumed — that Valerius Ashworth had signed the denial. He had been the ruling auditor at the time. His seal was on every other Reckoning from that year.

But Kael was saying no.

"You're lying," she said. "The records —"

"Were sealed by Corrin. Not by my father. He recused himself. He wrote a dissent. It was buried."

He took another step. Ten feet.

The transfer hummed in her veins. She could feel it measuring the distance. Calculating the cost. Ready to leap.

"Why are you telling me this?" she whispered. "Now. Here. In the dark."

"Because you're burning years you don't have to chase a number that won't save you. Because I read the dissent my father wrote. Because it says the Corrin ward-contract on your mother's flight was falsified. Because it says the cost-transfer that killed her was engineered."

The words fell into the chamber like stones into a well. Sol felt the bottom drop out of her.

"Engineered," she repeated. The word had no weight. No meaning. It simply existed.

"House Corrin controls the ward trade," Kael said. "They control the contracts. They control the auditors who sign them. Your mother's ward was a Corrin ward. The contract had a clause — a trigger clause. If the Kindling expenditure exceeded a threshold, the cost would transfer to the ward. But the threshold was set below what the route required. She was never meant to survive."

Sol's hand convulsed around the flare-stone. The Kindling surged, a wave of cold fire. She felt the transfer activate, felt it reach for the nearest soul —

She threw the flare-stone into the iron bowl. The crystal shattered. The light died.

The transfer collapsed back into her. The burn caught her in the chest, sudden and vicious. She gasped, doubling over, counting the beats. Eight. Nine. Ten.

Kael moved. Fast. He caught her before she hit the floor, his arms warm and solid around shoulders that had not been held in years.

"Breathe," he said. "Breathe, Vane. Count. I know you know how."

She counted. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen.

The subtraction settled. She sagged against him, shaking, her face pressed to the silver thread of his coat.

Thirteen beats. One year and a fraction. Gone.

She had just burned another year to stop herself from burning him.

He knew. He had to know. The transfer was involuntary. The nearest soul paid. She had been the nearest soul to her own flare-stone when she destroyed it. The cost had rebounded.

But if she had flared at him — if she had lost control for even a heartbeat — he would have paid.

He was holding her. He was the nearest soul. And he had not moved away.

"Why didn't you step back?" she whispered into his coat. "When you saw the stone. When you knew what it was. Why didn't you run?"

His hand came up, fingers threading through her hair. The touch was terrifyingly gentle.

"Because then I wouldn't be here," he said. "To tell you the truth. To give you the dissent. To make sure you don't fly into a trap they built twenty years ago."

He pulled back enough to meet her eyes. His own were grey in the lantern-light, shadowed and steady.

"The dissent is in my coat pocket," he said. "The original. My father's hand. I've carried it for three weeks. I haven't reported it. I haven't filed it. I haven't told anyone."

"Why?"

"Because the moment I do, House Corrin knows I have it. They'll bury it again. They'll bury me. And you'll fly the Reckoning with nothing but 0.58 and a prayer."

He reached into his coat. She tensed. He withdrew a folded parchment, wax seal intact, the Ashworth sigil pressed into red.

He held it out to her.

"Take it," he said. "Read it. Burn it if you want. But you needed to know. Before the Reckoning. Before you spend the last of yourself on a lie."

Sol stared at the parchment. Her fingers, chalk-dusted and trembling, did not move.

The Kindling in her blood had gone quiet. The transfer slept. The cage held.

But the nearest soul was still standing in her circle. Still holding the proof that everything she had built her life on was a fabrication. Still looking at her like she was something worth saving instead of something already spent.

She reached for the parchment. Her fingers brushed his. The contact sent a jolt through her that had nothing to do with Kindling and everything to do with the fact that he was warm and alive and twelve feet from the target board where her flares had struck true.

The parchment was heavy in her hand. The wax seal unbroken.

"Forty-three days," she said. "The Reckoning is forty-three days away."

"I know."

"And you're giving me this now. Tonight. After seventeen sessions of watching me burn."

"I wasn't watching," he said. "Tonight was the first time I found you. I followed the Kindling residue. It leaks, Vane. Even at 0.58. It leaks into the stone. The Corrin ward-traders have hounds that can track it. If I found you, they will too."

The words iced her blood. "Hounds."

"Kindling-scent hounds. Bred for it. Corrin uses them to find unregistered practitioners. To find wards who break contract. To find claimants who train without license."

He stepped back. The distance grew. Twelve feet. Thirteen. The transfer dormant in her veins relaxed.

"You need to stop," he said. "The training. The flares. Every use paints a brighter target on this house. On you. On anyone who stands near you."

"And if I stop? I fly the route at 0.83. I burn twenty-eight years I don't have. I die in the sky like my mother."

"Then we find another way."

"There is no other way. The Reckoning requires the flight. The flight requires the Kindling. The Kindling requires the cost."

"Then we change the Reckoning."

She laughed again, broken and raw. "You're an auditor. You uphold the Accord. You don't change it."

"I uphold the law," he corrected. "The Accord is law. But the Corrin ward-trade is corruption. My father's dissent proves it. If we surface it — publicly, formally, with witnesses — the Reckoning board has to investigate. The date moves. The terms change. You get time."

"And you lose everything. Your position. Your house. Your father's legacy."

Kael Ashworth stood in the practice chamber of a fallen house, surrounded by the evidence of a girl burning her future one decimal at a time. The lantern-light caught the exhaustion in the lines around his eyes, the tension in his jaw, the white-knuckle grip he kept on his coat where the dissent rested.

"My father's legacy," he said slowly, "is the truth he wrote down and couldn't speak. My position is a chair I sit in while Corrin pulls the strings. My house — "

He stopped. His gaze dropped to the slate, to the numbers she had written in chalk.

*0.58. 0.60. 0.54. 0.58.*

"My house," he finished, "has forgotten what it was built to protect."

He turned toward the stairs. He paused at the bottom, one hand on the rough stone.

"Vane."

She did not move. The parchment burned cold in her hand.

"Keep the flare-stone in the iron bowl when you're not using it. The residue leaks less that way. And — "

He hesitated. The silence stretched.

"And what?"

"And don't flare when I'm in the room. I'd rather not lose another year to your aim."

He climbed the stairs. The footsteps faded. The door creaked shut.

Sol stood alone in the circle. The lanterns guttered. The target board bore the dents of ten flares. The slate held the numbers that proved she could do what no Vane had done in generations.

She held the dissent that proved her mother had been murdered by contract.

She had 8.3 years left by the generous count. 7.3 by the honest one. The Reckoning was forty-three days away.

She walked to the slate. She picked up the chalk. She wrote a new line at the bottom of the ledger.

*Session 17 complete. Net expenditure: 5.15 years. Anomaly: external presence. Transfer averted. Dissent received. Verification required.*

She set the chalk down. She picked up the parchment. She broke the seal.

The handwriting was elegant, precise, the ink faded but legible. *Ruling Auditor Valerius Ashworth, Dissenting Opinion, Reckoning of House Vane, Year -20. The ward contract presented by House Corrin contains a triggered cost-transfer clause set at 0.75 years per flare. The ancestral route requires a minimum sustained expenditure of 0.80. The claimant was mathematically guaranteed to exceed threshold. This constitutes engineered failure. I refuse to sign.*

Below the text, a signature. *Valerius Ashworth, Ruling Auditor.*

And below that, a second signature, added later in different ink. *Sealed by Order of the Corrin Majority. K. Vane, Witness.*

K. Vane. Her father's initial.

He had witnessed the sealing. He had known. He had let them bury it and drank himself into the floor and never told her.

The parchment trembled in her hands. The Kindling in her blood stirred, waking slow and hungry.

She looked at the stairs where Kael Ashworth had disappeared. She looked at the target board. She looked at the numbers that were her only map out of the dark.

Forty-three days. 7.3 years. A dissent that could change everything or get them both killed.

She folded the parchment carefully. She tucked it into the hidden ledger beneath the floorboard. She climbed the stairs to her bedroom, each step a subtraction she could not afford.

The Reckoning was coming. The ward-traders had hounds. The auditor who had held her while she burned a year to save him was the only person who knew she had the proof.

And somewhere in the dark, the nearest soul was waiting for the next flare.
