The ledger fragment smelled of dust and iron gall ink, the particular sharpness of records kept too long in airless spaces. Sol turned the scrap of vellum between her fingers, the edge frayed where someone had torn it from a larger binding. The script was cramped, the hand of a clerk who had written the same columns until his wrist forgot any other shape.

Three percent. She read the figure again. Thirty percent.

The numbers ran in a steady column down the left side: ward contract fees assessed to House Vane, House Mire, House Pell. Each entry marked with the Corrin seal. Each entry inflated by the same precise margin. Not a clerical error. Not a fluctuation. A system.

Her back pressed against the cold stone of the archive alcove, the narrow stairwell that descended from the Ashworth aerie into the shared records vault. The Reckoning auditors were meant to have access. Kael had signed the chit himself, his signature still wet on the parchment she had carried past the guard at the upper gate. She had waited until the bell struck the sixth hour, until the senior clerks had gone to their evening meal, until the only sound in the vault was the slow drip of water through limestone.

She had not expected to find this. She had come for the flight-log of her mother's final Reckoning, the official record of the Kindling burn that had taken thirty-two years in a single morning. The log was sealed. The archivist had shaken his head and cited precedent. But the ledger fragment had been tucked into the back of a bound volume of ward mortality tables, as if someone had hidden it there and never returned.

Or as if someone had wanted it found.

Sol slipped the fragment into her coat pocket, the vellum crackling against the wool. Her fingers were cold. The archive kept its chill year-round, a preservation measure that left her breath visible in the lantern light. She should take it to the Reckoning court. She should file a formal challenge. She should do the thing that followed procedure and waited for justice to grind its slow course.

Instead she climbed the stairwell toward the Ashworth aerie, the fragment burning against her thigh with every step.

---

Kael sat at the long table in the aerie's receiving room, a stack of audit summaries spread before him. The fire had burned low. Shadows pooled in the corners where the lanterns did not reach. He did not look up when the door opened. He did not need to. The tread on the stone was light, deliberate, the gait of someone who had learned to move without announcing herself.

"Ashworth."

"Vane." His voice was flat. He turned a page. "You're late. The vault closes at the seventh bell."

"It's the sixth."

"The vault closes at the seventh. You were there at the sixth. That makes you late by my accounting." He set the summary aside and folded his hands. "What did you find?"

Sol crossed the room. The floorboards creaked under her weight. She stopped at the table's edge, her shadow falling across his papers. She did not sit. She reached into her coat and drew out the fragment, the vellum stiff in her grip.

She slid it across the table.

It caught on the edge of a summary, turned slightly. Kael did not reach for it immediately. His eyes tracked the movement. His jaw tightened. Then he picked it up, turning it to catch the lantern light.

Sol watched his face. The line of his throat as he swallowed. The muscle jumping at his jaw. The way his eyes moved across the column of figures, once, twice, a third time. He did not meet her gaze. He read the fragment the way he read every document: with the thoroughness of a man who had been trained to find the lie in the truth.

"Thirty percent," he said. The words came quiet. "Across how many houses?"

"Every entry I could see. Vane. Mire. Pell. The fragment's torn. There could be more."

"Could be." He set the fragment down. His fingers rested on the edge, not quite touching the numbers. "This is ward fee inflation. Systematic. Decade-long. If proven." He stopped. "If proven."

"The seal is Corrin. The hand is consistent. The margin is exact." Sol's voice pitched low. "That's not accident. That's policy."

"Policy requires proof. A fragment is not a ledger. A fragment is a piece of a story someone tore out." Kael finally looked at her. His eyes were the color of flint in winter light. "Where did you find it?"

"In the mortality tables. Bound into the back of volume three."

"Volume three covers the years eight through twelve of the Accord." He said it like a citation. "The fragment covers years five through fourteen. Someone moved it."

"Or someone put it there."

"Someone with access. Someone who knew the binding order." Kael's thumb brushed the vellum edge. "The archivist is a Corrin appointee. His deputy is Corrin's cousin. The guard at the upper gate answers to Corrin's steward."

"So we do nothing."

"I didn't say that." He stood. The chair scraped stone. "I said a fragment is not a ledger. We need the full record. The master ledger sits in the Corrin strongroom. It never leaves. It never gets copied. The auditors are given summaries. We verify the summaries against the contracts on file. The contracts match the summaries because the summaries are drawn from the contracts. The ledger is the only place the original numbers live."

"And you've never seen it."

"No auditor has seen the master ledger in sixty years. The Corrin monopoly on ward contracts predates the Accord. The Reckoning court accepts their summaries as prima facie evidence. To challenge the summary, you need the ledger. To see the ledger, you need a court order. To get a court order, you need evidence of fraud. The fragment is evidence of nothing except that someone tore a page from a book."

Sol's hands curled at her sides. "So the system is designed to protect the fraud."

"The system is designed to protect the wards. The contracts are binding. The fees are paid. The wards absorb the Kindling cost. The houses survive their Reckonings. That is the system working." Kael moved to the fireplace, poked the embers. Sparks rose. "What you're describing is a crime that leaves no victim able to testify. The houses pay. The wards absorb. The Corrin coffers swell. Everyone with power gets what they need."

"Except the wards."

"The wards are paid. They consent. They sign the contracts."

"Do they know they're paying thirty percent more than the true cost? Do they know the extra goes to Corrin profit while the houses bleed coin they don't have?" Sol took a step forward. "My mother died because House Vane couldn't afford a ward. Because the fee was inflated. Because Corrin priced us out of the only protection that mattered."

Kael was silent. The fire caught a log, sending a plume of smoke up the chimney. The light shifted across his face, catching the tension at his mouth, the stillness of his shoulders.

"Your mother's Reckoning was twelve years ago," he said. "The fragment covers years five through fourteen. Year five was the first Reckoning under the Accord. The inflation started at the beginning."

"Or before."

"Or before." He turned from the fire. "If this is true, then every house that failed a Reckoning in the last decade failed because they were priced out of a ward they should have been able to afford. Every ward who signed a contract paid more than they should have. Every Corrin ledger entry for ten years is a lie."

"Can you prove it?"

"I can't prove anything with a fragment. I told you." He crossed his arms. "But I can request the master ledger. As auditor for House Vane's claim, I have standing to petition the court for primary source verification. The court rarely grants it. The court almost never grants it. But I can ask."

"And if they say no?"

"Then we find another way." His eyes held hers. "Or we accept that the system works exactly as it was built to work."

Sol studied him. The auditor's neutrality was a mask she had seen slip twice now. Once in the training courtyard when the Kindling had flared and the cost had burned into his shoulder instead of her. Once in the vault beneath the aerie when he had signed her chit without asking why she needed the records. Now a third time, here in the receiving room with a torn piece of vellum between them.

He knew. He had always known. The Corrin monopoly, the ward trade, the quiet extraction of wealth from houses too proud or too poor to protest. He knew because his position required him to know. He knew because his father had been the ruling auditor when the Accord was signed, when the first inflated contracts were filed, when the system had been built.

He knew. And he had not said.

"Why give me the chit?" she asked. "Why let me into the vault?"

Kael did not answer immediately. He picked up the fragment again, turned it over. The back was blank. "You asked for the flight-log. I signed for the flight-log. The vault holds both. I did not know you would find the fragment."

"But you knew it existed."

"I knew there were irregularities. Every auditor knows there are irregularities. We are taught to verify the summaries against the contracts. We are not taught to question the ledger. The ledger is the foundation. You do not dig under the foundation unless you are prepared for the house to fall."

"Is that what this is? The house falling?"

"It could be. If the ledger matches the fragment. If the court accepts the evidence. If the other houses join a challenge. If Corrin doesn't bury it all before the Reckoning." He set the fragment down precisely, aligning its edge with the table grain. "The Reckoning is in six weeks. The court moves slowly. Corrin moves quickly. By the time a petition works through the process, your house's claim will have been decided. The ledger will be irrelevant to the outcome."

"Unless we make it relevant."

"How?"

Sol did not answer. She did not have an answer. She had a fragment, a suspicion, and a Kindling she had trained in secret for twelve years. She had a house that would lose its claim if the Reckoning went against them. She had a mother's ghost in the flight-log she still could not read.

She had Kael Ashworth, standing by the fire with the light catching the silver at his throat, the mark of his office, the weight of a legacy he had inherited and a system he had sworn to uphold.

"Six weeks," she said. "You said six weeks."

"The Reckoning date is fixed. It was fixed in the first chapter of the Accord. Every countdown since has been consistent." His voice carried something she could not name. "Six weeks from today. Emberfall sixteen to Frostfall twenty-eight. Forty-two days."

"Forty-two days to topple a monopoly."

"Forty-two days to find a ledger no one has seen in sixty years." He looked at the fragment. "Or to decide what you're willing to burn to get it."

The words hung between them. The Kindling cost. The involuntary transfer. The night in the training courtyard when she had flared and he had caught the burn meant for her, the years searing into his skin without his consent, without her control. The marks on his shoulder that he had not reported. The compromise he had made in the vault when he signed her chit.

She had not asked him to be her ward. She could not ask. The transfer chose the nearest body. It always chose the nearest body. The only way to protect him was to stay far enough away that the cost could not reach him. The only way to use the Kindling at all was to accept that someone would pay.

"You didn't report it," she said. "The burn. In the courtyard. You didn't report the transfer."

"No."

"Why?"

Kael was quiet. The fire popped. A log shifted. "Because reporting it would have triggered an investigation. An investigation would have found your training. Your training would have been reported as unauthorized Kindling use. Unauthorized use carries a penalty of house forfeiture. Your house is already on the edge. One more violation and the claim is void before the Reckoning begins."

"So you protected the claim."

"I protected the process. The claim stands or falls on its merits at the Reckoning. Not on a procedural violation manufactured by a rival house looking for cause." He met her eyes. "That doesn't mean I approved. It means I chose the lesser corruption."

"Corruption."

"Auditor's discretion. We call it discretion." He almost smiled. "It means I decide which rules to enforce and which to let bend. Every auditor does it. My father did it. His father did it. The system survives because we let it bend. The system breaks when someone pushes until it snaps."

"Corrin is pushing."

"Corrin has been pushing for ten years. They push in increments. Thirty percent on ward fees. A favorable ruling here. A delayed petition there. They push until the houses forget what the true cost was. They push until the wards forget what fair payment looks like. They push until the only people who remember are the ones who can't prove it."

"And you let them."

"I enforce the rules as written. The rules say the summaries are evidence. The rules say the ledger is proprietary. The rules say the auditor verifies, the court decides. I am not the court. I am not the ledger. I am the verification." His voice hardened. "Until I'm not. Until the evidence forces a choice that discretion cannot cover. This fragment." He tapped the vellum. "This fragment might be that choice. If it leads to the ledger. If the ledger matches. If the court accepts it. If."

"A lot of ifs."

"Every case is a lot of ifs. The art is making the ifs line up." He picked up the fragment, held it out to her. "Take it. Keep it hidden. Do not show it to anyone. Not your steward. Not your siblings. Not the court. Not yet. Let me petition for the ledger. Let me work the process. If the court grants it, we have the proof. If the court denies it, we know the process is compromised. Either way, we move from suspicion to knowledge."

"And if Corrin moves faster?"

"Then we improvise." He did not withdraw his hand. "But you have to trust me to work the process. You have to trust that I want the ledger seen as much as you do."

Sol looked at the fragment in his fingers. The cramped script. The Corrin seal. The thirty percent that had bled her house dry and killed her mother and left her training alone in the dark with a gift that burned everyone near her.

She looked at Kael. At the line of his jaw. The tension in his shoulders. The way he held the fragment like it was something fragile, something dangerous, something that could cut both ways.

She did not take it.

"You want me to trust you," she said. "But you haven't told me why you signed the chit. You haven't told me why you didn't report the burn. You haven't told me what you know about my mother's Reckoning that you're not saying."

Kael's hand did not waver. "You haven't told me what you're training for. You haven't told me why you can reduce the burn but not redirect it. You haven't told me what happens when the Kindling takes you all the way."

"Because you already know."

"Because I read the summaries. The summaries don't say what you can do. They say what the Kindling does. They don't say what you've made it do." His fingers tightened on the vellum. "We both have secrets, Vane. The difference is mine are recorded in the archives. Yours are written on your skin."

Sol's hand rose to her collarbone, to the faint tracery of silver lines that marked the Kindling's path. The cost she had burned. The years she had given. The training that had taught her to hold the flame closer to her own bones, to let less of it reach outward, to make the transfer smaller when it came.

She could not make it zero. She could not choose who paid. She could only stand far enough away that no one was near enough to catch it.

Except Kael. Who kept standing near. Who kept signing chits. Who kept reading fragments without meeting her eyes.

"Take it," he said again. "Please."

She took the fragment. The vellum was warm from his fingers. She folded it carefully, precisely, and returned it to her coat pocket. The crackle sounded loud in the quiet room.

"I'll wait for your petition," she said. "But I won't wait forever."

"I wouldn't ask you to."

She turned toward the door. Her hand found the latch. The cold night air hit her face as she opened it, the scent of pine and stone and the distant smoke of the lower forges.

"Vane."

She paused. Did not turn.

"The fragment suggests systemic fraud. If proven, it could topple Corrin's monopoly." His voice followed her into the stairwell. "But proof requires the ledger. The ledger requires the court. The court requires time. We have six weeks. The clock started twelve years ago. It doesn't stop for us."

She closed the door behind her. The stairwell swallowed her footsteps. The fragment pressed against her thigh, a heart beating in paper and ink.

Behind her, in the receiving room, Kael Ashworth stood by the dying fire and read the numbers one more time, his eyes fixed on the column of thieves' arithmetic, his hands steady on a truth he had always known and never spoken.

The ledger fragment suggests systemic fraud that could topple Corrin's monopoly if proven.
