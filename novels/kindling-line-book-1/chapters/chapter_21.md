The wind off the Reach carried salt and the sharp mineral scent of exposed limestone. Sol stood at the edge of the Vane training ledge, her boots braced on the pitted stone, and watched Kael pace the perimeter of the ward-circle he had chalked himself. Three weeks since the hearing in the High Auditor's chamber. Three weeks since he had returned from the Ashworth archive with something folded in his coat pocket that he had not shown her.

He had not mentioned it. He had not mentioned the offer either, the one his uncle's steward had delivered on House Ashworth stationery, dated and sealed. She knew about it only because the steward had misjudged the acoustics of the corridor outside Kael's quarters, and Sol's ears had caught the phrase *rule against the Vane claim* before the door shut.

Kael stopped pacing. He turned, his face pale in the thinning light, and for a moment his eyes held the same flat calculation she had seen in the corridor. Then his expression smoothed into the neutral mask he wore for official business.

"Again," he said. His voice lacked its usual edge. "The cascade sequence. Slow enough that I can track the transfer vector."

Sol did not move. The Kindling stirred in her chest, a low heat behind her sternum that had nothing to do with the cold. She had learned to read the micro-movements of his jaw, the way his left hand drifted toward the inside of his coat where the ledger lived. He had been recording something every night since the archive. She had heard the scratch of his pen through the shared wall of their quarters, steady and relentless.

"Kael." The name came out quieter than she intended. "You've had me run the cascade sequence nine times in four days. My control is not improving because you are not watching. You are waiting."

He did not deny it. The silence stretched, filled only by the cry of a gull riding the updraft along the cliff face. Somewhere below, the lower wards of Thornmere Reach burned their evening fires, the smoke rising in thin ribbons that curled past the lineage-houses like offered prayers.

"I am tracking the data," he said finally. "The transfer efficiency has shifted. Your output is more consistent. The bleed radius has narrowed by point-three meters per flare."

"Because I have been drilling alone before dawn," she said. "While you were in the archive. While you were meeting with your uncle's steward. While you were doing whatever it is you do when you leave this ledge and do not return until the bells ring midnight."

Kael's jaw tightened. The muscle jumped. "The Reckoning is fixed. The date does not move for anyone's convenience. My obligation is to prepare the record. Your obligation is to survive the test. Neither of those obligations requires conversation."

The words were technically true. They were also a wall, built brick by deliberate brick, and she had watched him lay each one since the day he returned from the Ashworth stronghold with ink-stained fingers and a secret he carried like a second heartbeat.

"Then why are you here?" she asked. "Why not send a clerk? Why not file the numbers from your office in the Spire?"

Because you made a promise. The thought rose unbidden, sharp as a blade. Because you stood on this same ledge six weeks ago and let the Kindling burn into your shoulder rather than let me fall. Because the mark on your skin is proof you have already paid a price you never agreed to pay.

Kael's hand pressed flat against his coat, over the place where the ledger rested. Over the place where the transfer mark lay hidden beneath his shirt, a spiral of pale lines that had not existed before the night chase across the Corrin warehouse district.

"Because the Auditor's Office requires direct observation," he said. "Because the Vane claim rests on demonstrated control, and demonstrated control requires a witness who can testify under oath. Because I am the only auditor the High Council will accept for a house with your history."

Each reason was valid. Each reason was a shield. She had spent her life learning to read the spaces between words, the gaps where truth hid behind procedure. The gap yawned wider every day.

"Fine," she said. "The cascade sequence. Again."

She closed her eyes. The Kindling answered the call she had trained into muscle and breath, rising from the deep well of her years like water drawn from a hidden spring. Heat bloomed in her chest, raced down her arms, pooled in her fingertips. The air around her shimmered, the moisture in the wind condensing into tiny crystals that caught the last light.

She released the flare in a controlled spiral, the cascade pattern she had refined through hundreds of solitary dawns. The energy unfurled, a ribbon of golden light that arced toward the target post at the ledge's far edge. The post had been a boundary marker once, before House Vane fell. Now it wore scorch marks in concentric rings, a record of every session.

The flare struck true. The post absorbed the energy with a sound like tearing silk.

Sol gasped. The cost hit her like a fist driven between her ribs, the familiar theft of time. She had learned to measure it in breaths now. This flare had taken perhaps three months. Three months of future mornings, three months of seasons she would not see. A fraction less than last week. A fraction less than the week before.

Progress. Measured in stolen time.

She opened her eyes. Kael stood exactly where he had stood before, his notebook open, his pen moving. He did not look at her. He did not ask if she was well. He recorded the transfer vector, the bleed radius, the output consistency. He recorded the data that would go into the official record, the record that would decide whether House Vane kept its place on the cliff or fell into the sea.

The silence between them was not empty. It was full of all the things he would not say, all the things she could not ask without breaking the fragile truce that let them stand on the same stone.

"Again," he said, not looking up. "The recovery interval is within parameters. We can fit two more before the light fails."

Sol's hands curled into fists at her sides. The Kindling simmered, patient and hungry. She could feel the years draining, a slow leak she had learned to plug with discipline and breath and the sheer bloody-minded refusal to die before her house was restored.

"Two more," she repeated. "Then I drill alone until midnight. As always."

She turned away before he could answer, before she could see if his face held anything besides the mask. The ledge was narrow, bounded on one side by the sheer drop and on the other by the cold wall of the Vane ancestral hall. She walked the length of it, her boots finding the worn grooves where generations of Vane heirs had practiced their forms.

At the far end, she stopped. The wind tugged at her hair, at the hem of her training leathers. Below, the Reach spread out in tiers of light and shadow, the great houses clinging to the rock like barnacles, the bridges spanning the chasms between them. Somewhere in that warren of stone and ambition, House Corrin held the ward contracts that had doomed her mother. Somewhere, her father's name was written in the ledgers of debt that no honest house could ever repay.

She raised her hands. The Kindling rose to meet them, eager and terrible. She shaped the flare, released it, watched it arc into the darkening sky toward no target at all. Just practice. Just waste. Just another few months burned from a life that had never been long enough.

The cost came. She breathed through it, counted the breaths, marked the tally in the mental ledger she kept beside the one Kael wrote in ink.

*Three months. Two months. Four months. One.*

The numbers climbed. The years shrank. She had started this year with perhaps twenty-three years remaining, by the old estimates. She was down to eighteen now, maybe seventeen. Every flare a payment. Every session a small death.

She did not stop until her legs shook and her vision tunneled and the Kindling sputtered like a candle in a draft. Then she slumped against the cold stone, gasping, her palms pressed to the ledge as if she could anchor herself to the rock and refuse to fall.

Above her, the first stars pricked through the violet dark. The Reckoning was coming. The date was fixed. The auditor who held her fate in his pen stood twenty meters away, writing numbers in a book she would never be allowed to read.

She closed her eyes and whispered the only prayer the Vane line had ever known: *Let it be enough. Let me be enough.*

Kael Ashworth sat in the narrow window seat of his assigned chamber, the ledger open on his knee, the scratch of his pen the only sound in the room save the wind. The fire had burned low. He had not bothered to feed it. The cold kept him sharp.

The numbers on the page blurred, then resolved. He had been tracking the transfer efficiency since the first session, since the night on the Corrin warehouse roof when the Kindling had flared between them and the cost had split its terrible path across both their bodies. He had expected a pattern. He had found a catastrophe.

*Session 1: Transfer 12% to auditor. Cost to subject: 2.3 years. Cost to auditor: 0.4 years.*
*Session 7: Transfer 18% to auditor. Cost to subject: 1.8 years. Cost to auditor: 0.6 years.*
*Session 14: Transfer 24% to auditor. Cost to subject: 1.4 years. Cost to auditor: 0.9 years.*
*Session 21: Transfer 31% to auditor. Cost to subject: 1.1 years. Cost to auditor: 1.3 years.*

The percentages were climbing. The auditor's share was accelerating. His control improvements were not reducing the total cost. They were redirecting it. Every fraction of a year she saved burned from his own future instead.

He set the pen down. His hand trembled, barely perceptible. He pressed his palm flat against the page, feeling the paper's texture, the faint ridge of dried ink.

The mark on his shoulder ached. It had ached every day since the warehouse, a dull phantom pain that flared when she used the Kindling nearby, that pulsed in rhythm with her heartbeat when he stood close enough to feel the heat of her skin. The healers had no name for it. The Auditor's Office had no category. The ward contracts specified voluntary absorption, negotiated terms, consent signed in blood and witnessed by a Corrin notary.

This was none of those things. This was theft. This was the Kindling's oldest law: proximity demands payment. The nearest vessel pays. No choice. No contract. No escape.

He had known the theory. Every auditor learned it in the first year of training. The Kindling recognizes no authority but distance. But theory was clean and abstract. Theory did not wake him at night with the phantom sensation of years peeling away, of a future shortening in increments he could not measure but could feel in the cold hollow of his chest.

He picked up the pen again. Turned to a fresh page. Began a new column.

*Personal cost-count. 1 Frostveil, Year 3.*
*Baseline projection (pre-transfer): 42 years remaining.*
*Current projection (adjusted for Sessions 1-21): 38.7 years remaining.*
*Accelerated loss rate: 0.16 years per session.*
*Projected loss by Reckoning (22 sessions remaining): 3.5 years.*
*Total projected auditor cost: 4.3 years.*

The numbers stared back at him, black ink on cream paper, indifferent to the man who wrote them. Four years. Four years of his life, transferred without consent, without contract, without a single word spoken between them about what it meant.

He had not reported it. The Auditor's Code required immediate disclosure of any involuntary transfer. The penalty for concealment was censure, loss of license, possible prosecution under the Reckoning Accord. He had committed his first deliberate violation the night of the warehouse. He had committed his second every day since, when he closed the ledger and said nothing to the High Council, nothing to his uncle, nothing to the Corrin ward-masters who would have paid fortunes for this data.

He had committed his third violation when he took the proof from the Ashworth archive and hid it in his coat instead of delivering it to the Vane girl who deserved to know her mother's failure had been engineered.

The paper in his pocket was crisp, folded into a precise rectangle. Valerius Ashworth's signature on the ruling that had stripped House Vane of its ward contract. A Corrin ledger entry showing the bribe payment, dated three days before the ruling. A memorandum in his father's handwriting: *The Vane claim cannot succeed. Ensure the auditor understands the required outcome.*

His father. Valerius Ashworth. The ruling auditor at the time of Sol's mother's Reckoning. The man who had sat in the high seat and signed the order that sent a woman to her death because House Corrin had paid for the result.

Kael's throat tightened. He had known his father was corrupt. He had known the Ashworth name was built on compromises and quiet deals. He had not known the corruption reached this far, or that the deal had cost the life of a woman whose daughter now stood on a ledge burning her own future to save a house that had already been sold.

The offer from his uncle's steward had arrived two days after the archive. *Rule against the Vane claim. The Ashworth seat on the High Council is yours at the next vacancy. Your father's debts to House Corrin will be settled. Your sister's dowry will be secured. All that is required is the ruling.*

He had not refused. He had not accepted. He had said he would consider it, and the steward had left with the satisfied smile of a man who knew the answer was already decided.

Kael's pen hovered over the ledger. The numbers waited. The Reckoning waited. The girl on the ledge waited, drilling alone in the dark, burning years she could not afford to lose while he sat in a warm room recording the mathematics of her sacrifice.

He wrote one more line.

*Decision point: 1 Frostveil. The proof remains concealed. The offer remains open. The transfer continues. The auditor records.*

He closed the ledger. The fire had died. The room was cold, lit only by starlight through the narrow window. Somewhere on the training ledge, a flare arced into the night, golden and brief. He felt the phantom ache in his shoulder answer it, a sympathetic resonance across the distance.

He stood, crossed to the window, and watched the light fade. The Vane ledge was a darker shadow against the cliff face. He could not see her. He could not see the cost written on her face. He could only see the numbers climbing in his ledger, and the proof burning in his pocket, and the offer waiting on his desk like a contract with the devil.

The wind shifted. The smell of salt grew stronger. Somewhere below, a bell began to toll the midnight hour, its voice carried up the rock face on currents of air that had witnessed a thousand Reckonings and would witness a thousand more.

Kael pressed his forehead to the cold glass. His breath fogged the pane. He did not move. He did not go to her. He did not tell her what he knew. He did not refuse the offer. He did not report the transfer.

He waited. The numbers climbed. The years burned. The ledger recorded.

And the girl on the ledge kept burning, alone in the dark, paying a price she had never agreed to pay for a house that had already been betrayed by the man whose signature now rested in his pocket.
