CHAPTER TEN

Yusuf kept his office the way he kept everything else about himself since taking the council chair permanently: tidy to the point of austerity, nothing on the walls but a county map and a single framed photograph of his mother standing in front of the old grange hall, taken sometime in the seventies when the building still had its original roof. Mara had been in this office a dozen times since the wedding, mostly for water rights disputes and grazing permits, and had never once seen him look as tired as he looked now, listening to her lay out four weeks of research across his desk.

She had brought the drought file, the incident log Priya had started, and a single sheet where Wren had written the decade pattern out in her own careful hand, dates and intervals lined up like the rungs of a ladder. She had not brought Dev's account of Walt Pruitt yet. That still felt too raw, too secondhand, to survive a first telling.

Yusuf read through it twice without speaking, and when he finally looked up, his face had gone carefully neutral in the way Mara recognized from council sessions where he was trying very hard not to let a room see what he actually thought.

"You're telling me," he said slowly, "that you believe Denise Voss."

"I'm telling you what the records show," Mara said. "What they show is a pattern. Seven incidents in four decades, every one on or beside Voss land, spaced almost exactly the same distance apart. I'm telling you a woman lost a memory with no other symptom three weeks ago, clean as a surgical cut, and that the drought file has a name in it that should belong to a dead woman and doesn't."

"You're telling me you believe Denise Voss," Yusuf said again, quieter this time, "did this. All of it. For fifty years."

Priya, standing by the window with her arms crossed, spoke before Mara could answer. "We're telling you the evidence points somewhere, and it keeps pointing the same direction no matter how many times we try to make it point somewhere else. That's not the same as an accusation. It's a reason to look."

"It's Denise." Yusuf said her name like it settled something on its own, like the word itself was a kind of proof. "You understand what you're asking me to do. You're asking me to open a council inquiry into a woman who ran the harvest fundraiser for thirty years running. Who sat with my mother every single night for the last six weeks of her life so I wouldn't have to do it alone, because I was too much of a coward to watch her go. Who has fed, clothed, and buried half this valley at one point or another, without ever once asking for anything back."

The room went quiet. Mara had known there was history there, everyone in the valley had some version of a Denise story, but she hadn't known the shape of this one, and hearing it now made the weight of what she was asking land differently in her chest.

"I didn't know that," she said. "About your mother."

"No reason you would. It's not something I talk about." Yusuf set the papers down and pressed both hands flat against the desk, as if steadying himself against something only he could feel moving. "She held my mother's hand at two in the morning when the pain got bad enough that I couldn't be in the room anymore. She never once made me feel small for leaving. She just stayed, and let me come back when I could, and never mentioned it again after. That's the woman you're asking me to treat as a suspect."

Wren, who had been silent since they'd walked in, finally spoke, her voice steadier than Mara expected. "I'm not asking you to treat her as anything yet. I'm asking you to look at what I found the way you'd look at it if it belonged to anyone else. If this pattern led to a stranger, someone with no history in this valley, would you already be authorizing a look into it?"

"That's not a fair question."

"It's the only fair question," Wren said. "Because right now the only thing standing between this evidence and an actual investigation is that you love her. And I understand that. I'm not trying to take that away from you. But it can't be the reason we stop looking, because if it is, then anyone smart enough to spend fifty years being loved by everyone who might otherwise suspect them has already won before anyone starts."

Caleb, who had been quiet by the door, shifted his weight and finally spoke, his voice even and unhurried in a way that seemed to pull some of the heat out of the room without softening what he had to say. "Yusuf. I've known you a long time. I know you don't make decisions on feeling, even when feeling is loud in the room. That's why you're good at this job. So I'm asking you to do the thing you do best, not the thing that's easiest right now. Look at the dates. Look at the pattern. Tell me honestly if you can explain it away with anything other than coincidence stretched further than coincidence usually stretches."

Yusuf picked the sheet back up, the one with the years written out in Wren's hand, and studied it for a long moment. Mara watched something shift behind his eyes, some internal argument playing out that none of them were allowed to see the terms of.

"Even if I believed every word of this," he said finally, "and I'm not saying I do, you understand what happens the moment I open a formal inquiry. It doesn't stay quiet. It can't. The council has rules for exactly this kind of thing, rules written after what happened with Kell, and every single one of them exists to keep something like that from happening again unchecked. That means notice. That means Denise finds out, possibly within the day, that four people she's fed pie to and comforted at funerals believe she's been stealing pieces of this town's memory for half a century."

"We're not asking for a formal inquiry," Mara said quickly. "Not yet. We're asking you to know. To be with us on this quietly, the way the council was quiet with Kell before there was enough to act on. If it comes to nothing, no harm done, no one outside this room ever needs to know we looked."

"And if it comes to something?"

"Then we'll need you," Mara said. "Because Priya can investigate, and Wren can read, and I can build the pattern until it's airtight, but none of that means anything if it never reaches the council in a form they'll actually act on. You're the only person in this room who can carry it that far."

Yusuf was quiet for a long time, long enough that Mara heard the clock on his wall tick through half a minute, long enough that she started composing, in her head, the argument she'd give if he simply said no.

"I need time," he said finally. "Not to decide whether to help you. To decide whether I can look at her the same way afterward, knowing what I'll be looking for every time she smiles at me across a table. That's not nothing, Mara. That costs something too, even if it's not the kind of cost you can write down in a log."

"Take the time," Caleb said. "We're not asking you to walk into her kitchen tomorrow and accuse her of anything. We're asking you to hold what we've told you, quietly, and let us keep working while you sit with it."

"And if I decide, after sitting with it, that this is exactly the kind of thing that destroys a good woman's life over a coincidence that happens to have bad timing?"

"Then you tell us that," Mara said, "and we listen, because you'll have earned the right to say it by actually looking first instead of refusing to. That's all we're asking. Not agreement. Just a real look."

Yusuf gathered the papers into a single stack, squaring the edges with the same careful precision Mara had watched him bring to every dispute that had ever crossed his desk, and for a moment she thought he might simply hand them back and end the conversation there.

Instead he set them down again, deliberately, on his side of the desk rather than hers.

"I'll keep these," he said. "I'm not agreeing to anything yet. But I'll keep them, and I'll read them again tonight without the four of you standing here watching my face while I do it. That's the most honest thing I can offer you right now."

"That's enough," Mara said, though something in her chest told her it wasn't, not really, not with the interval Priya had warned them was already closing.

They left his office a few minutes later, the four of them stepping back out into the square in a silence that felt different from the silence after Priya's office, heavier, less resolved. Wren was the first to break it, once they were well clear of the council building.

"He's not going to help us," she said. "Not in time to matter."

"You don't know that," Caleb said, though without much force behind it.

"I read rooms for a living, sort of. That wasn't a man deciding whether the evidence is real. That was a man deciding how much of himself he's willing to lose finding out." Wren shoved her hands into her jacket pockets, jaw tight. "I don't blame him for that. I just don't think we can wait on him."

Priya glanced at Mara, some silent question passing between them that Mara understood without needing it spoken aloud. "She's not wrong," Priya said. "We keep building the case regardless. If he comes around, good, we'll have somewhere to bring it. If he doesn't, we still need it airtight enough to go over his head if it comes to that."

"Over the council chair's head," Caleb said. "That's a hell of a sentence to hear out loud."

"It's a hell of a sentence to be living," Mara said, and found, walking back toward the orchard road with the afternoon light slanting gold across the fields, that she believed every word of what she'd just said to Yusuf, and believed, just as strongly, that belief alone had never once been enough to make a hard thing easy.

Behind them, in his office with the door closed, Yusuf sat with four names and seven dates spread out across his desk, and did not, for a long while, pick up the phone he kept glancing toward, the one with Denise's number written on a card taped inside the top drawer for exactly the kind of emergency he had never once imagined would involve her.
