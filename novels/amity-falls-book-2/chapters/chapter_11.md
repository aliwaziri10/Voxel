CHAPTER ELEVEN

Three days passed without a word from Yusuf, and by the second morning Mara had stopped pretending she wasn't watching the road for his truck every time she stepped out onto the porch.

They kept working anyway, because Priya had said to and because the alternative was sitting still, which none of them had the stomach for. The incident log grew a page a day, Priya cross-referencing old dispatch records against Wren's dates, Mara sketching out the property lines around the Voss homestead from the county plat maps until she had a version of the valley in her head that existed nowhere on paper, built entirely from the shape of what kept happening near one particular stretch of land.

It was Caleb who finally said the thing none of them had been willing to say out loud.

"He's not calling because he doesn't want to be the one who says no," Caleb said, over coffee that had gone cold an hour before either of them remembered to drink it. "If he calls and says no, that's a decision he has to live with. If he just doesn't call, he gets to keep telling himself he hasn't decided yet."

"That's not fair to him," Mara said, though she didn't entirely believe it as she said it.

"Maybe not. But it's what people do when a decision costs them something they don't want to pay." Caleb turned his mug in slow half circles on the table, the way he did when he was working through something he didn't like the shape of. "I've done it myself. Put off telling you something true because saying it out loud would make it real in a way that not saying it wouldn't."

Mara looked at him, and something in his face told her this wasn't only about Yusuf anymore. "You're talking about your missing years."

"I'm talking about everything," Caleb said. "But yes. That too." He didn't elaborate further, and Mara didn't push, because there was a difference between asking someone to be honest and dragging honesty out of them before they were ready to give it, and she had learned, slowly and with real effort, which side of that line she wanted to stand on with him.

Wren arrived a little after nine, letting herself in the way she'd taken to doing since the investigation started, no knock, straight through to the kitchen table like she lived there half the time now. She dropped a folder in front of Mara without preamble.

"Priya sent this over. Full incident log, formatted the way she wants it if this ever has to go in front of anyone official." Wren sat down hard, the particular exhaustion of someone who hadn't slept well written plainly across her face. "It's good. It's airtight, as airtight as something like this can be. And it doesn't matter, because Yusuf's the only door that leads anywhere from here, and he's not opening it."

"He might still," Mara said.

"He might. Or he might sit on it until the interval closes and something else happens to someone else, and then we're back to reacting instead of preventing anything." Wren rubbed at her eyes. "I keep thinking about what Priya said. That we're due, or close to it. Every day we spend waiting on Yusuf is a day closer to whoever's next."

Caleb set his mug down. "So what are you suggesting. That we go around him."

"I'm suggesting we stop pretending we need his permission to keep doing exactly what we've already been doing." Wren's voice had an edge to it that Mara recognized, the particular sharpness that came when Wren's patience, never long to begin with, had finally worn through. "We don't need a formal inquiry to watch a woman's property. We don't need the council's blessing to talk to Odette, or to look harder at what happened to Ruth, or to figure out exactly what a stakeout would even look like if it came to that."

"A stakeout." Mara let the word sit in the room a moment. "You're talking about surveilling her."

"I'm talking about watching, carefully, from a distance, the same way Priya watches anyone she's building a case against before she has enough to make an arrest. That's not vigilante justice, Mara. That's exactly the kind of patient, careful work Priya herself told us we needed more of."

"With Priya's blessing," Mara said. "Not without it. And not without at least trying Yusuf one more time first."

Wren looked like she wanted to argue, and then visibly decided against it, some of the sharp edge going out of her posture. "Fine. One more try. But if he stalls again, I want us moving, formal permission or not."

They found Yusuf that afternoon not in his office but out at the grange hall, standing alone in the empty room where his mother's photograph had been taken decades before, looking up at the rafters like he expected to find an answer written somewhere in the old wood.

He didn't turn around when they came in, though he must have heard the door.

"I went to see her," he said, before any of them could speak. "Yesterday. Told myself I was just checking in, the way I check in on half the valley. Sat in her kitchen for an hour."

Mara felt something tighten low in her stomach. "And?"

"And nothing happened. She fed me coffee cake and asked about the council budget and told me a story about my mother I'd never heard before, about the two of them as girls, stealing peaches off the Whitlock orchard and getting caught by old man Whitlock himself." Yusuf finally turned to face them, and his eyes were red in a way that told its own story. "It was warm, and it was kind, and it was exactly the kind of hour I've spent in that kitchen a hundred times before. I sat there the whole time looking for something wrong, and I couldn't find it. Not one thing."

"That doesn't mean there's nothing there," Wren said, gently, for her. "You said yourself, it's surgical. Small. She's not going to hand you a reason to suspect her over coffee cake."

"I know that." Yusuf's voice cracked slightly on the last word, and he pressed his palm flat against the wall for a moment, steadying himself. "I know that, and it doesn't make sitting in that kitchen feeling like a traitor for looking any easier. I kept thinking, the whole time, what if I'm wrong. What if this is nothing but a pattern in old paperwork and a scared young woman's gift making her see danger in every ordinary hesitation. What if I let this destroy a good woman's life for nothing."

"And if you're right," Caleb said quietly. "What if it's not nothing."

Yusuf was silent for a long moment, and when he spoke again his voice had steadied, gone into the careful, deliberate register Mara associated with him chairing a difficult session. "I've decided something. Not everything. Not yet. But something." He looked at each of them in turn. "I'll authorize you to keep looking, quietly, informally, with Priya's oversight and mine. Not a formal inquiry. Not yet. That still notifies her, and I won't risk that until there's something that can't be explained away over coffee cake. But I'll know what you're doing, and I'll help where I can without putting it in front of the full council."

"That's not everything we asked for," Wren said.

"No. It's what I can give you honestly, today, without lying to myself about what I actually believe yet." Yusuf's jaw tightened. "I owe her more than most people in this valley will ever understand. I also owe this valley the truth, whatever it turns out to be. Those two things are fighting each other right now, and I won't pretend the fight is settled just because you need it to be for your own peace of mind."

Mara felt the tension in her shoulders ease, not all the way, but enough. It wasn't the full backing she'd hoped for. It was, she thought, probably the most honest thing Yusuf had it in him to offer, and there was a kind of integrity in that honesty that made her trust it more than she would have trusted an easy yes.

"That's enough to start with," she said. "For now."

Wren didn't look fully satisfied, but she didn't argue either, and Mara caught the look that passed between her and Priya later, once they were walking back through the square, something wordless and determined that told Mara plainly enough that Wren intended to push the pace of this investigation whether Yusuf's caution kept up with her or not.

"He's scared," Wren said finally, once the grange hall was well behind them. "Not of Denise. Of finding out he was wrong to love her as much as he does."

"Aren't we all a little scared of that," Mara said. "Everyone who's ever sat in that kitchen and eaten her pie."

"Yeah." Wren pulled her jacket tighter against the wind picking up off the fields. "That's what makes her so good at this. Everyone's a little bit in love with her. Even the people trying to catch her."

They walked the rest of the way home in silence, the valley settling into its late afternoon quiet around them, and somewhere behind them, in a kitchen that still smelled faintly of coffee cake, a woman who had just spent an hour being watched without once letting it show sat alone at her table, turning a wooden spoon slowly between her fingers, and thought her own careful, patient thoughts about how much longer this particular quiet could possibly last.
