CHAPTER ONE

The letter lived in the top drawer of Mara's dresser for six weeks before she let herself open it again.

She told herself there wasn't any rush. Whatever Adelaide Whitlock had been trying to warn someone about had waited the better part of a century already, folded inside a water-stained envelope at the bottom of an attic ledger, and six more weeks of a new marriage and a valley finally exhaling after its worst year in living memory wasn't going to be the difference between catching something and losing it for good. That was the structural argument, anyway, the one she made to herself most mornings while Caleb slept beside her and the orchard outside their bedroom window held onto the last of its blossoms a week longer than it had any right to.

The honest argument was smaller and less comfortable. She hadn't wanted to know yet. She'd spent eight months learning this valley's worst secret from the inside, watching a man she'd once loved without reservation collapse in on himself in front of the whole town, and she wasn't in any hurry to find out there was another one waiting underneath it.

But Wren didn't share Mara's patience, and by the second week of May she'd stopped pretending to.

"You've read it four times," Wren said, dropping into the chair across from her at the kitchen table on a Tuesday morning that had started, like most of them now, with the two of them working before Caleb was even awake. Wren had let herself in with the spare key she'd been given without much ceremony sometime in April, the kind of quiet trust that got extended in this valley now that used to get withheld. "I know because I found it on your nightstand in March, and it had a different fold pattern than it does now. You keep opening it and closing it and not doing anything with it."

"I'm doing something with it." Mara didn't look up from the county plat map spread across the table, property lines she'd requested under the entirely true pretext of a drainage survey for the co-op. "I'm being careful."

"You're being scared." Wren said it without any heat, which was somehow worse than if she'd meant it as an accusation. Twenty years old now, formally enrolled in an apprenticeship this valley hadn't had a name for a year ago, and she'd gotten disturbingly good at reading people who weren't handing her an object to touch. "Which, fine. I get it. But I found the drought file three weeks ago and you still haven't told Caleb, and I don't think that's carefulness anymore. I think that's avoidance wearing a hard hat."

Mara set down her pencil. "That's an oddly specific metaphor."

"I've been spending time with an engineer." Wren didn't smile, not quite, but something in her face loosened the way it did when she was trying not to. "Show me the file again."

Mara had it in the same folder as the letter now, the two of them travelling together everywhere she went, which was its own kind of tell she wasn't ready to examine. She slid the drought file across the table, the photocopy she'd made at the county records office in April under her own name, because a Voss requesting county documents raised no eyebrows in a town where her grandmother's name was carved into half the load-bearing beams from here to the mill.

Nineteen eighty-one. A drought that had cracked the valley floor in three places and dropped the reservoir low enough to expose a foundation nobody remembered building. Her grandfather's name was in the margin, his careful engineer's hand noting soil composition and water table depth, the same handwriting she'd grown up tracing in old blueprints without understanding, until this year, exactly what he'd actually been investigating underneath the official drainage report.

And near the bottom, in a resident registry nobody but a county clerk or a Finder with too much time would ever think to cross-reference against anything: a name.

Denise Voss. Age recorded as thirty-one.

Mara had done the arithmetic more times than she wanted to admit. If that entry was accurate, if it belonged to the same Denise who'd pressed a slice of pie into her hands at her own wedding four weeks ago, insisting she hadn't eaten all day, then Denise Voss was currently somewhere north of seventy-five years old and looked, to every eye in this valley including Mara's own, like a woman in her late fifties who baked too much and gossiped just enough and had never once, in Mara's entire lifetime of knowing her, seemed like anything other than exactly what she appeared to be.

"It's the surname that gets me," Wren said, tapping the page. "Voss. That's your grandmother's line. You never mentioned a Denise anywhere in the family tree."

"Because I don't think there is one. Not one anybody's talked about in decades, anyway." Mara had spent three nights with her grandmother's old church directory and a legal pad, and the Voss family tree she'd reconstructed had a gap in it right around the 1950s that she hadn't been able to explain with anything other than the kind of quiet erasure this valley specialized in. A sister, maybe. A cousin. Somebody close enough to share the name and distant enough that nobody currently living remembered exactly how.

"So either it's a coincidence," Wren said, "a completely different Denise Voss who happened to live here forty-five years ago and happened to share a surname with your family and happened to disappear from every record since without anyone noticing. Or."

"Or it's not."

They sat with that for a moment, the kitchen quiet except for the tick of the stove clock and, somewhere upstairs, the creak of Caleb finally getting up.

"I don't want it to be her," Mara said, and hadn't quite meant to say it out loud. "That's the part I haven't figured out how to be careful about. Denise brought a casserole to Thomas's funeral. She's the reason half this valley's children learned to swim, teaching lessons at the reservoir every summer for as long as anyone can remember. If this is real, if she's been doing what Adelaide's letter says somebody's still doing, then either she's been lying to this entire town for decades, or she doesn't think of it as lying at all. And I don't know which one of those is worse."

Wren didn't have an answer for that. Neither, if Mara was honest, did she.

Caleb came down a few minutes later, hair still damp from the shower, and stopped in the kitchen doorway the way he'd started doing lately whenever he caught the two of them with the folder open between them, a careful, deliberate pause that Mara had come to recognize as him giving her room to decide how much to say rather than assuming he was owed all of it at once. It was, she'd come to understand over the past eight months, one of the ways he'd learned to love her without the memory he still didn't have back. Patience where certainty used to be.

"Morning," he said, and poured coffee instead of asking, which was its own kind of question.

"We're not ready to tell you everything yet," Mara said, because she'd promised herself months ago she wouldn't do to him what this valley had spent two years doing to both of them, wouldn't let a kindness become a withholding without noticing the difference. "But we're close. Today, maybe. I need one more piece first."

He nodded, accepting it the way he'd learned to accept most things about their new marriage, without demanding the shape of it before she was ready to give it to him. "Whatever it is," he said, "I'd rather know late and accurate than early and wrong. You've earned that much trust from me by now."

It should have made her feel better. Instead it settled into her chest like one more weight she wasn't sure yet how to carry, because the last time somebody in this town had decided to spare someone the whole truth a little longer for their own protection, it had cost a family eight years and nearly cost this one everything.

She looked back down at the drought file, at Denise Voss's careful, decades-old signature on a document that shouldn't have had any reason to still matter, and thought about pie pressed into her hands at her own wedding, and felt the old, familiar hum of unfinished business she'd hoped, four weeks ago under the string lights, might finally leave her alone.

It hadn't. She was starting to understand it probably never fully would, not in a valley built the way this one was, on bargains nobody wrote down and debts nobody remembered taking out.

"One more piece," she said again, mostly to herself this time, and reached for her coat.

The county records office opened at nine. She intended to be there when it did.
