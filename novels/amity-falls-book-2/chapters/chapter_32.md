CHAPTER THIRTY-TWO

By midnight, Denise's kitchen table had disappeared entirely under paper.

Wren's notebook sat at the center of it, pages filling fast with a diagram that looked, Mara thought, uncomfortably like one of her own load charts: Denise's name at the top in a small circled box, lines branching downward toward six other names, each line labeled in Wren's cramped hand with a fraction, a question mark, a guess dressed up as math because none of them had anything better to offer yet.

"If we're guessing at proportions," Priya said, leaning over the page, "shouldn't the split favor whoever's most able to carry it, rather than simply dividing evenly among six people who have wildly different lives waiting for them tomorrow morning?"

"That assumes we understand what 'able to carry it' even means," Caleb said. "Denise has fifty years of practice absorbing grief that isn't hers, and it's still killing her. I don't think willingness is the only variable that matters here."

"It might be the only one that matters at all," Denise said quietly, from where she'd been sitting mostly silent for the last half hour, watching six people argue over the shape of her own burden with an expression Mara couldn't quite read, somewhere between grief and wonder. "I never asked to carry what I carry. Adelaide came to me in the worst week of my life, right after our mother died, and I said yes because I loved her and because I was twenty-eight and certain love alone would be enough to make anything bearable. I think that's part of why it's broken me the way it has. Not the weight itself, but the fact that I never chose it freely, not really, not with my eyes fully open."

The room went quiet at that, and Mara felt the shape of the actual problem shift slightly under her, less an engineering question than she'd let herself believe and more one that would require every bit as much honesty as calculation.

"Then that's the first rule," Mara said slowly. "Whatever this becomes, nobody carries a piece of it who hasn't chosen it with their eyes fully open. No love pressed into an obligation. No one talked into it because the math needs six equal shares instead of five, or four, or however many people actually mean it when they say yes."

"That could leave us short," Yusuf said. "If even one of us hesitates honestly, and I think all of us should be honest enough to say we might."

"Then we're short," Mara said. "I'd rather attempt this with four people who chose it completely than six people where two are only pretending certainty they don't actually feel. Adelaide's mistake wasn't that she only had one person willing. It's that she never asked whether the one person she found had truly chosen it, or simply loved her enough not to say no."

Denise flinched slightly at that, though she didn't argue the point.

"Practically," Priya said, pulling the conversation back toward solid ground, "what does 'carrying a piece of it' actually look like, once we're standing at that well. Is it a ritual. Words. Something physical, the way the well itself seems tied to the mechanism."

"I don't fully know," Denise admitted. "Every renewal I've ever performed followed the same shape, more instinct passed down from Adelaide than anything written or formalized. Standing at the well at dusk. A physical token from the person losing the memory, something small enough to carry meaning, since the well seems to need a focus rather than simply an intention. Speaking the loss plainly, out loud, to acknowledge what's actually being taken rather than letting it happen silently."

"So if we're splitting it instead of taking it," Wren said, working through it as she spoke, "maybe the shape stays the same, but instead of one person absorbing what comes back through the well, several of us stand there together, and each of us takes a portion instead of Denise taking all of it alone."

"That's my best guess," Denise said. "I want to be honest with all of you. It is a guess. I don't know if the well will divide what it releases evenly among however many people are standing there, or whether it will simply try to give the whole of it to whoever it finds easiest to take from, the way water finds the lowest point regardless of how many channels you dig for it."

Mara turned that over carefully, an engineer's instinct for failure modes rising instantly to the front of her thinking. "Then we need a way to make sure it can't simply choose the path of least resistance. If there's a real risk of it concentrating onto one person instead of dividing the way we intend, we need every person standing there to actively hold their portion, not passively wait for the well to sort it out for us."

"Actively how," Caleb asked.

"I don't know yet," Mara admitted, and felt the rare, uncomfortable honesty of standing at the edge of a problem she genuinely couldn't yet solve. "But I know enough about how loads distribute in systems humans build to know that even distribution never happens by accident. It happens because something is specifically designed to force it. If Adelaide's bargain was built as a single point of failure, one person absorbing everything, then splitting it safely probably means building something that actively resists collapsing back down to that same single point the moment it's under stress."

Denise looked at her for a long moment, unmistakable admiration breaking through the exhaustion. "Adelaide would have liked you," she said quietly. "She always said the valley needed more people willing to actually build the solution instead of simply hoping the problem would eventually solve itself."

"Then let's build one," Mara said. "We have, what. Two days left, maybe three, before your reserve runs out entirely."

"Two," Denise said. "I felt it shift again an hour ago. I didn't want to say so in front of all of you until I had to."

The urgency of that landed differently than anything said before it, and Mara watched the room reorganize itself around the number, plans that had felt merely difficult a moment ago suddenly sharpening into something closer to triage.

"Then let's talk about the actual design," Mara said, pulling Wren's notebook toward her and sketching a rough circle with six smaller marks around its edge, the same shorthand she'd have used for a load-bearing ring in any ordinary structure. "If the well tries to find the path of least resistance, we need to make every path equally resistant, so there isn't a single weak point for it to collapse toward. That means whoever stands closest to the well physically can't also be the person offering the least, emotionally, because that combination is exactly the kind of weakness a system finds on its own without anyone intending it."

"So position matters as much as offering," Priya said, following the logic.

"I think it might. I don't know for certain, none of us do, but if I were designing this as a structure instead of guessing at folklore, I'd want the strongest, most certain offerings distributed evenly around the ring rather than clustered together, so no single arc of it is weaker than the rest." Mara looked around the table. "Which means we need to talk honestly, right now, about what each of us is prepared to offer, instead of discovering it for the first time standing at the well tomorrow night."

Caleb was the first to speak into the silence that followed. "I'd offer the two years themselves, if that's even possible. Not take them back exactly, I don't think that's how any of this works, but offer up my own grief over losing them, the two years of trying to live around a hole I never understood, in exchange for helping carry what's already inside her. It feels like the only offering that actually closes the loop instead of just adding a new debt on top of the old one."

"That might be exactly the kind of offering Wren was describing," Denise said softly. "Something that means what it's supposed to mean, rather than something chosen simply because it's available."

Yusuf spoke next, his voice steadier than it had been all night, though something raw still moved underneath it. "There's a shame I've carried since my mother's last six weeks. I found reasons to step outside her room most nights, and left Denise to sit with her instead, and I've never told anyone how much of that was cowardice rather than grief. I'd rather give that shame up for something that actually helps someone than keep hoarding it as a private punishment that's never done anyone any good."

Priya was quiet a moment longer, turning something over before she spoke. "I don't have a grief that clean to offer. I have twenty years of this valley's trust, built one small kindness at a time, every person I've ever helped believing I'd keep them safe. I'd offer the certainty that trust gives me. The comfort of always being the one people call when something's wrong, never the one who needs calling. I think I've hidden behind that certainty more than I've ever let myself admit, and giving some of it up honestly might be exactly the kind of real cost this needs."

Wren, when it came to her, hesitated longer than any of them had. "I don't know what I have that's mine to give yet. Everything I've offered so far has been the read itself, the physical cost of using what I can do. Maybe that's still what this needs from me. Maybe my offering is simply agreeing to read again, at the well, fully this time, no holding back the way I've held back every time since the nosebleed, and letting whatever that costs be the thing I put into the ring."

"That's not the same as the rest of ours," Caleb said, worry plain in his voice. "That's a physical risk, not an emotional one."

"Maybe the ring needs both kinds," Wren said. "Maybe that's exactly why it takes six of us instead of one. Different kinds of weight, distributed instead of matched."

Mara looked around the table at all of them, at the shape of five honest offerings sitting where an hour ago there had only been guesses, and felt her own fear settle into something steadier despite everything still sitting underneath it. "Then I'll offer the thing I've been most afraid to admit since this whole investigation started," she said quietly. "That some part of me has been grieving Denise this whole time, even while building the case against her. That losing the version of her I grew up loving felt like its own kind of theft, long before I understood what she'd actually taken from anyone else. I'd offer that grief. Not because it cancels out what she's done. Because pretending I never felt it would be its own kind of dishonesty, and this whole plan only works if none of us walks into it pretending."

Denise's composure broke fully at that, silent tears finally spilling over in a way none of her earlier admissions had managed to produce. "I don't deserve that grief," she whispered. "But I am more grateful for it than I have words to say."

Around the table, one by one, they nodded, the shape of the plan still unfinished, still full of guesses dressed up as method, but real now in a way it hadn't been an hour before: an attempt built by people willing to build it, standing between an old woman's fifty years of stolen grief and whatever came next.

"Two days," Wren said, closing her notebook at last. "Let's make them count."
