CHAPTER THIRTY-TWO

By midnight, Denise's kitchen table had disappeared entirely under paper.

Wren's notebook sat at the center of it, pages filling fast with a diagram that looked, Mara thought, uncomfortably like one of her own load charts: Denise's name at the top in a small circled box, lines branching downward toward six other names, each line labeled in Wren's cramped hand with a fraction, a question mark, a guess dressed up as math because none of them had anything better to offer yet.

"If we're guessing at proportions," Priya said, leaning over the page, "shouldn't the split favor whoever's most able to carry it, rather than simply dividing evenly among six people who have wildly different lives waiting for them tomorrow morning?"

"That assumes we understand what 'able to carry it' even means," Caleb said. "Denise has fifty years of practice absorbing grief that isn't hers, and it's still killing her. I don't think willingness is the only variable that matters here."

"It might be the only one that matters at all," Denise said quietly, from where she'd been sitting mostly silent for the last half hour, watching six people argue over the shape of her own burden with an expression Mara couldn't quite read, somewhere between grief and wonder. "I never asked to carry what I carry. Adelaide came to me in the worst week of my life, right after our mother died, and I said yes because I loved her and because I was twenty-eight and certain love alone would be enough to make anything bearable. I think that's part of why it's broken me the way it has. Not the weight itself, but the fact that I never chose it freely, not really, not with my eyes fully open."

The room went quiet at that, and Mara felt the shape of the actual problem shift slightly under her, less an engineering question than she'd let herself believe and more something that would require every bit as much honesty as calculation.

"Then that's the first rule," Mara said slowly. "Whatever this becomes, nobody carries a piece of it who hasn't chosen it with their eyes fully open. No love pressed into an obligation. No one talked into it because the math needs six equal shares instead of five, or four, or however many people actually mean it when they say yes."

"That could leave us short," Yusuf said. "If even one of us hesitates honestly, and I think all of us should be honest enough to say we might."

"Then we're short," Mara said. "I'd rather attempt this with four people who chose it completely than six people where two are only pretending certainty they don't actually feel. Adelaide's mistake wasn't that she only had one person willing. It's that she never asked whether the one person she found had truly chosen it, or simply loved her enough not to say no."

Denise flinched slightly at that, though she didn't argue the point.

"Practically," Priya said, pulling the conversation back toward solid ground, "what does 'carrying a piece of it' actually look like, once we're standing at that well. Is it a ritual. Words. Something physical, the way the well itself seems tied to the mechanism."

"I don't fully know," Denise admitted. "Every renewal I've ever performed followed the same shape, more instinct passed down from Adelaide than anything written or formalized. Standing at the well at dusk. A physical token from the person losing the memory, something small enough to carry meaning, since the well seems to need a focus rather than simply an intention. Speaking the loss plainly, out loud, to acknowledge what's actually being taken rather than letting it happen silently."

"So if we're splitting it instead of taking it," Wren said, working through it as she spoke, "maybe the shape stays the same, but instead of one person absorbing what comes back through the well, several of us stand there together, and each of us takes a portion instead of Denise taking all of it alone."

"That's my best guess," Denise said. "I want to be honest with all of you. It is a guess. I don't know if the well will divide what it releases evenly among however many people are standing there, or whether it will simply try to give the whole of it to whoever it finds easiest to take from, the way water finds the lowest point regardless of how many channels you dig for it."

Mara turned that over carefully, an engineer's instinct for failure modes rising instantly to the front of her thinking. "Then we need a way to make sure it can't simply choose the path of least resistance. If there's a real risk of it concentrating onto one person instead of dividing the way we intend, we need every person standing there to actively hold their portion, not passively wait for the well to sort it out for us."

"Actively how," Caleb asked.

"I don't know yet," Mara admitted, and felt the rare, uncomfortable honesty of standing at the edge of a problem she genuinely couldn't yet solve. "But I know enough about how loads distribute in systems humans build to know that even distribution never happens by accident. It happens because something is specifically designed to force it. If Adelaide's bargain was built as a single point of failure, one person absorbing everything, then splitting it safely probably means building something that actively resists collapsing back down to that same single point the moment it's under stress."

Denise looked at her for a long moment, something like genuine admiration breaking through the exhaustion. "Adelaide would have liked you," she said quietly. "She always said the valley needed more people willing to actually build the solution instead of simply hoping the problem would eventually solve itself."

"Then let's build one," Mara said. "We have, what. Two days left, maybe three, before your reserve runs out entirely."

"Two," Denise said. "I felt it shift again an hour ago. I didn't want to say so in front of all of you until I had to."

The urgency of that landed differently than anything said before it, and Mara watched the room reorganize itself around the number, plans that had felt merely difficult a moment ago suddenly sharpening into something closer to triage.

"Then tomorrow," Priya said, "we bring in whoever's actually willing, ask them plainly, with everything Denise has told us laid out honestly, no softening it to make the yes easier to give. And tomorrow night, or the night after at the very latest, we go to that well and find out whether an old story half-remembered by an old woman's grandmother is enough to actually save anyone."

Around the table, one by one, they nodded, the shape of the plan still unfinished, still full of guesses dressed up as method, but real now in a way it hadn't been an hour before: a genuine attempt, built by people willing to build it, standing between an old woman's fifty years of stolen grief and whatever came next.

"Two days," Wren said, closing her notebook at last. "Let's make them count."
