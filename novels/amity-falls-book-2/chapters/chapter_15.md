CHAPTER FIFTEEN

Priya arrived just after six, still in uniform, and stood over the kitchen table for a long moment without saying anything at all, her eyes moving from the red pins to Mara's handwriting to the stack of index cards squared off at the corner like evidence already prepped for a courtroom that didn't exist yet.

"Four times at the well," she said finally. "Twice at the cellar. Once at the foundation."

"That's what the survey records give us," Mara said. "I checked it twice."

"I believe you checked it twice." Priya pulled out a chair and sat, setting her hat on the table beside her the way she always did when she wanted a room to know she was thinking as a person and not just as a badge. "That's not the part I'm stuck on."

Wren, cross-legged on the counter with a mug of tea gone cold in her hands, tipped her head. "Then what's the part."

"The part where this is still a map, not a warrant." Priya tapped the plat with one finger, careful, like she didn't want to smudge Mara's pins out of place. "I can take a map like this to Yusuf and remind him he already granted us informal backing. I can take it to a judge someday, if it ever gets that far, and it'll help. But it doesn't let me stand in Denise Voss's tree line with binoculars and call that lawful."

"We're not talking about arresting her," Caleb said from the doorway, arms crossed, coffee long since abandoned somewhere behind him. "We're talking about watching a piece of ground."

"I know what we're talking about." Priya's voice stayed even, but there was an edge under it that hadn't been there the last time she'd sat at this table. "I'm the one who has to explain it if it ever goes sideways. Not you, not Mara. Me. If this ever becomes a real case, and I hope to God it does, because it means we actually stopped something, then every step we took to get there gets picked apart by someone whose whole job is finding the crack that lets a woman like Denise walk free."

Mara sat back, arms folded, studying Priya the way she studied a stress point before deciding whether it would hold. "You think we shouldn't watch the property."

"I think I'd be lying if I said the thought of getting this wrong doesn't scare me more than the thought of Denise getting away with it." Priya rubbed her eyes, and for a second the exhaustion under her professionalism showed all the way through. "She sat with half this valley through the worst nights of their lives. If we go after her the wrong way and it turns out we're wrong, or even if we're right and we've botched how we proved it, this town doesn't forgive that. It doesn't forgive us, and it doesn't forgive itself for believing us."

"We're not wrong," Wren said, quieter than her usual bluntness, but with a certainty under it that made both Mara and Caleb look at her. "I felt her hands, Priya. I don't have the face yet, but I felt what those hands were doing, and it wasn't kneading pie dough."

"I'm not saying you're wrong." Priya's tone softened, the way it always did when she talked to Wren, some older instinct in her that had never fully stopped protecting the girl she'd known before any of this started. "I'm saying feeling it isn't the same as being able to stand in front of a judge, or a council, or this whole valley, and prove it in a way that survives someone else trying to tear it apart."

Caleb finally crossed the room and sat, the chair scraping once against the floor. "Then tell us what would survive it. You know this work better than any of us. If watching the property the way we're imagining it isn't clean enough, what is."

Priya was quiet for a long moment, turning her hat slowly on the table by its brim, and Mara recognized the look on her face, the same look Priya had worn in the substation office weeks ago when she'd first agreed to help at all: someone doing the math on how far she was willing to bend a set of rules she genuinely believed in, for a reason she believed in more.

"There's a difference between watching from land you have a right to be on and watching from land you don't," Priya said at last. "The tree line along the county road is public right-of-way. Nobody needs anybody's permission to stand there with a set of eyes open. If something happens on that property that we can see plainly from a place we're lawfully allowed to stand, that's not the same animal as trespassing onto her land to get a better angle."

"So we don't go onto Voss land," Mara said, already reworking the shape of the plan in her head the way she reworked any structure once a load-bearing constraint got handed to her.

"Not unless something's happening in real time that can't wait for me to get a warrant, and even then, God help us if it comes to that." Priya looked up, and the fear in her expression had settled into something harder, more decided. "I'm not telling you no. I watched Ruth Calloway lose the sound of her own husband's voice over one bad night, and I've read enough of Wren's timeline to know Denise has been doing that to somebody in this valley every six to eight years since before either of us were born. I want her stopped as much as anyone in this room. I'm telling you that wanting it isn't enough. It has to survive scrutiny, or all we'll have done is warn her, and give her forty more years to get better at hiding it."

The room went quiet at that, the kind of quiet that came after someone said the true worst version of a plan out loud instead of letting everyone privately fear it alone.

"How much time do we actually have," Caleb asked. "Before the interval closes."

"Weeks, if the pattern holds as tightly as it has." Mara pulled the timeline card toward her, running a finger down the dates one more time as though a fresh count might give her a different, kinder number. "Not months. Not enough time to build something slow and careful from nothing."

"Then we don't build it from nothing," Priya said. "We use what we already have. Yusuf's informal backing covers quiet investigation. I can make a case that a lawful watch from public right-of-way, logged and dated the same way every other piece of this file has been, falls inside that. It's not everything I'd want. It's not nothing either."

Wren slid down off the counter, restless energy finally finding somewhere to go. "So we're doing it."

"We're doing a version of it." Priya held up a hand before Wren could get further ahead of her. "Rotating shifts, from the road, logged times, logged observations, nobody alone, nobody improvising once we're out there. If something happens that looks like a renewal in progress, we do not go closer, we do not confront her, we document it and we bring it straight back here. That's the whole plan until I say otherwise."

"And if it happens somewhere we can't see from the road," Mara said. "The cellar's set back from the property line. The angle might not hold."

Priya didn't answer right away, and the silence told Mara everything the words eventually did. "Then we're limited to the well," Priya said finally. "It's the one structure close enough to the road to actually watch lawfully. If she's using the cellar instead this cycle, we might not see it at all, and that's a risk I can't engineer my way around any more than you can, Mara. Some problems don't have a clean version."

Mara looked back down at the map, at the four red pins clustered around the well and the two around the cellar, and felt the same old frustration she always felt when a structure refused to resolve as cleanly as she wanted it to. Four out of seven wasn't proof of anything. It was a favorite, not a guarantee.

"Then we watch the well," she said. "And we hope the pattern holds one more time before it ever occurs to her that someone's finally paying attention."

"And if it doesn't hold," Wren said. "If she goes to the cellar instead, this whole cycle, and we're standing at the road watching nothing happen at the wrong hole in the ground."

Nobody answered that for a moment, because nobody had an answer that didn't sound like a gamble dressed up as a plan.

"There might be a way to hedge it," Caleb said slowly, working the problem the way Mara recognized from watching him fix fence lines and failing pumps, turning a stuck thing over until some angle of it gave. "What if we're not choosing between the well and the cellar. What if we put someone close enough to see both, even if the angle from the road only gives us the well cleanly."

"There's a rise about two hundred yards past the tree line," Mara said, already picturing the property in her head the way she'd sketched it a dozen times over the last week. "Public land, if the old survey markers are right. It's not as close as the road, but from up there you'd have a sightline onto both the well and the edge of the cellar door, weather permitting."

Priya considered that, turning it over with the same care she'd given everything else tonight. "That's better. If the markers hold and it really is public land, that changes what we can lawfully do up there. I'd still want confirmation on the boundary before anyone sets foot on that rise, because if I'm wrong about where the line actually sits, everything we gather from there becomes worthless the moment a defense attorney finds the error."

"I can pull the deed records tomorrow," Mara said. "I know exactly which office to ask and exactly what to ask for. I've done it enough times this month that the clerk probably thinks I'm buying property."

That got the first real laugh out of Wren in days, small and startled, like she'd forgotten laughing was still something available to her. "Tell her you're building a very elaborate shed."

"Confirm the boundary," Priya said, "and I'll fold the rise into the shift plan. Two vantage points instead of one changes the odds considerably, even if it doesn't fix the underlying problem, which is that we still don't know for certain which structure she'll use this cycle, or if she'll use either of them at all."

"Then we've lost six to eight years," Caleb said quietly. "And somebody else in this valley loses a piece of themselves we could have stopped."

Priya stood, retrieving her hat, settling it back onto her head with the small, deliberate motion of someone putting a version of herself back on along with it. "I'll draw up the shift schedule tonight and bring it by tomorrow. I want eyes on that tree line starting within the week, sooner if any of you notice anything that suggests she's already moving toward it."

"What would that look like," Mara asked. "Moving toward it."

"I don't know yet," Priya admitted, and for the first time since she'd sat down, something almost like fear crossed her face, quick and gone. "That's the part that scares me most. We're about to start watching a woman who's had fifty years to learn exactly how not to be watched, and none of us actually knows what it looks like when she decides it's time."

She let herself out a few minutes later, and Mara stood at the window watching her taillights recede down the dark road, past the fence line, past the orchard, toward the low hill beyond which, somewhere in the gathering dark, an old well sat waiting for whichever night it was that Denise Voss decided, once again, that the valley owed her something only a stranger's grief could pay.
