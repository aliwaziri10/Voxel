CHAPTER TWENTY-EIGHT

Yusuf's office had become, over the course of one long week, something closer to a war room than any of them had originally intended.

Maps covered one wall now, the Voss homestead rendered in careful detail, wells and cellars and property lines all marked in Mara's precise engineering hand, the kind of drawing she usually reserved for load calculations and structural sections, not for a house she'd eaten pie in a dozen times over the years. She'd drawn it twice, actually, the first version torn down and redone after she caught herself sketching the kitchen window a full foot too far to the left, some part of her hand apparently unwilling to trust a memory it now knew had been curated for her comfort. The second version she'd checked against Wren's account of the stakeout, against Caleb's own recollection of the porch steps, cross-referencing until three separate memories agreed closely enough that she trusted the lines.

The inquiry file Yusuf had demanded sat compiled in three thick binders on his desk, letter and drought file and marriage record and stakeout log all assembled into something that could, if it ever needed to, survive a challenge from anyone inclined to doubt it. Mara had spent an entire evening on the tabs alone, color-coded and cross-referenced the way she'd once organized permit paperwork for a bridge inspection, because some old instinct in her refused to let a case this important rest on anything less than the same rigor she'd demand of a structure people were meant to trust their lives to. And beside all of that, a single sheet of paper covered in Wren's cramped handwriting, the only physical trace left of what she'd seen on the pocket watch, since some proof, as Mara had told her that first terrible night, could only ever exist for the people willing to believe it without demanding to see it themselves.

The room smelled of coffee gone cold in three different mugs and the dry, papery dust of old records disturbed after years of sitting undisturbed in a filing cabinet, a smell Mara had come to associate more with courtrooms than kitchens, and outside the window the grange hall parking lot sat empty except for their own vehicles, the whole building holding the same hollow, waiting quality it had held that very first night they'd come here with nothing but suspicion and no proof at all.

"We have everything we're going to get through investigation alone," Priya said, standing at the head of the table with the focus she brought to any briefing, her uniform pressed the way it always was even after what Mara now knew had to be several nights of very little sleep. "Denise knows we're watching her closely. She's already retaliated once. Every day we spend gathering more proof is another day she has to prepare for whatever we do next, or worse, another day she decides to act again before we're ready."

"Agreed," Caleb said. "The question isn't whether we go to her directly. It's when, and with what plan for what happens once we're standing in front of her."

Yusuf spread his hands over the compiled binders, and Mara noticed, not for the first time this week, how much older he looked under the office's harsh overhead light than he had a month ago, the toll of the decision he'd already made sitting visibly in the lines around his eyes. "I've read every page of this twice. I believe it. I think if I'm honest with myself, I've believed it since the night Priya lost that hour, maybe even before, somewhere underneath all the reasons I kept finding not to look too closely." He looked up, something steady and sad settling into his expression. "But believing it and knowing what to actually do about it are two very different problems. Exposing her publicly protects the valley from future harm, but it doesn't undo fifty years of damage already done, and from everything in Adelaide's own letter, it doesn't necessarily stop the bargain itself from simply passing to whoever comes next."

"That's the part we haven't solved yet," Mara admitted, pulling her chair closer to the table, the wood creaking in a way that felt, absurdly, like the only honest sound in the whole room. "Odette gave us a thread. This idea that the weight can be split, shared among several willing people instead of passed whole to one keeper. But a thread isn't a plan. We don't know how it would actually work, what it would cost the people who agreed to carry it, or whether it's even possible outside of whatever story Odette's grandmother half-remembered from her own grandmother before that."

"Then that's tomorrow's work," Wren said, still pale from two nights ago but stubbornly present at the table despite everyone's insistence she rest longer, a fading bruise still visible along her jaw where she'd struck the co-op floor. She'd refused the chair nearest the window, Mara had noticed, choosing instead the one with its back to the wall, some new wariness in her that hadn't been there before the pocket watch fought back. "Going back to Adelaide's letter, and to Odette again if we have to, and actually working out the mechanics before we walk in there with nothing but good intentions and hope."

"You're not doing any more solo work," Caleb said, not quite a question, his voice carrying the same flat certainty Mara had come to recognize as the closest he ever got to giving an order.

"I'm not doing any more solo reads," Wren corrected, a flicker of her usual dry humor returning despite everything, though it didn't quite reach her eyes the way it once would have. "Research from a book doesn't come with the same kind of teeth. I can read Odette's family bible cover to cover without bleeding on anybody's floor, I promise."

Priya cleared her throat, drawing the room's attention back to the map on the wall, her finger tracing the line of the property fence Mara had drawn from memory. "Regardless of what method we eventually settle on, we need to think practically about the confrontation itself. Who goes. What's said. What we do if she denies everything outright, the way Kell tried to before the evidence became too obvious to argue with."

"All of us," Dev said immediately, from his spot near the door where he'd been quietly listening through most of the meeting, arms crossed, the posture of someone who'd learned to make himself smaller in rooms like this and was visibly fighting the habit now. "Whatever this ends up being, I don't think it should happen with half the people who've earned a place in that room standing outside it instead."

"Dev." Priya's voice carried real concern, sister rather than deputy for just a moment. "This could be genuinely dangerous, in ways none of us fully understand yet. You heard what happened to Wren just watching an old memory. Imagine what she might do to someone standing in the same room as her, in the flesh, with nothing between them but a kitchen table."

"I heard," Dev said steadily, uncrossing his arms and pushing off from the doorframe to stand fully in the room's light. "I'm still asking to be there. I spent a year being the reason this town had something to be afraid of. I'd like to spend whatever comes next being part of the reason it doesn't have to be afraid anymore. That's not a debt I get to pay off from the hallway."

Nobody argued further. Mara watched something pass across Priya's face, pride and fear tangled together in a way she recognized instantly from her own feelings about Wren, that exact ache of loving someone brave enough to walk toward the very thing you'd give anything to keep them away from. She understood, not for the first time, how much this investigation had asked of every single person willing to stand in this room, and how little any of them had actually been given the choice to refuse it once they'd learned what they knew, once knowledge itself had turned out to carry a weight nobody had warned them about in advance.

"Then we plan for all of us," Yusuf said, drawing the meeting back to its practical center. "Six people. A homestead we've already scouted once, at dusk, the same time Wren and Mara caught her working the well before. We go prepared with everything in these binders, prepared with whatever method Wren and Mara work out for the redirection, and prepared, God willing, to actually end this properly instead of simply exposing it and hoping the next keeper does better than the last one did."

"And if she doesn't go quietly," Caleb said, his jaw tight in the way Mara had learned to read as him steeling himself for an answer he already suspected he wouldn't like. "If she fights us the way that watch fought Wren."

The room went quiet at that, the question nobody had wanted to voice out loud finally sitting plainly on the table between them, and for a long moment the only sound was the office's old radiator ticking somewhere behind the filing cabinets, an ordinary noise that felt, in the silence, almost aggressively mundane against the size of what they were actually discussing.

"Then we're prepared for that too," Mara said, though her voice carried none of the certainty the words were meant to project. "As prepared as any of us can be, against something none of us fully understands yet." She looked around the table at each of them in turn, Caleb steady beside her, Wren pale but resolute, Priya and Dev standing closer together than they had in months, Yusuf carrying the weight of a man finally choosing the harder, truer thing over the comfortable lie he'd wanted to believe. "I keep coming back to something from my old work," she added, quieter now. "When you're inspecting a structure nobody's fully trusted in years, you don't wait for certainty before you act. You act on the best information you have, you build in redundancy everywhere it's possible to build it, and you accept that some risk simply doesn't go away no matter how carefully you plan around it. I think that's where we are. Not certain. Prepared."

"Prepared for what, specifically," Wren asked, leaning forward, the old restlessness creeping back into her voice now that the fear had settled enough to leave room for it. "If she comes at one of us the way she came at Priya's memory, what does 'prepared' even look like. None of us can build a wall around our own minds."

"No," Yusuf agreed slowly. "But we can make sure no one goes in believing this will be simple, or safe, or over quickly. We can make sure every one of you knows exactly what you're choosing to risk before you choose it, plainly, without softening it for anyone's comfort. That's the only kind of preparation I actually trust, in the end. Not armor. Honesty."

"We have three days," Mara said, gathering the threads of the meeting back together the way she might close out a site inspection, every open item accounted for before anyone left the table. "Three days to work out the mechanics of the redirection, brief everyone fully on their role, and get Wren enough rest that she's not walking into this already depleted. Then we go to her. Together. And we finish what Adelaide started and couldn't."

Around the table, one by one, the others nodded, the weight of the decision settling over all of them differently but landing, finally, in the same place. Priya reached over and squeezed Dev's shoulder once, wordless. Wren pulled her knees up onto the chair despite the bruise along her jaw, some of the earlier tension easing now that the plan had a shape instead of just a fear attached to it. Caleb's hand found Mara's under the table, steady, the way it always did when there was nothing left to say that hadn't already been said.

Yusuf gathered the binders back into a single stack, squaring their edges with the same careful precision he brought to every piece of council business, a small, ordinary gesture that felt, tonight, like a bracing of its own. "Get some rest, all of you," he said. "I mean that. Whatever's coming, none of it gets easier because you walked into it exhausted."

Nobody moved to leave right away. Wren stayed curled in her chair a while longer, watching the map on the wall with an intensity Mara recognized as her trying to memorize every line of it before she'd have to walk the real ground it represented. Dev stood close to Priya near the door, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, neither of them quite ready yet to step back out into the ordinary dark of the parking lot and whatever waited for each of them once this room's borrowed certainty gave way to the far less certain hours between now and the well.

Mara and Caleb were the last to leave, and at the door Mara paused, looking back once at the office, at the maps and the binders and the single sheet of Wren's handwriting sitting apart from everything else, the only proof that could never be entered anywhere but trusted by the people who already believed it. Three days, and then an ending, one way or another, to a debt fifty years in the making.
