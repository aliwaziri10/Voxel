CHAPTER FIVE

Caleb didn't sleep that night, and Mara, lying beside him in the dark long after she'd finished telling him everything Wren had found, understood exactly why.

He was quiet about it, the way he was quiet about most things that hurt him, lying still enough that a stranger might have believed he'd drifted off somewhere around midnight. Mara knew better. She'd learned the exact stillness of Caleb actually asleep, the way his breathing changed, the way one hand always found her hip and stayed there, loose and unthinking. This wasn't that. This was a man holding his whole body rigid against something he couldn't outrun by lying still enough, his breath too even, too deliberate, the breath of someone counting instead of resting.

Outside, the orchard held itself motionless in the windless dark, and somewhere down toward the reservoir a single dog barked twice and gave up. The house made its usual small sounds around them, the settling creak of old beams cooling after the day's heat, sounds Mara had stopped noticing months ago and now, lying awake beside Caleb's rigid quiet, noticed all over again, the way every ordinary thing in this valley had started to feel newly worth noticing since the letter.

"Talk to me," she said finally, somewhere past two in the morning.

He didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice came out rough in a way she rarely heard from him.

"I keep trying to remember what it felt like. The moment it happened, whatever it was, wherever those two years went. And there's nothing there. Not even the absence feels like anything. That's the part nobody warns you about, when something's taken clean. You don't get to grieve a hole. A hole isn't a shape. It's just where a shape should be."

Mara turned onto her side, watching him in the dim light from the window, the faint edge of moon catching along his jaw. "You told me once you didn't need the record back. That you'd rather choose me with what you had than spend your whole life chasing a gap."

"I meant that." His jaw worked. "I still mean it. But there's a difference between not needing something and not wanting to understand who took it from me, and why, and whether the man I blamed for two years actually deserved all of that blame or whether he was carrying it for somebody else the whole time." He finally turned to look at her, and the rawness in his face made something in her chest tighten painfully. "If it's Denise. If what Wren found is real. Then somewhere in those two missing years, there's a version of me that met her, that maybe trusted her the way this whole valley trusts her, and something happened that she decided was worth erasing. I want to know what that version of me chose. I want to know if he'd forgive me for not remembering him well enough to finish what he started."

"That's not how memory works," Mara said gently. "You're not two different people."

"I know that." He almost smiled, though it didn't reach his eyes. "Structurally, I know that. You'd tell me the load doesn't care which beam is carrying it, as long as something is. But it doesn't feel that way at three in the morning. It feels like there's a stranger living somewhere behind two years I can't reach, and he knows something I need, and I have no way to ask him."

"What would you ask him, if you could?"

Caleb was quiet long enough that Mara thought he might not answer at all. "Whether it was worth it," he said finally. "Whatever he traded, whatever he agreed to or didn't agree to. Whether he'd do it again, knowing what it would cost the rest of me. I think I need to know that before I can decide how angry to actually be, at Denise or at anyone. I don't know how to be properly angry at a stranger who might have made a choice I'd have made myself."

Mara reached for his hand in the dark, and he let her take it, his fingers cold despite the warm night.

"When we confront her," Mara said, "if it comes to that. I want you there. Not because I think you need to be, for me. Because I think you've earned the right to ask her directly, whatever version of the question you need to ask."

"And if she doesn't have an answer that helps? If whatever she took from me is just gone, permanently, the way Ruth's memory of her husband's voice is gone?"

Mara didn't have a comfortable answer for that, and she'd learned enough this past year not to manufacture one just to fill the silence. She let the question sit a moment, feeling the shape of it the way she'd feel the shape of a load she hadn't yet calculated, resisting the urge to offer him a number before she trusted it. "Then we grieve the hole," she said instead. "The way you'd grieve anything else that's actually gone. Not pretending it has a shape it doesn't have anymore. Just admitting the shape used to be there, and it mattered, and it's allowed to still hurt even without a clear edge to hold onto."

He was quiet for a long moment, and when he finally spoke again, some of the rigidity had gone out of his voice, his hand loosening slightly around hers.

"I used to think the worst thing that ever happened to me was losing those two years," he said. "I don't think that anymore. I think the worst thing would have been losing them and never finding anyone willing to sit up with me at three in the morning while I tried to make peace with not getting them back."

"You're not going to have to make peace with anything alone," Mara said. "Not this time."

He pulled her closer, and for a while neither of them spoke, the valley quiet outside their window, the orchard holding its last blossoms in the dark the way it had held them through everything else this town had survived. Mara listened to his heartbeat slow gradually under her ear, counted it the way she counted most things when she couldn't sleep, and found some comfort in the fact that it was, at least, still steady.

"There's something else," Caleb said eventually, his voice steadier now. "Something I haven't said out loud, because saying it makes it feel more real than I want it to be yet."

"Say it anyway."

"If Denise has been doing this since before Kell was even born. If she taught him, or he learned it independently and she was just the original source he was echoing without knowing it. Then whatever she's protecting, whatever reason she's told herself justifies fifty years of quietly stealing pieces of people's grief and joy and calling it a mercy. It's not going to end with us catching her. Adelaide thought her own bargain would end things. It didn't. What makes us any different?"

Mara thought about the letter, still sitting in the top drawer of her dresser, Adelaide's careful, frightened handwriting describing exactly the same hope Caleb had just voiced and exactly the same fear underneath it. She thought, too, of the drought file, of a woman's name recorded in ink that had faded to the color of the paper around it, of how many other names might exist in other drawers, other ledgers, waiting for somebody else's grandchild to notice them decades from now.

"Nothing," she admitted quietly. "Nothing makes us different, except that we know what Adelaide didn't. We know it kept going after her. We're not walking into this blind, hoping it's a clean ending. We're walking in already knowing it might not be, and choosing to try anyway."

"That's not exactly comforting."

"No," Mara agreed, and pressed a kiss to his shoulder in the dark. "But it's honest. And I think, after everything this valley has survived on careful, comfortable lies, honest is the only kind of comfort either of us actually trusts anymore."

"When did you get so wise about comfort," Caleb asked, and there was the faintest thread of warmth back in his voice now, something almost like humor finding its way up through the exhaustion.

"I married an engineer's idea of romance and somehow ended up an engineer myself within a year," Mara said. "It rubs off."

He laughed, quiet and real, the first easy sound he'd made all night, and some of the tension finally left his shoulders under her hand.

"I don't know what we're going to find when we actually sit across from her," he admitted. "I keep picturing it a dozen different ways, and none of them feel right. Sometimes I picture her crying, telling us she never meant for any of it to go this far. Sometimes I picture her not even surprised we found her, like she's been waiting fifty years for someone patient enough to actually look. I don't know which version I'm more afraid of."

"Maybe both are true," Mara said. "Maybe she's spent fifty years being both, depending on the day. People aren't usually just one thing, even when what they've done is enormous."

"Even Kell wasn't just one thing," Caleb said quietly. "I hated him for what he took from me, and some nights I still hate him for it, plainly and simply, no complications. But I also remember him teaching me to cast a fishing line off the east dock when I was nine, patient in a way my own father never had the time to be that summer. Both of those are true. I don't know how to hold both of them at once without it feeling like a kind of betrayal to myself."

"You don't have to hold them at exactly the same weight," Mara said. "You just have to stop pretending one of them isn't there."

They lay quiet again for a while after that, and Mara found her own mind drifting somewhere she hadn't let it go all evening, toward the plat map still spread across the kitchen table downstairs, toward the drought file with its careful margin notes in her grandfather's hand. She'd spent her whole adult life trusting structures because structures obeyed rules. A beam either held the load or it didn't, and the math told you which, cleanly, in advance, with no room for a beam to decide on its own that the rules didn't apply to it this year. Whatever Denise was, whatever this valley had been quietly carrying inside itself for decades, didn't obey any rule Mara knew how to calculate, and some small, stubborn part of her resented that more than she'd admitted to anyone, even Caleb.

"Can I tell you something that isn't really about you," she said.

"Always."

"I'm scared that whatever this is, it doesn't have an equation. I've spent this whole year learning that some of what happened to you can be mapped, at least partially, if you're patient and careful enough. The well had a shape. Kell's whole scheme had a shape, once we found the right documents. I could draw you a diagram of most of it now, start to finish, cause to effect. I don't know if I can draw this one. I don't know if there's a diagram anywhere that explains why a woman can steal fifty years of memory and still look, to every person in this valley, like someone who'd never hurt a soul."

"Maybe that's the actual shape," Caleb said slowly, working it out as he spoke, the way she'd watched him work out plenty of hard things this past year. "Not a mechanism. A pattern. People convincing themselves that kindness in one direction cancels out harm in another. Denise probably tells herself the pie and the swimming lessons matter as much as whatever she's taking. Maybe they do, to her. That doesn't make it true, but it might be the actual mechanism, if you're looking for one. Not physics. Self-forgiveness, repeated so many times it calcifies into something that looks, from the outside, exactly like an ordinary, decent life."

Mara considered that in the dark, turning it over the way she turned over any theory before deciding whether to trust it. "That's a colder answer than I expected from you."

"I've had time to get cold about it," Caleb said. "Some nights it's the only way I can look at any of this without it undoing me completely. You get to have your equations. I think I need mine to be a little uglier than yours, or I don't think I'll survive actually sitting across from her when the day comes."

"Then have it," Mara said. "I won't ask you to be gentler about her than you need to be. Not after everything."

He turned his head toward her in the dark, and for a moment neither of them said anything else, the silence between them carrying more than either version of the conversation they'd just had out loud.

"Thank you," he said eventually, "for not needing me to have already made peace with this before you'd sit up with me tonight."

"That's not how any of this works," Mara said. "I don't think peace is something you arrive at once and keep. I think it's something you have to keep choosing, especially on the nights it feels furthest away."

Caleb's arm tightened around her, and somewhere past three, exhaustion finally won out over both of them, sleep arriving not as peace but as something closer to truce, a few hours of quiet before the valley woke up and asked, once again, for more than either of them felt ready to give it.
