CHAPTER FIVE

Caleb didn't sleep that night, and Mara, lying beside him in the dark long after she'd finished telling him everything Wren had found, understood exactly why.

He was quiet about it, the way he was quiet about most things that hurt him, lying still enough that a stranger might have believed he'd drifted off somewhere around midnight. Mara knew better. She'd learned the specific stillness of Caleb actually asleep, the way his breathing changed, the way one hand always found her hip and stayed there. This wasn't that. This was a man holding his whole body rigid against something he couldn't outrun by lying still enough.

"Talk to me," she said finally, somewhere past two in the morning.

He didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice came out rough in a way she rarely heard from him.

"I keep trying to remember what it felt like. The moment it happened, whatever it was, wherever those two years went. And there's nothing there. Not even the absence feels like anything. That's the part nobody warns you about, when something's taken clean. You don't get to grieve a hole. A hole isn't a shape. It's just where a shape should be."

Mara turned onto her side, watching him in the dim light from the window. "You told me once you didn't need the record back. That you'd rather choose me with what you had than spend your whole life chasing a gap."

"I meant that." His jaw worked. "I still mean it. But there's a difference between not needing something and not wanting to understand who took it from me, and why, and whether the man I blamed for two years actually deserved all of that blame or whether he was carrying it for somebody else the whole time." He finally turned to look at her, and the rawness in his face made something in her chest tighten painfully. "If it's Denise. If what Wren found is real. Then somewhere in those two missing years, there's a version of me that met her, that maybe trusted her the way this whole valley trusts her, and something happened that she decided was worth erasing. I want to know what that version of me chose. I want to know if he'd forgive me for not remembering him well enough to finish what he started."

"That's not how memory works," Mara said gently. "You're not two different people."

"I know that." He almost smiled, though it didn't reach his eyes. "Structurally, I know that. You'd tell me the load doesn't care which beam is carrying it, as long as something is. But it doesn't feel that way at three in the morning. It feels like there's a stranger living somewhere behind two years I can't reach, and he knows something I need, and I have no way to ask him."

Mara reached for his hand in the dark, and he let her take it, his fingers cold despite the warm night.

"When we confront her," Mara said, "if it comes to that. I want you there. Not because I think you need to be, for me. Because I think you've earned the right to ask her directly, whatever version of the question you need to ask."

"And if she doesn't have an answer that helps? If whatever she took from me is just gone, permanently, the way Ruth's memory of her husband's voice is gone?"

Mara didn't have a comfortable answer for that, and she'd learned enough this past year not to manufacture one just to fill the silence. "Then we grieve the hole," she said instead. "The way you'd grieve anything else that's actually gone. Not pretending it has a shape it doesn't have anymore. Just admitting the shape used to be there, and it mattered, and it's allowed to still hurt even without a clear edge to hold onto."

He was quiet for a long moment, and when he finally spoke again, some of the rigidity had gone out of his voice.

"I used to think the worst thing that ever happened to me was losing those two years," he said. "I don't think that anymore. I think the worst thing would have been losing them and never finding anyone willing to sit up with me at three in the morning while I tried to make peace with not getting them back."

"You're not going to have to make peace with anything alone," Mara said. "Not this time."

He pulled her closer, and for a while neither of them spoke, the valley quiet outside their window, the orchard holding its last blossoms in the dark the way it had held them through everything else this town had survived.

"There's something else," Caleb said eventually, his voice steadier now. "Something I haven't said out loud, because saying it makes it feel more real than I want it to be yet."

"Say it anyway."

"If Denise has been doing this since before Kell was even born. If she taught him, or he learned it independently and she was just the original source he was echoing without knowing it. Then whatever she's protecting, whatever reason she's told herself justifies fifty years of quietly stealing pieces of people's grief and joy and calling it a mercy. It's not going to end with us catching her. Adelaide thought her own bargain would end things. It didn't. What makes us any different?"

Mara thought about the letter, still sitting in the top drawer of her dresser, Adelaide's careful, frightened handwriting describing exactly the same hope Caleb had just voiced and exactly the same fear underneath it.

"Nothing," she admitted quietly. "Nothing makes us different, except that we know what Adelaide didn't. We know it kept going after her. We're not walking into this blind, hoping it's a clean ending. We're walking in already knowing it might not be, and choosing to try anyway."

"That's not exactly comforting."

"No," Mara agreed, and pressed a kiss to his shoulder in the dark. "But it's honest. And I think, after everything this valley has survived on careful, comfortable lies, honest is the only kind of comfort either of us actually trusts anymore."

Caleb's arm tightened around her, and somewhere past three, exhaustion finally won out over both of them, sleep arriving not as peace but as something closer to truce, a few hours of quiet before the valley woke up and asked, once again, for more than either of them felt ready to give it.
