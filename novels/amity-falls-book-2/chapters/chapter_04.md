CHAPTER FOUR

By two in the morning, Wren had covered the co-op's back table in a mess that would have looked, to anyone who wandered in without context, like the work of someone unraveling.

To Mara, arriving at midnight with coffee and staying because leaving felt worse than the exhaustion, it looked like exactly what it was. A pattern, finally visible, laid out in eleven separate folders across a table too small to hold them properly.

Priya had come by around one, drawn in by the light still burning behind the co-op's front window on her last patrol loop of the night, and had taken one look at the table before pulling up a third chair without being asked. "I'm not going to be able to sleep anyway, knowing you're both in here doing this without me," she'd said, and set a second thermos of coffee down next to Mara's, already going cold. She hadn't said much since, just read, and passed folders, and occasionally made a small sound in the back of her throat that meant she'd found something that mattered.

"Nineteen seventy-four," Wren said, not looking up, a pencil behind one ear and another in her hand, moving between stacks with the restless energy she got when a read wasn't the tool anymore and pure stubborn research was. "A man named Aldous Kepler. Town record says he stopped attending his own wife's grave, cold, after fifteen years of visiting every Sunday without fail. Everyone assumed he'd finally made peace with her death. His daughter's diary, which somebody donated to the historical society in the nineties, says something different. Says he woke up one Sunday and couldn't remember why the grave mattered to him at all. Not that he'd made peace. That the reason was just gone."

She flipped to the next folder.

"Nineteen eighty-one, we already know about, that's the drought file, that's Denise's own name sitting right there in the resident registry. But look what's ELSE in the same file." She slid a page toward Mara, a secondary report buried behind the main drought survey. "A farmhand named Otis Reyes. Filed a complaint that he could no longer recall the exact field boundary he'd walked every single day of his working life, a boundary his own father had shown him, one clear landmark, a bent fence post that had marked the edge of the Kepler land for two generations. Gone overnight. Everyone assumed he was drunk or lying to cover some kind of dispute. He was fired within the month."

"Same year as the drought," Mara said slowly. "Same year Denise's name shows up in the registry at an age that doesn't match her now."

"Same year," Wren confirmed. "I think that's not a coincidence. I think that's a renewal year. I think whatever this cycle actually is, it needs more than one payment sometimes, or it takes a while to settle, and the drought and the disappearances and the memory losses all cluster in the same twelve-month window because they're all symptoms of the same event."

Priya set down the folder she'd been holding. "There's a complaint in here from Otis Reyes's own son, filed decades later, in the nineties, asking the county to reopen his father's employment record. Said his father spent the rest of his life insisting somebody had done something to him that night, not an accident, not drink, something deliberate, and nobody in this town ever took him seriously enough to write it down properly. He died still saying it." She looked up. "That's not proof. But it's the first time anybody in this valley's history put it in writing that they thought it was done to them."

She kept moving through the folders, faster now, Mara reading over her shoulder.

Nineteen eighty-nine. A schoolteacher who forgot, entirely and without warning, the exact lesson plan she'd used successfully for a decade, a small thing, replaceable, except her students remembered her weeping over it in class, inconsolable in a way that made no sense for a lost piece of paper unless the paper had meant something the record never explained.

Nineteen ninety-six. A grandmother who lost, in one night, her memory of her own wedding song, played at every anniversary for forty years, gone so completely she couldn't recognize it when her granddaughter hummed it for her, though everything else about that marriage remained sharp and intact.

Two thousand three. Two thousand nine. Two thousand sixteen. Each one smaller than the last, each one more carefully chosen, a pattern that had learned, decade over decade, exactly how invisible it could make itself if it only ever took one precise, irreplaceable thing and left everything surrounding it untouched.

"Six to eight years apart," Mara said, doing the arithmetic out loud the way she did with load calculations, numbers settling into place with the click of something finally, terribly making sense. "Consistent enough to be a cycle. Irregular enough that nobody who wasn't specifically looking for a pattern would ever notice one."

"And every single victim," Wren said, pulling the last folder toward her, "lived within walking distance of the same property line."

She laid the county plat map over the folders, the one Mara had requested weeks ago under the drainage-survey pretext, and traced her finger along a border that ran, unbroken, through every decade's incident.

The Voss homestead.

Mara stared at the map for a long moment, feeling the last comfortable uncertainty of the past three weeks fold in on itself and collapse into something with an actual shape, a shape with a name attached to it that she'd known her whole life, that had pressed pie into her hands six weeks ago at her own wedding and told her it wasn't right to grieve on an empty stomach.

"I need you to say it isn't what it looks like," she said quietly. "I need you to find one reason this could be a coincidence."

Wren didn't look away from the map. "I've been trying to find that reason for the last hour. I don't have one." She finally met Mara's eyes, and there was no triumph in her face at all, only the same exhausted grief Mara felt settling into her own chest. "Every single one of these happened on or beside land that's belonged to the Voss family for over a hundred years. Land that belongs, right now, today, to Denise."

Priya rubbed a hand over her face, the gesture of someone who'd spent a career trusting the evidence in front of her and hated, this once, what it was telling her to trust. "I've known Denise my whole life," she said quietly. "She taught me to float on my back at the reservoir when I was six years old, because my mother couldn't swim and didn't know how to teach me. If you'd told me a month ago I'd be sitting in this room building a case against her, I'd have laughed you out of the building." She looked at the map again, at the border tracing itself through a century of small, careful thefts. "I'm not laughing now."

"None of this proves intent," Mara said, half to herself, the structural engineer in her reflexively looking for the load-bearing flaw in her own conclusion before she let herself trust it. "It proves proximity, and pattern, and timing. It doesn't prove she's the one doing it on purpose, rather than someone else on that same stretch of land, someone we haven't considered yet."

"You're hoping there's someone else," Wren said gently. It wasn't an accusation.

"I'm hoping there's someone else," Mara admitted. "I don't think there is. I just want to be sure before I let myself believe it, because once I believe it, I don't think there's any way to sit across a table from her again and pretend I don't."

Outside the co-op's window, the valley slept on, unaware, the way it had slept through every quiet, careful loss for a hundred years, trusting the wrong kind of ordinary because ordinary was, by its very nature, the thing nobody thought to watch. Mara found herself looking out at the dark shapes of the orchard rows beyond the parking lot, at the porch light of a house two streets over that she knew belonged to somebody who'd once, decades ago, lost some small irreplaceable thing and called it grief, and felt the weight of all those unremarked losses settle onto her at once, a hundred years of ordinary tragedy that had never once been allowed to look like anything but bad luck.

Mara folded the map carefully, the way she'd learned to fold every important document in this town, precise and unhurried even when her hands wanted to shake, and thought about a slice of pie, and a swimming lesson at the reservoir thirty years ago that Caleb still talked about fondly, and a casserole brought twice to two different funerals because buying a new dish felt like bad luck.

"Okay," she said. "Now we go to Caleb. And then, God help us, we figure out how you confront someone this town has loved for longer than any of us have been alive."

Priya began gathering the folders into some kind of order, careful with each one the way she'd have been careful with evidence at any other scene, and Wren watched her do it with an expression Mara recognized, the look of someone realizing that the thing they'd spent all night proving was real was about to stop being a puzzle on a table and start being a person they had to face.

"There's something else," Priya said, not looking up from the folder in her hands. "If she's been doing this since before any of us were born, she's not going to panic just because three people stayed up late with a plat map. She's had a hundred years to plan for the day somebody finally noticed. Whatever we do next, it can't be clumsy. It can't tip her off before we know what we're actually asking her to answer for." She closed the folder and set it squarely on top of the stack. "And it can't be just the three of us in a room with her. Not if there's any chance this goes the way Kell did."

"You think she'd hurt someone directly," Wren said. "Like Kell did."

"I think a woman who's spent a hundred years being careful enough that nobody ever caught her isn't going to suddenly stop being careful the moment she's caught," Priya said. "I think we plan this like it's dangerous, because it is, even if she's never once raised her voice at any of us in our whole lives."

Mara thought of Caleb's hand tightening around hers in the co-op parking lot only hours ago, the promise they'd made each other not to carry pieces of this alone anymore, and understood that whatever came next would have to be built the same way, carefully, together, with nobody holding back the hardest part to spare somebody else the weight of it.

"Then we bring Caleb in tonight," she said, "and we don't sleep until we have a plan that doesn't leave any of us standing alone in front of her." She looked at the two of them, exhaustion and resolve sitting side by side on both their faces, mirrors of whatever was sitting on her own. "Whatever it costs to finally end this properly, I'd rather pay it awake and prepared than wake up in six years to find out she's taken something else from somebody else we love."

Wren began folding the last of the folders into a single stack, careful, deliberate, the way she'd learned to be careful with everything that carried a history now. "For what it's worth," she said quietly, "I don't think she's evil. I think the letter, and the drought file, and every single one of these years, all point to somebody who believes she's protecting this valley, not hollowing it out. That doesn't make it right. But it might be the only thing that keeps this from turning into another Kell, if we go in understanding that she thinks she's the one keeping this town safe."

Mara nodded slowly, filing that thought away with everything else she wasn't ready to say out loud yet, and reached for her coat. Outside, the sky over the orchard was beginning, faintly, to grey toward morning, and somewhere on the other side of that grey a woman who had pressed pie into her hands under string lights six weeks ago was still sleeping, unaware that the century she'd spent being careful had finally run out of room to hide in.
