CHAPTER TWENTY-NINE

Denise opened her door before they'd even finished walking up the porch steps, as though she'd been waiting for them, and the sight of six people standing on her lawn at dusk didn't seem to surprise her at all.

"I wondered how long it would take," she said, and her voice carried none of the warmth Mara had spent her whole life associating with it, replaced now by something tired and old and, underneath all of it, achingly sad. "Come in, then. I'll put the kettle on. Seems wrong to have this conversation standing on the porch like strangers."

Nobody moved to follow her inside.

"We're not here for tea, Denise," Mara said, the words costing her more than she'd expected, even now, even with everything she knew. "We know what you are. What you've been doing for fifty years."

Something flickered across Denise's face, and for a moment Mara thought she might deny it after all, might fall back on the same warm, deflecting charm that had disarmed this town for five decades. Instead she simply nodded, slow and resigned, the mask finally, completely falling away to reveal the exhaustion underneath it.

"I know you do," she said quietly. "I've known since the kitchen. I felt you watching from the treeline three weeks ago, and I felt something reach for me through Ruth's husband's old watch two nights past, sharper than anything's reached for me in years. I'm old, whatever I look like standing here. I'm not blind to what's coming for me anymore, and I stopped believing I could keep outrunning it around the time your Wren nearly paid with her life to see my face."

"Then confirm it," Priya said, official and steady despite the tremor Mara could hear underneath her deputy's voice. "Plainly. For the record we've built, and for the six of us standing here who deserve to hear it from you directly instead of piecing it together from old files."

Denise's eyes moved over each of them in turn, lingering longest on Caleb, something unreadable passing behind her gaze before she finally spoke.

"Yes," she said. "I am what your evidence says I am. I have been renewing a bargain my sister Adelaide struck almost a hundred years ago, once every six to eight years, for as long as most of you have been alive and long before that besides. I have taken pieces of this valley's grief and loss, carefully, one small precious thing at a time, and I have told myself for fifty years that what I was doing was protection rather than theft." She let out a breath that sounded like it had been held far too long. "I don't expect any of you to believe that was ever true kindness. I've had a great deal of time to sit with the possibility that it never was, however much I wanted it to be."

"Protection from what," Caleb asked, and something raw had entered his voice, the question of his own missing years sitting plainly underneath it. "What exactly did you think you were protecting this valley from, by stealing pieces of the people who live in it?"

"From the alternative," Denise said simply. "From what happens if the bargain isn't renewed at all. Adelaide believed, when she struck it, that a single sacrifice could satisfy it permanently. She was wrong, and by the time she understood how wrong, she was too old and too frightened to carry it any further herself. She came to me because I was the only person left who loved her enough to take it rather than let the valley face what would happen if nobody did." Denise's hands, Mara noticed for the first time, were trembling slightly, an old woman's tremor finally visible now that the illusion of her ordinary middle age had fully dropped away. "I was twenty-eight years old. I didn't fully understand what I was agreeing to either, not really, not until it was already mine to carry."

"And after that," Wren said, her voice tight with a fury Mara understood completely. "Every six to eight years since. Ruth's husband's voice. A dead father's reason for visiting a grave. A wedding song. A single hour out of Priya's own mind, less than two weeks ago. You're going to stand there and tell all of us those were necessary. That there was no other way."

"I'm going to tell you," Denise said, and her voice cracked slightly for the first time, "that I have asked myself every single one of those questions more times than you could possibly imagine, alone, in this house, for fifty years, with no one else who understood what I was carrying to ask them alongside me. I'm going to tell you that I don't have a clean answer that makes any of it acceptable, and I stopped pretending I did a long time before tonight." She looked directly at Caleb now, something like genuine grief moving across her face. "And I'm going to tell you, because you deserve to hear it plainly and not pieced together from whatever fragments you've already gathered, that your missing two years were mine. My renewal, eight years ago, the same year Kell was young enough and desperate enough that I made the mistake of letting him see too much of what I was doing, thinking I could trust him to understand it the way I never let anyone else understand it. I took two years from you because the timing lined up, because you were grieving something already and I told myself the loss would be easier to carry buried inside grief you'd already have to work through anyway."

The silence that followed felt like something physical, a weight settling over the whole porch, and Mara watched Caleb absorb the confirmation of what he'd feared and half-hoped for in equal measure since the night he'd first learned any of this was possible.

"Why tell us now," Mara asked quietly, when the silence had stretched long enough that someone needed to break it. "You could have denied it. You could have kept running the same charm that's protected you for fifty years. Why confirm everything, standing here, with all of us against you and nothing to gain from honesty."

Denise's smile, when it came, held no warmth at all, only a profound, ancient exhaustion. "Because I'm tired, Mara. I have been tired for longer than you've been alive, carrying something I never fully understood how to put down, and some small, foolish part of me has been hoping, every single renewal for the last decade, that someone would finally come and force the question I never had the courage to force myself." She stepped back from the doorway, gesturing them inside at last, no pretense of warmth left in the motion, only the plain, weary invitation of a woman finally ready to stop running. "Come in. Ask me everything you need to ask. I don't have the strength left to keep protecting a secret that's cost this valley more than it ever protected."

One by one, they followed her inside, into the kitchen Wren had already seen once, decades younger, on the other end of a dying man's pocket watch.
