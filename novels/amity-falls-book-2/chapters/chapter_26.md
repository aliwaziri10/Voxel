CHAPTER TWENTY-SIX

Wren waited until Mara and Caleb had gone home before she let herself take the pocket watch out of the evidence folder a second time.

She'd told them she was staying late to help Priya finish compiling the inquiry file, which was true, technically, right up until the moment Priya herself had left for a few hours' sleep before dawn and Wren found herself alone in the co-op's back room with the watch, and the letter, and every fragment of proof they'd assembled sitting spread across the table like the bones of an animal nobody had quite finished identifying.

She wasn't supposed to do this. She knew that with total clarity, the same clarity she'd had the night she read the envelope alone, and it hadn't stopped her then either.

The difference this time was what had happened to Priya.

An hour of somebody's mind, taken clean, precise as a scalpel, and all Wren had to offer against that kind of surgical cruelty was fragments. A flash of hands. A gap in the air where a memory used to sit. She was tired of fragments. She wanted a face.

She held the watch and went under.

It came harder than the first time, the way the bad ones always got harder rather than easier, her own body some kind of ledger keeping score of every debt she'd already paid it. Cold first, then the low kitchen, then the woman's hands folding the letter, all of it familiar now, worn into a groove her mind seemed to know how to find on its own.

She pushed past it.

*Show me her,* she thought, not quite words, closer to the shape of a demand than a request, the way she imagined prayer might feel to someone who actually believed in it. *Not the letter. Not Adelaide. Show me who's still doing this.*

The vision buckled sideways, the way it always did when she asked for more than the object wanted to give, and for a moment Wren thought it had failed entirely, thought she was about to surface with nothing but a nosebleed and another lecture waiting for her.

Then it opened.

A different kitchen this time, newer, a kitchen Wren half-recognized from a dozen town events over the years, the Voss homestead's own worn countertops and the particular slant of afternoon light through a window facing west. A woman stood at the counter, her back to whatever vantage point the vision had given Wren, hands moving through some careful, repeated motion over a small object Wren couldn't make out clearly, murmuring something too quiet to catch.

The woman turned.

It wasn't Denise. Not the Denise who fed them pie and asked after babies by name and had aged, publicly, gently, into her late fifties over the span of Wren's entire life. This woman was younger, thirty maybe, dark hair without a trace of grey, but the face beneath it was unmistakably the same face, the same set of the jaw, the same small scar at the temple Wren had noticed without registering at the wedding.

Denise, decades younger, mid-ritual, alone in a kitchen that time had not yet finished changing into the one Wren knew.

The vision held for three full seconds, long enough for Wren to memorize every detail she could, and then it tore sideways all at once, a violence to the ending that hadn't been there in her first read, like something on the other end had noticed her looking and yanked the connection shut.

She came back to herself already falling, the co-op's floor rushing up to meet her before she'd even registered losing her balance, and the last thing she was aware of before the room went dark at the edges was the taste of copper flooding her mouth, far more than a simple nosebleed should produce.

---

Mara found her twenty minutes later, having come back for a folder she'd forgotten, and the sight that met her in the co-op's back room stopped her cold in the doorway.

Wren was on the floor, conscious but barely, blood soaking through her shirt from what looked like more than one source, her breathing too fast and too shallow, her eyes struggling to focus on anything at all.

"Wren." Mara was on her knees beside her in an instant, already reaching for her phone. "Wren, look at me. What did you do."

"Saw her," Wren managed, the words slurred and effortful. "Saw her face. Young. Real young. It's her, Mara. It's really her."

"I'm calling Caleb. We're getting you to the clinic."

"Wait." Wren's hand closed weakly around Mara's wrist, more force in the grip than her voice suggested she had left. "It knew. Whatever's on the other end of that watch, whatever's left of her intention in it. It knew I was looking and it pushed back. That's never happened before. Not with Ruth's glasses, not with the envelope. Something in there is aware enough to fight back when it's being watched too closely."

Mara felt something cold settle into her chest alongside the fear already sitting there for Wren's physical state. "You're saying this isn't passive residue anymore. You're saying whatever we're dealing with can actively resist a read."

"I'm saying I don't think we should assume we know the limits of what she's capable of," Wren said, and her eyes finally focused properly on Mara's face, fear and stubborn triumph mixed together in a way that made her look, for a moment, much younger than twenty. "I got the proof. Real proof, not circumstantial, not a birth certificate or a marriage record. I saw her. I saw Denise Voss decades younger than she should be, alone, working whatever this is with her own hands. That's not deniable the way everything else has been."

"It's not admissible either," Mara said gently, helping her sit up despite Wren's protests, checking the bleeding with hands that had gone steady out of sheer necessity even while her mind still reeled. "Not to a council inquiry. Not to anyone who didn't watch you nearly bleed out on this floor to get it. This is proof for us. It's not proof for them."

Wren's face fell slightly, some of the triumph draining out of it as the cost of what she'd just done finally caught up with her exhaustion. "Then what was the point."

"The point," Mara said, pulling her into a careful, fierce hug despite the blood still soaking both their shirts, "is that now three of us know, beyond any shadow of doubt, exactly what we're facing. That matters. It matters for how careful we are going forward, even if it never sees the inside of a formal hearing." She pulled back, holding Wren's face in both hands, forcing eye contact. "But I need you to hear this clearly, because I don't think you fully understood it before tonight. You cannot do this again. Not alone. Not without one of us in the room, not without a hard limit set in advance, not ever again. What happened tonight wasn't just a cost. It was a warning, from whatever's on the other end of that connection, and I am not willing to find out what happens if you push past it a second time."

Wren didn't argue, which frightened Mara more than anything else that had happened in the last twenty minutes, because Wren always argued, on principle if nothing else, and her silence now said more about how close tonight had actually come to something unrecoverable than any protest could have.

"Okay," Wren said finally, quiet, the fight gone out of her voice entirely. "Okay. No more. I promise."

Mara helped her to her feet, one arm braced firmly around her shoulders, and together they made their slow way toward the door, toward Caleb, toward whatever came next in a fight that had just gotten far more dangerous than any of them had let themselves fully believe until tonight.

Behind them, on the table, the pocket watch sat exactly where Wren had left it, ordinary and still, giving no sign at all of the violence it had just shown her, or the violence it might yet be capable of showing someone else.
