CHAPTER TWENTY-SIX

Wren waited until Mara and Caleb had gone home before she let herself take the pocket watch out of the evidence folder a second time.

She'd told them she was staying late to help Priya finish compiling the inquiry file, which was true, technically, right up until the moment Priya herself had left for a few hours' sleep before dawn and Wren found herself alone in the co-op's back room with the watch, and the letter, and every fragment of proof they'd assembled sitting spread across the table like the bones of an animal nobody had quite finished identifying. The overhead light buzzed faintly the way it always did after midnight, and somewhere outside a truck passed on the county road, its engine fading slowly into the ordinary quiet of a town that had no idea what one of its own was about to do to herself in a back room nobody else was watching.

She wasn't supposed to do this. She knew that with total clarity, the same clarity she'd had the night she read the envelope alone, and it hadn't stopped her then either.

The difference this time was what had happened to Priya.

An hour of somebody's mind, taken clean, precise as a scalpel, and all Wren had to offer against that surgical cruelty was fragments. A flash of hands. A gap in the air where a memory used to sit. She was tired of fragments. She wanted a face. She thought of Priya sitting at her own desk that morning, scared in a way Wren had never once seen from her, reaching for a memory and finding only the outline where it used to be, and something in Wren's chest had gone hard and certain in a way that made careful seem like the wrong word for what this moment actually required.

She held the watch and went under.

It came harder than the first time, the way the bad ones always got harder rather than easier, her own body a ledger keeping score of every debt she'd already paid it. Cold first, then the low kitchen, then the woman's hands folding the letter, all of it familiar now, worn into a groove her mind seemed to know how to find on its own, the way a path through tall grass gets easier to walk the second and third time even if the ground underneath never actually changes.

She pushed past it.

*Show me her,* she thought, not quite words, closer to the shape of a demand than a request, the way she imagined prayer might feel to someone who actually believed in it. *Not the letter. Not Adelaide. Show me who's still doing this.*

The vision buckled sideways, the way it always did when she asked for more than the object wanted to give, a wrenching, vertiginous lurch that felt less like falling and more like being turned inside out for half a second, every sense scrambled and then reassembled slightly wrong. For a moment Wren thought it had failed entirely, thought she was about to surface with nothing but a nosebleed and another lecture waiting for her.

Then it opened.

A different kitchen this time, newer, a kitchen Wren half-recognized from a dozen town events over the years, the Voss homestead's own worn countertops and a slant of afternoon light through a window facing west, dust motes turning slowly in the gold of it, ordinary and domestic in a way that made what she was watching feel somehow worse, not better. A woman stood at the counter, her back to whatever vantage point the vision had given Wren, hands moving through a careful, repeated motion over a small object Wren couldn't make out clearly, murmuring something too quiet to catch, the words themselves lost but the rhythm of them unmistakably ritual, a cadence Wren recognized from somewhere she couldn't name, old and patient and practiced.

The woman turned.

It wasn't Denise. Not the Denise who fed them pie and asked after babies by name and had aged, publicly, gently, into her late fifties over the span of Wren's entire life. This woman was younger, thirty maybe, dark hair without a trace of grey, but the face beneath it was unmistakably the same face, the same set of the jaw, the same small scar at the temple Wren had noticed without registering at the wedding, a detail so precise it felt less like memory and more like a photograph developing in real time behind her eyes.

Denise, decades younger, mid-ritual, alone in a kitchen that time had not yet finished changing into the one Wren knew.

The vision held for three full seconds, long enough for Wren to memorize every detail she could, the exact angle of the light, the small object catching it, the strange stillness in the young woman's face that looked less like concentration and more like grief being carefully managed, and then it tore sideways all at once, a violence to the ending that hadn't been there in her first read, like something on the other end had noticed her looking and yanked the connection shut with a force that felt personal, deliberate, aware.

She came back to herself already falling, the co-op's floor rushing up to meet her before she'd even registered losing her balance, and the last thing she was aware of before the room went dark at the edges was the taste of copper flooding her mouth, far more than a simple nosebleed should produce, and a pressure behind her eyes like something had reached in and squeezed.

---

Mara found her twenty minutes later, having come back for a folder she'd forgotten, and the sight that met her in the co-op's back room stopped her cold in the doorway.

Wren was on the floor, conscious but barely, blood soaking through her shirt from what looked like more than one source, her breathing too fast and too shallow, her eyes struggling to focus on anything at all, the pocket watch lying a few feet away where it must have fallen from her hand.

"Wren." Mara was on her knees beside her in an instant, already reaching for her phone, her own hands shaking badly enough that she nearly dropped it. "Wren, look at me. What did you do."

"Saw her," Wren managed, the words slurred and effortful, her eyes finding Mara's slowly, like they were struggling against something heavy. "Saw her face. Young. Real young. It's her, Mara. It's really her."

"I'm calling Caleb. We're getting you to the clinic."

"Wait." Wren's hand closed weakly around Mara's wrist, more force in the grip than her voice suggested she had left. "It knew. Whatever's on the other end of that watch, whatever's left of her intention in it. It knew I was looking and it pushed back. That's never happened before. Not with Ruth's glasses, not with the envelope. Something in there is aware enough to fight back when it's being watched too closely."

Mara felt something cold settle into her chest alongside the fear already sitting there for Wren's physical state, the two feelings tangling together until she couldn't tell where one ended and the other began. "You're saying this isn't passive residue anymore. You're saying whatever we're dealing with can actively resist a read."

"I'm saying I don't think we should assume we know the limits of what she's capable of," Wren said, and her eyes finally focused properly on Mara's face, fear and stubborn triumph mixed together in a way that made her look, for a moment, much younger than twenty. "I got the proof. Real proof, not circumstantial, not a birth certificate or a marriage record. I saw her. I saw Denise Voss decades younger than she should be, alone, working whatever this is with her own hands. That's not deniable the way everything else has been."

"It's not admissible either," Mara said gently, helping her sit up despite Wren's protests, checking the bleeding with hands that had gone steady out of sheer necessity even while her mind still reeled. "Not to a council inquiry. Not to anyone who didn't watch you nearly bleed out on this floor to get it. This is proof for us. It's not proof for them."

Wren's face fell slightly, some of the triumph draining out of it as the cost of what she'd just done finally caught up with her exhaustion, her shoulders sagging under a weight that had nothing to do with the blood. "Then what was the point."

"The point," Mara said, pulling her into a careful, fierce hug despite the blood still soaking both their shirts, "is that now three of us know, beyond any shadow of doubt, exactly what we're facing. That matters. It matters for how careful we are going forward, even if it never sees the inside of a formal hearing." She pulled back, holding Wren's face in both hands, forcing eye contact, her thumbs brushing gently at the blood already drying along Wren's jaw. "But I need you to hear this clearly, because I don't think you fully understood it before tonight. You cannot do this again. Not alone. Not without one of us in the room, not without a hard limit set in advance, not ever again. What happened tonight wasn't just a cost. It was a warning, from whatever's on the other end of that connection, and I am not willing to find out what happens if you push past it a second time."

Wren didn't argue, which frightened Mara more than anything else that had happened in the last twenty minutes, because Wren always argued, on principle if nothing else, some stubborn engine in her that rarely idled even when exhausted, and her silence now said more about how close tonight had actually come to something unrecoverable than any protest could have.

"Okay," Wren said finally, quiet, the fight gone out of her voice entirely, her head dropping forward slightly against Mara's shoulder. "Okay. No more. I promise."

"I need you to actually mean that," Mara said, quieter now, some of her own fear finally breaking through the calm she'd been holding together for both their sakes. "Not just tonight, exhausted and scared on this floor. Next week too, when you're rested and stubborn again and convinced you can push a little further than you should."

"I mean it," Wren said, and something in the way she said it, small and honest, made Mara believe her more than the words alone would have.

Mara helped her to her feet, one arm braced firmly around her shoulders, and together they made their slow way toward the door, Wren's steps unsteady enough that Mara had to stop twice to let her breathe, once nearly buckling against the doorframe before catching herself with a hand braced flat against the wood. Neither of them spoke during the short, careful walk to the car, the night outside gone properly cold, the co-op's gravel lot empty except for Mara's truck sitting alone under the one working streetlamp, and Mara found herself grateful, in a distant, numb sort of way, that no one else had been out at this hour to see the two of them stumbling together toward it, blood on both their shirts, one of them barely able to stand.

She got Wren settled into the passenger seat, buckling her in the way she might have buckled in a child, and Wren let her do it without protest, her head tipping back against the seat, eyes half closed, her breathing slowly evening out from the ragged, too-fast rhythm it had held on the floor.

"You're going to call Caleb," Wren said, not quite a question, her voice thin but steadier now than it had been minutes ago.

"I already did, from the doorway. He'll meet us at the house instead of the clinic, since you won't let me take you there, and God knows arguing with you about it right now isn't going to end well for either of us." Mara pulled out of the lot slowly, checking Wren's face every few seconds in the dim glow off the dashboard, watching for any sign the color draining out of her was getting worse instead of better. "I'm still furious with you, for the record. I want that clearly established before you fall asleep and I lose my chance to say it properly."

"Noted," Wren murmured, something almost like her old dry humor threading faintly through the exhaustion. "Furious. Got it."

"Toward what's on the other end of that watch, mostly," Mara admitted, softer now, her hands tight on the wheel. "Some of it toward you too. I don't think those feelings cancel each other out. I think I'm just going to keep carrying both of them for a while."

Behind them, on the table, the pocket watch sat exactly where Wren had left it, ordinary and still, giving no sign at all of the violence it had just shown her, or the violence it might yet be capable of showing someone else.
