CHAPTER SEVEN

By morning, Mara had talked herself out of everything she'd felt the night before at least twice, and back into it once, and by the time she and Wren sat down at the kitchen table with the drought file spread between them again, she'd stopped trusting her own certainty enough to say out loud which side she currently believed.

"Tell me again what the pause sounded like," she said. "Exactly. Not what you think it meant. Just the sound of it."

Wren didn't need to close her eyes to remember. She'd been running it on a loop since they'd left the Voss kitchen, the way she ran every read she'd ever done, turning it over for an angle she hadn't tried yet. "Half a beat too long. Like someone catching a word before it left their mouth and swapping it for a safer one. You do it too, sometimes. When you almost say something true and decide against it."

"I do that," Mara admitted. "Everyone does that. That's the whole problem with using it as evidence. A hesitation before a sentence about a woman's own dead husband isn't proof of a crime. It's proof of being human around grief."

"This wasn't grief. This was a story about Caleb as a kid. There's no reason to hesitate over that unless there's a version of it she didn't want us to hear."

"Or she was choosing her words the way people choose their words when they're being interviewed, even informally, even by two people they've known for years. People get careful around questions, Wren. Careful isn't guilty." Mara pushed the drought file an inch away from her, as if putting distance between herself and the temptation to see what she wanted to see. "I keep thinking about how easy it would have been to walk into that kitchen with no suspicion at all and come out with exactly the same impressions. A kind old woman, tired from talking, careful with her words because she's careful with everyone's feelings. That story fits the evidence too. It fits it better, honestly. It's the simpler explanation."

Wren's jaw set in the way it did when she felt cornered by logic she couldn't argue but didn't believe. "You're doing the thing you do. Building the case against your own read because the read is inconvenient."

"I'm doing the thing that's kept me from being wrong about important things for most of my life," Mara said, though there was less conviction in it than she wanted. "It's not the same as dismissing you."

"Isn't it?"

The question landed harder than Wren had probably meant it to, and for a moment neither of them said anything. Outside, a truck rumbled past on the county road, and somewhere in the orchard a dog was barking at nothing, the ordinary sounds of a morning that had no idea what the two of them were sitting inside trying to decide.

"I believe what you felt," Mara said finally. "I believe the scar was real and the pause was real and that spoon meant something to you the moment you looked at it. What I don't know yet is whether any of that means what we're afraid it means, or whether we've spent so many weeks staring at old records that we'd read guilt into anyone who so much as blinked wrong in front of us."

Wren was quiet for a while, turning her mug in slow circles on the table. "That's the part that scares me most, honestly. Not that she's guilty. That we've gotten so deep into this that we can't tell the difference anymore between finding something real and just wanting to find something, so all of this hasn't been for nothing."

"I think about that too," Mara said. "Every night, if I'm honest. What if we're doing to Denise what half this valley did to Kell before anyone had proof? Deciding, ahead of the facts, because it's easier to have an answer than to keep living with a question."

"Kell was guilty."

"Kell was guilty, and it still cost this valley something, the way it happened. The waiting for proof mattered as much as the proof itself, in the end. I don't want to skip that step twice in one lifetime just because it's slower than I'd like."

Wren pulled the file back toward herself and flipped it open again to the page with the drought years listed out in her own handwriting, dates circled in three different colors from three different nights of work. "So what do we actually do with a hesitation and a scar."

"We don't do anything with them," Mara said. "Not yet. We treat them the way you'd treat an unverified measurement. You don't throw it out, and you don't build a structure on top of it either. You mark it, and you keep looking for something that either confirms it or breaks it entirely."

"And if nothing ever confirms it? If it's always going to be this. A feeling and a scar and a pause, forever, with no hard proof underneath it?"

Mara didn't have an easy answer for that, and she'd learned by now not to manufacture one. "Then we live with not knowing longer than we want to. That's not failure. That's just what careful looks like sometimes, even when it's slow, even when every instinct in you wants an answer today instead of in six weeks."

Wren exhaled, some of the tightness leaving her shoulders, though not all of it. "I keep thinking about how good she was at it. Not the lying, if it is lying. The comfort. She made me want to believe her without even trying. If I hadn't already been suspicious walking in, I don't think I would have noticed anything at all."

"That's what worries me most too," Mara admitted. "Not the scar. The fact that fifty years of that kind of warmth is exactly what you'd build if you needed somewhere safe to hide for the rest of your life. I don't know if that's true of her. But I know it's true in general, and that's enough to keep me from letting go of the question, even while I'm telling you to be careful about the answer."

They sat with that for a while, the file open between them, dates and names that had started to feel less like history and more like a slow accusation nobody had officially made yet.

"There's another problem," Wren said eventually. "If we're wrong. If it's nothing, and we've spent all this time treating a kind old woman like a suspect, and somehow she finds out. What does that do? Not to the investigation. To her. To us. To how this valley sees us afterward, if word gets out we went digging into Denise Voss's past over a pause in a sentence."

Mara hadn't let herself think that far ahead, not fully, and hearing it said out loud made her stomach tighten in a way the pie hadn't the day before. "It would hurt her," she said slowly. "Even if we never said a word to her directly, even if this stayed completely inside the four of us, being watched changes a person. If she felt it and she's innocent, we'd have taken something from her that she never deserved to lose, just by looking too hard at a life that gave this valley nothing but kindness."

"And if we're right?"

"Then whatever discomfort she feels in the meantime is a small price compared to what she's already taken from other people, quietly, for longer than either of us has been alive." Mara said it steadily, though it cost her something to say. "That's the whole weight of this, Wren. We don't get to know which version is true before we act. We only get to decide how carefully we act while we still don't know."

Wren nodded slowly, and reached for her tea, gone cold in the mug, and drank it anyway.

"I think I need to talk to Priya," she said. "Not because I doubt what I felt. Because I need someone in this who isn't already halfway convinced, the way you said last night. Someone who'll tell me honestly if I'm building a case or building a story."

"I think that's the right instinct," Mara said. "It's the one I had too, watching you across that kitchen table. You're not the only one who's spent two weeks needing someone outside our own heads to check the shape of what we're seeing."

"Will you come with me? When I talk to her?"

"Of course," Mara said. "But let it be your read, first. Your words, your evidence, in whatever order makes sense to you. I'll fill in what I saw after, if she needs it. This started with your gift finding something the rest of us couldn't see. It should be your voice that carries it forward first."

Wren managed something close to a smile, thin but real. "You just want to see if I can build the case without an engineer standing over my shoulder correcting the load calculations."

"That," Mara said, allowing herself the smallest smile back, "and I think you're more ready for this than you believe you are. You've been ready since you found that first name buried in a drought file nobody else had opened in forty years."

They packed up the file together, careful with the pages the way they'd learned to be careful with everything about this investigation, and outside the morning kept moving forward around them, ordinary and unhurried, the valley going about its business with no idea that two of its own were sitting at a kitchen table trying to decide how much to trust a pause, a scar, and the strange, quiet ache of doubting someone they'd both loved for most of their lives.

Caleb found them there an hour later, still at the table, the file closed now but not put away, two mugs gone cold between them. He didn't ask what they'd decided. He'd learned, over the past two weeks, that some conversations weren't finished just because the talking had stopped, and that Mara most of all needed a while afterward to let a hard thing settle before she could talk about it again without reopening it.

"I'm heading into town," he said instead. "Priya's on shift this morning. If either of you wanted to walk with me."

Mara looked at Wren, and something passed between them without either of them saying it out loud, the small, unspoken agreement of two people who'd already decided the same thing separately and were relieved to find they still agreed once it was said.

"I want Wren to lead it," Mara said. "When we get there."

"Lead what?" Caleb asked, though the careful way he asked it suggested he already had a fair guess.

"Telling Priya what we've found," Wren said. "All of it. The records, the pattern, yesterday. I want her to hear it from me, in order, so she can tell me honestly whether I'm seeing something real or just seeing what two weeks of research has trained me to expect to see."

Caleb studied her for a moment, something like pride moving across his face before he could stop it. "You've been carrying this a long time without anyone outside the three of us to check your work."

"I know."

"It's a good instinct. Bringing someone in who isn't already inside it with you." He picked up the two cold mugs from the table, an old habit, tidying without being asked. "For what it's worth, I trust your read. I trusted it before either of you told me about the scar. That doesn't mean I think you're automatically right about what it means. It means I think you're the last person in this valley who'd let herself believe something just because believing it was easier than doubting it."

Wren didn't say anything to that, but something in her shoulders eased slightly, the relief of being trusted by someone who'd earned the right to withhold it and chose not to.

They left for town within the half hour, the three of them walking together along the road that ran past the orchard and down toward the square, none of them talking much, each turning over their own version of the same unfinished question. Mara watched the valley pass by on either side, the fields and fences and rooftops of a place that had survived one impossible year already and had no idea it might be asked to survive another, and found herself hoping, quietly and without much confidence, that this time the answer would come easier than it had with Kell.

It was, she knew even as she hoped it, probably not going to be that simple. Nothing about this valley ever was.

The square was quiet at that hour, the way it always was before the shops properly opened, a handful of early risers moving between the bakery and the feed store with the unhurried rhythm of people who had nowhere urgent to be. Mara found herself looking at each familiar face a little differently than she had a week ago, wondering, against her own better judgment, how many other ordinary people in this valley were carrying something nobody had ever thought to ask them about.

"You're doing it too," Caleb said quietly, close enough that only she could hear.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at everyone like they might be hiding something. I did the same thing after Kell. For months, I couldn't walk past a neighbor without wondering." He nudged her shoulder gently with his own. "It passes. Eventually you remember most people are exactly what they look like. It's just hard to remember that while you're in the middle of proving one of them isn't."

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"Not really. Just didn't want you to think you were the only one who's ever felt it." He glanced ahead to where Wren was walking a few steps in front of them, spine straight, jaw set, already rehearsing in her head, Mara suspected, the exact order she meant to lay everything out for Priya. "She's stronger than she thinks she is. You both are. I've watched you carry harder things than this without breaking."

"This one might be different."

"Might be," Caleb agreed. "But you won't know until you've actually tried it. And for what it's worth, I'd rather walk into this not knowing, with you both, than sit at home not knowing by myself, wondering if today was the day everything finally made sense."

Mara reached over and took his hand, and neither of them said anything else the rest of the way, the square opening up ahead of them, ordinary and warm in the early light, utterly unaware of the quiet reckoning being carried toward it.
