CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR

It was Dev who noticed first, though it took him most of the morning to understand what he was actually seeing.

He'd come by the substation early, before his shift technically started, the way he'd taken to doing since the night Priya finally told him he wasn't the person she once had to doubt. She was at her desk already, coffee gone cold beside her, staring at a case file with an expression he couldn't quite place, somewhere between confusion and a fear she didn't seem to recognize as fear yet. The morning light through the substation's front window was still thin and grey, the early hour Dev had come to associate with the quiet, private version of his sister nobody else in town ever really saw.

"You're in early," he said, leaning in the doorway, a paper cup of gas station coffee in each hand, one of which he set down in front of her without asking.

"Trying to finish the Hendricks report before the county deadline." She didn't look up, didn't even seem to register the coffee. "Should have been done yesterday. I don't know where the morning went."

Dev frowned. Priya finished reports early, always had, some old habit from a stricter version of herself she'd built after everything with him had happened. He'd never once seen her scrambling against a deadline she should have cleared with days to spare, and the wrongness of it, so unlike her, made the hair on the back of his neck rise before his mind had even caught up to why.

"You feeling all right?"

"Fine. Just tired." She rubbed at her temple, an unfamiliar gesture from a woman who moved through exhaustion the way other people moved through mild weather, barely registering it. "I keep losing my place in this file. Like I've read the same paragraph six times and none of it's sticking."

Dev came further into the room, an old instinct from childhood, watching for the signs their mother used to show before a bad spell, prickling at the back of his neck, cold and sharp and impossible to ignore now that he'd let himself notice it. "Priya. What's the last thing you remember clearly? Before this morning."

She looked up at that, an attempt to answer that visibly stumbled somewhere in the reaching. "Last night. Mara called, something about Denise, I went over there." She frowned harder, her hand going still against the desk. "I talked to all three of them. I remember that."

"What did you tell them. What did you decide."

"We decided..." Priya's voice trailed off, and Dev watched a genuinely frightening blankness cross his sister's face, the look of someone reaching for a memory and finding only the outline where it used to be, the shape of a room with all the furniture gone. Her eyes moved rapidly, side to side, the way someone's might when they were trying to read words off a page that had already been erased. "I know we decided something. I can feel that we decided something important. I just can't..."

"Priya."

"I can't remember what it was, Dev." Her voice had gone thin, scared in a way he'd never once heard from her, not during his trial, not during the worst of what he'd put their family through, not even the night their mother finally didn't wake up. "I know it mattered. I know it was about Denise. But whatever we actually agreed to do next, it's just gone. There's a hole where it should be."

Dev's stomach dropped, cold and sudden, as the pieces he'd been half-listening to over the past weeks finally clicked into a shape he understood, every conversation he'd overheard through the substation's thin walls suddenly rearranging itself into something with teeth. "Ruth Calloway," he said quietly. "This is what happened to Ruth Calloway. A memory taken clean, no other symptoms."

Priya went very still, her hand curling slowly into a fist against the desk. "You think Denise did this to me."

"I think you need to call Mara right now," Dev said, already reaching for his phone before she could argue. "And I think you need to hear this from me plainly, because I don't think you're in a state to trust your own read on this right now. This isn't tired. This isn't overworked. And it happened exactly the way it happened to Ruth."

Mara arrived within twenty minutes, Caleb and Wren close behind her, and the four of them crowded into Priya's small office while she tried, with obvious and growing distress, to reconstruct a conversation that had happened less than twelve hours earlier and now sat in her memory like a page torn cleanly out of a book, the surrounding pages intact but the missing one leaving no ragged edge behind, nothing at all to suggest anything had ever been there. Dev stood near the window the whole time, arms crossed tight against his chest, watching his sister struggle in a way that visibly cost him something to witness and do nothing about.

"Tell me what you do remember," Wren said, gentler than Mara had heard her in weeks, some instinct from her own training with careful, frightened testimony guiding her now, her voice pitched low the way Mara imagined a good counselor's might be. "Everything, even the parts that don't seem to connect."

"I remember Mara telling me what happened with Denise. The warning. I remember being angry on her behalf." Priya pressed her palms flat against the desk, grounding herself. "I remember all of you being here, in this room, or somewhere like it. I remember deciding something mattered enough to act on quickly. And then there's just nothing. Like someone reached into my head and lifted out one single hour, leaving the hours on either side stitched together so smoothly I never would have noticed the seam if Dev hadn't caught it."

"The rule," Caleb said slowly, dread building in his voice. "We set a rule last night. Wren said it, right before Priya left. Nobody goes anywhere alone."

"That's gone too, then," Priya said, her voice cracking slightly, the first real fear Mara had ever heard from her in all the weeks of this investigation. "I don't remember setting that rule. I don't remember agreeing to it. If you hadn't just said it out loud, I wouldn't know it existed at all."

The room went quiet, the weight of what this meant settling over all of them differently. Mara felt it first as a physical thing, cold moving up through her chest, because if Denise could reach this precisely, could take exactly the piece of a conversation that mattered most and leave everything around it untouched, then nothing about Priya's earlier reassurance held anymore. Denise wasn't reactive. She wasn't even simply defensive. She was capable of surgical, targeted retaliation against the very people investigating her, delivered so cleanly the victim couldn't even identify what had been taken except by the shape of its absence.

"Why this," Wren said quietly, almost to herself, rubbing at her temples as though the question itself hurt to hold. "Why take the memory of the rule specifically, out of an entire conversation. Why not something bigger."

"Because it's exactly the piece that keeps us safe," Mara said, the answer settling cold into her chest. "She didn't take the investigation. She didn't take our suspicion of her, or the birth certificate, or any of it. She took the one thing standing between us and being caught alone somewhere we shouldn't be. That's not random. That's someone thinking several steps ahead, exactly the way Wren warned us she might."

Caleb's jaw had gone tight, his hand finding Mara's without either of them acknowledging it out loud. "This isn't a warning anymore," he said. "A warning was the kitchen. This is an attack. She reached into someone's mind and took something specific, deliberately, to make us less safe going forward. That's not fear talking. That's a decision."

"We don't know for certain it was her," Priya said, though her voice carried none of the conviction the words should have. "I want to say that honestly, even now. I have no proof. I only have an absence and a theory that fits the absence perfectly."

"How much more proof do you need," Wren said, sharper than she probably intended, though nobody in the room seemed inclined to correct her for it. "She warned Mara. Less than a day later, the one person building the actual case file has a memory surgically removed, and it's exactly the memory that would have kept all of us safest. If that's coincidence, I don't know what conviction is supposed to look like anymore."

Priya was quiet for a long moment, and when she finally spoke, the fear in her voice had given way to a steadiness Mara recognized from every difficult moment she'd watched this woman carry since the two of them had first been thrown together by everything that happened with Kell. "You're right," she said. "I know you're right. I think some part of me needed to say the careful thing out loud one more time before I let myself feel what I actually believe." She looked around the small office at all three of them, her spine straightening the way it always did when she moved from grief into resolve. "She came after me. Not Mara, who confronted her directly. Me. The one holding the official record, the one whose word would eventually matter most if this ever reached a courtroom or a formal council inquiry. That wasn't random either."

"She's protecting the investigation from becoming official," Mara said slowly, the shape of it clarifying as she spoke. "Not us specifically. The paper trail. The credibility. If she can keep chipping away at exactly the pieces that would make this stick in front of people who don't already believe us, she doesn't need to stop the four of us from investigating at all. She just needs to make sure nothing we find ever survives contact with anyone who could actually act on it."

The room sat with that, the true shape of the danger finally visible in a way it hadn't been even after the kitchen warning, and Mara felt her own fear settle into a colder, steadier resolve, the same feeling she got standing in front of a structural failure that had to be solved correctly the first time, because there wouldn't be a second chance to get it right.

"Then we stop being careful the slow way," she said. "Tonight. All of us. We finish planning the confrontation, and we do it soon, before she takes something from one of us we can't recover from as easily as an hour nobody can fully prove was ever missing at all."

Dev had stayed quiet through most of the planning, hovering near the doorway the way he did whenever he wasn't sure his presence was wanted, his untouched coffee gone cold on the corner of Priya's desk, but at that he stepped fully into the room, his usually careful expression hardening into something fiercer than Mara had seen from him before.

"I want in," he said. "Not standing outside a supply closet hearing about it secondhand. In."

Priya looked at him, surprise softening her face for a moment. "Dev, this isn't community service. This isn't something the terms of your release cover, and if it goes wrong, it could cost you everything you've spent the last year rebuilding."

"She did this to my sister," Dev said, and his voice had none of the careful hesitation Mara had grown used to hearing from him. "Took an hour out of her head like it was nothing, left her scared in her own office over something she couldn't even name. I've spent a year proving I'm not someone who runs from a mess I helped make. I'm not about to start running from one I didn't."

Priya's jaw worked, some old protective instinct warring visibly with the plain fact of what he was offering, her eyes going bright for just a moment before she blinked it back. "This could be dangerous," she said. "Not metaphorically. Physically dangerous, in a way none of us fully understand yet."

"I know," Dev said. "I'm asking anyway."

Mara watched an old wound between the two siblings begin, quietly, to close, and found herself grateful, in the middle of everything else pressing down on the room, for at least one thing in this whole tangled mess that was allowed to heal cleanly.

"Then you're in," Priya said finally, quiet, decided. "Careful. Backed up. But in."

She stood, gathering the scattered case file back into some order, her hands steadier now than they'd been all morning, the frightened stillness from earlier finally replaced by the brisk, practical motion of a woman with a job in front of her again. "I'm going to write down everything I do remember, right now, while it's fresh, in case there's more to lose later. And then we plan properly, all six of us together, tonight."

Wren caught Mara's eye across the small office, the shared understanding passing wordlessly between them that the ground under this whole investigation had just shifted, permanently, into something none of them could pretend was still merely dangerous in theory.
