CHAPTER NINETEEN

They sat with the marriage record between them for a long time after Caleb went to check on the woodstove for the second time, an errand Mara understood without either of them saying so, a way of giving Wren and Mara a few minutes alone before he came back to hear whatever came next.

"I keep waiting to feel something like relief," Wren said, turning her mug in slow circles on the table without drinking from it. "Everyone talks about finally knowing something like it's supposed to feel like winning."

"It doesn't, does it." Mara reached over and stilled the mug with two fingers, gently, the way she'd once steadied a beam that was shaking loose in a wind it shouldn't have been strong enough to move. "It just feels like carrying something heavier than the not knowing did."

"Worse than that." Wren's voice had gone small in a way Mara rarely heard from her anymore, the girl who'd grown so much steadier over the past weeks suddenly sounding closer to the age she actually was. "It feels like we finally have proof, and all the proof does is make it real. Before this she was just a name in an old file. Now she's Denise. She's the woman who taught me to shell peas the summer I turned twelve. She's real, and she did this, and I don't know how to hold both of those at once."

Mara didn't answer right away, because she didn't have an answer that wouldn't sound false coming out of her mouth too fast. She thought instead of every version of Denise she'd known across her own life, the pie at the wedding, the warm hand on her shoulder at Thomas's funeral, decades of small ordinary kindnesses stacked up against the shape of what Wren had just laid on the table.

"I don't think you're supposed to know how to hold both of those at once," Mara said finally. "I think you just do it anyway, because the alternative is pretending one of them isn't true, and we don't get to do that anymore."

She thought of her own grandmother, gone a decade now, who'd once told Mara that the hardest part of getting older wasn't losing people, it was learning to hold complicated feelings about the ones who stayed, the ones who turned out to be more than the single kind thing you'd always known them for. Mara hadn't understood it then. She understood it now, sitting across from a girl she loved trying to reconcile a woman who taught her to shell peas with a woman who'd been quietly stealing pieces of other people's grief for half a century.

"Does it get easier," Wren asked. "Holding both of those things."

"I don't know," Mara said honestly. "I've never had to do it before. Maybe it doesn't get easier. Maybe you just get stronger at carrying it."

Caleb came back in with an armful of wood he didn't strictly need, stacking it by the stove with more care than the task required before he finally sat back down across from them both. "What's next," he asked, not looking away from Wren. "Now that we know."

"Now we tell Priya," Mara said. "Tonight, if she'll come."

"And then what." Wren looked up at both of them, some of her old bluntness returning even through the tiredness. "We've got a name, a marriage record, a decades-spanning pattern, an eyewitness account from a stakeout, and a Finder read that matches all of it. At what point does knowing become enough to actually do something."

"That's the part I keep circling," Mara admitted. "Priya's told us more than once that knowing and proving aren't the same thing. We know it's Denise. I believe it completely. But everything we have is still circumstantial in a courtroom sense, or supernatural in a way no council is going to accept without something they can hold in their hands and call undeniable."

"So we confront her," Wren said. "Straight out. Lay it all in front of her and see what she does."

"Not yet." Caleb's voice was quiet but firm, the tone he used when he'd already turned something over enough times to be sure of it before he spoke. "If we go to her now, with what we've got, and she denies it, which she will, because fifty years of not getting caught means she's had fifty years to practice denying exactly this, we've spent our one chance at surprise for nothing. She stops. She waits us out. And we're right back where Priya warned us we'd be if we moved too soon."

Wren's jaw tightened, the old impatience Mara recognized from the early weeks of this whole investigation, back when waiting had felt to her like the same thing as doing nothing. "So we just keep watching. Keep logging. Keep letting her go to that well every few nights while we sit on a public road with binoculars and call it progress."

"We keep building until what we have can survive her denying it," Mara said. "That's different from doing nothing, and you know it's different, even when it doesn't feel like it at three in the morning after a night like the one you just had."

Wren was quiet for a moment, and some of the fight went out of her shoulders, replaced by something closer to the exhaustion that had been building in all of them for weeks now. "I know," she said finally. "I just don't want it to be another six to eight years before anyone finally does something more than watch."

"It won't be," Caleb said. "We're closer than we've ever been. A name. A pattern. An eyewitness account of the ritual itself. What we don't have yet is a reason strong enough to bring the whole council in without her finding out first that we're coming."

Mara pulled the marriage record toward herself again, studying the two names side by side, Denise Voss and Harold Pruitt, a whole life built on top of a lie so old it had outlived the man she'd married into it. "There's something else we need before we can plan a confrontation that actually works," she said slowly, thinking it through as she spoke. "We need to understand exactly how the original bargain was struck. Not just that it happened, we've known that since the letter. We need the mechanics. How Adelaide actually did it, what it required, whether there's a way to prove someone's mid-cycle without catching them in the act a second time."

"The letter," Wren said, already reaching for it in her mind, the one document in this whole tangled case that predated everything else, the one none of them had reread as closely as the drought file or the marriage record because it had felt, wrongly, like something already fully understood.

"We've read it for what it tells us about Denise," Mara said. "We haven't read it yet for what it tells us about how the bargain actually works. That might be the difference between confronting her with an accusation she can deny and confronting her with something she can't talk her way around."

Caleb nodded slowly, some of the tension in his shoulders easing now that the conversation had turned from an argument about waiting into a plan for what the waiting was actually for. "Tomorrow, then. We go back to the letter with fresh eyes, all three of us, and we call Priya tonight to tell her what Wren found."

"Tonight," Mara agreed, and reached across the table for the phone, already composing in her head how she'd explain this to Priya in a way that didn't require the whole marriage record to be read aloud over a line that might not be as private as any of them wanted it to be.

Priya arrived within the hour, still in the flannel she must have been wearing at home rather than her uniform, and read the marriage certificate twice under the kitchen light the same way she'd read the stakeout log two nights before, careful, methodical, unwilling to let excitement outrun what the document actually proved.

"How'd you find this," she asked, looking up at Wren. "The county doesn't just hand marriage records to anybody who walks in."

"I told the clerk I was doing a family history project for school," Wren said. "It wasn't even really a lie. Denise is family now, whether anybody ever meant her to be or not."

Priya's mouth twitched at that, something between a wince and a reluctant smile. "That's either the smartest cover story I've heard all year or the saddest one, and I honestly can't decide which."

"This is good," she said finally, setting it down. "This is real good. It closes the loop between the drought file, the folk story, and the woman we've all known our whole lives. It's not enough to arrest her on. But it's enough to justify going further, and further is what we need right now."

"Further meaning what," Wren asked.

"Meaning I start looking harder at Walt Pruitt myself, official capacity or not, and see if there's anything else in that family's history that corroborates what Dev already told us." Priya folded the certificate carefully and slid it back across the table to Wren, the same gesture of trust she'd shown with the stakeout log, treating the evidence as something that belonged to the person who'd found it as much as to the case itself. "And meaning you three do what Mara's already suggesting, from what Caleb told me on the phone. Go back to that letter. If Adelaide left behind anything about how the bargain was actually made, that could tell us how to prove Denise is mid-cycle right now, instead of just proving she's been mid-cycle sometime in the last fifty years."

"And if we find it," Caleb asked. "What then."

Priya was quiet for a moment, looking around the table at the three of them, the same weight in her expression that Mara had seen the night Priya first agreed to help at all, someone doing the math on how much further she was willing to go for a reason she believed in more than the risk to herself.

"Then we start planning what a real confrontation actually looks like," she said. "Not tonight. Not this week, probably. But soon. Because the interval's closing whether we're ready or not, and I'd rather go into it with everything we can possibly carry than wait for the ground to move under us again."

She stood to go, then paused at the door with her hand on the frame, looking back at the three of them gathered around the table one more time. "For what it's worth," she said. "I know this doesn't feel like winning. It didn't feel like winning to me either, the day I finally had enough on my own brother to bring him in. Knowing you're right about someone you care for doesn't make it easier. Sometimes it makes it worse. But it's still better than not knowing, even on the nights it doesn't feel that way."

Nobody had an answer for that, and Priya didn't seem to expect one. She let herself out into the dark, and the sound of her car pulling away faded slowly down the road toward whatever quiet corner of her own life waited for her tonight.

The three of them sat back down at the table where the letter already waited, folded in its worn envelope, a hundred years old and heavier now than it had ever felt the first night Wren laid it down and told Mara everything.

"We should sleep first," Caleb said, though he made no move to stand. "None of us have slept properly since the stakeout, and reading Adelaide's letter with fresh eyes means actually having fresh eyes to read it with."

"I don't think I could sleep if I tried," Wren admitted. "Every time I close my eyes I see that marriage certificate. Denise Voss. Harold Pruitt. Like the words themselves are stuck behind my eyelids now."

"Then we wait until morning to open the letter, but we don't have to wait alone." Mara reached over and covered Wren's hand with her own, flour long since washed off but the gesture the same as it had been at breakfast, a small steady thing offered without needing to be asked for. "Stay here tonight. Both of you. I don't want any of us going back to separate rooms carrying this by ourselves."

Wren nodded, and something in her seemed to loosen slightly at the offer, the loneliness of the last few days finally finding somewhere to set itself down even if the fear underneath it hadn't gone anywhere at all. Caleb banked the stove one final time, and the three of them settled into the front room together, blankets pulled from the hall closet, the letter still sitting untouched on the kitchen table where it would wait for morning and whatever it still had left to tell them about a bargain nearly a hundred years old and a debt the valley still hadn't finished paying.
