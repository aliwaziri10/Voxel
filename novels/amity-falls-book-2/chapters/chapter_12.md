CHAPTER TWELVE

Ruth Calloway kept her late husband's things in a cedar chest at the foot of her bed, and when Wren asked, gently, if she could borrow one item for a day, Ruth handed over his old pocket watch without a second thought, the way people who have lost something already tend to give away what remains without weighing it too carefully.

"He never went anywhere without it," Ruth said, standing in her doorway with her arms crossed against the morning chill. "Wound it every night before bed, same time, forty years running. Never told me why it mattered so much to him. Never asked."

"You don't remember his voice at all," Wren said, careful, the way Priya had taught her to ask hard questions without making them sound like an interrogation. "Nothing left of it."

"Nothing." Ruth's composure held, but something in her eyes went distant, reaching for a sound that wasn't there anymore. "I remember his laugh, if I try. I remember the shape of his hands. I remember him humming while he shaved, though I couldn't tell you the tune. But his voice, actually speaking, saying my name. Gone. Like it was never recorded in the first place."

Wren thanked her and left with the watch wrapped carefully in a handkerchief, and by the time she reached Mara's kitchen table her hands were already shaking, not from cold.

"I want to read it properly," she said, setting the watch down between them. "Not a light touch. A full read. I've been holding back every time I've tried something like this, keeping it shallow on purpose because I know what a full read costs. I want to stop holding back."

Mara felt the words land like a weight dropping. "Wren."

"I know what you're going to say."

"Then say it back to me, so I know you actually heard it and aren't just letting me talk so you can do what you were already planning to do."

Wren's jaw tightened, but she said it anyway, flat and exact, like reciting something memorized under duress. "A full read without training or supervision risks real harm. Nosebleeds, blackouts, worse if I push past what my body can handle. You want me to wait until someone with more experience can be there, or at minimum until I've eaten something and slept properly, neither of which I've done today."

"And?"

"And I'm doing it anyway, because Ruth doesn't have forty more years to wait for me to get comfortable, and because a shallow read hasn't given us anything new in three weeks. This watch was in the room when it happened. If anything in this valley remembers what actually took her husband's voice, it's this." Wren wrapped both hands around the watch, knuckles pale. "I need you here. Not to stop me. Just in case."

Mara wanted, badly, to argue, to insist they wait for Priya or Caleb or anyone who might talk sense into a stubbornness Mara recognized because she carried a version of it herself. But she had watched Wren grow into this gift over two years, had watched her learn its edges the hard way more than once, and understood, finally, that the choice standing in front of her now belonged to Wren alone to make.

"I'm here," Mara said instead. "Tell me what you need."

"Just watch. If it goes wrong, you'll know. You'll know before I do, probably."

Wren closed her eyes, and her breathing slowed, and for a long moment nothing happened at all, the kitchen quiet except for the tick of the old watch itself, steady and unbothered by what was being asked of it.

Then her whole body went rigid.

Her breath caught, a short, strangled sound, and her hands tightened around the watch hard enough that Mara half expected to hear the glass crack. Her eyes, still closed, moved rapidly beneath their lids, the way eyes move during the deepest part of a dream, and a thin line of red appeared at one nostril, sliding slow and deliberate down toward her lip.

"Wren." Mara's voice came out sharper than she meant it to. "Wren, that's enough, come back."

Wren didn't answer. Her free hand had found the edge of the table and gripped it white-knuckled, and a second thread of blood joined the first, and Mara was half out of her chair, reaching for her, when Wren's eyes snapped open, wide and unfocused, fixed on something that wasn't in the room.

"Hands," she gasped. "I saw hands. A woman's hands, older, but not old, moving fast, like she was in a hurry, like she didn't have long. Working something between her fingers, small, I couldn't tell what. No face. I tried to see the face and it just wasn't there, like the read wouldn't give it to me, like something was protecting it."

"Wren, you're bleeding."

Wren touched her own lip, seeming to notice the blood for the first time, and some of the wild focus went out of her eyes, replaced by an exhaustion so total it looked almost like collapse. "I'm okay. I'm okay, I just need a second."

She was not okay. Mara could see that clearly even as Wren insisted otherwise, the color gone entirely from her face, her hands trembling now with an effort that had nothing left to spend. Mara grabbed a dish towel from the counter and pressed it gently to Wren's nose, guiding her head back, the same motion she'd have used on a child.

"Tilt forward, not back. Pinch here." Mara's voice had gone steady out of pure necessity, the way it always did when panic needed somewhere useful to go. "Talk to me. Stay with me. What else did you see, besides the hands."

"Nothing else. It cut off. Like a door closing." Wren's words came slower now, effort dragging behind each one. "I pushed for the face and it just wouldn't give it to me. Felt like hitting a wall inside my own head. Hurt more than the read itself did."

The kitchen door opened, and Caleb stepped in mid-sentence, some ordinary greeting dying on his lips the moment he took in the scene in front of him, Wren slumped forward with blood on her hand and Mara pressing a towel to her face with the exact, careful calm of someone holding herself together through sheer will.

"What happened," he said, already crossing the room.

"Full read," Mara said. "Without backup. Without asking anyone first."

"Wren." Caleb crouched beside her chair, his voice gentler than Mara's had managed to stay. "Look at me. Can you see me clearly?"

"I can see you," Wren said, and managed something close to a smile, though it came out crooked and thin. "Don't lecture me. I already know."

"I'm not going to lecture you." Caleb's jaw was tight, the effort of not doing exactly that written plainly across his face. "I'm going to sit here until you can stand up on your own, and then I'm going to drive you home, and then, tomorrow, once you've slept and eaten something and the color's back in your face, I'm probably going to lecture you a little. Tonight I'm just glad you're breathing."

Wren let out a wet, tired laugh, and the towel came away from her nose showing the bleeding had finally started to slow. "Hands," she said again, quieter this time, more to herself than either of them. "A woman's hands. Working something small and fast. Not old, but not young either. It's the closest I've ever gotten to actually seeing her."

"It's not nothing," Mara said, though her heart was still hammering from the last five minutes. "But it's not worth what it cost you. Not if it happens again like this."

"I know." Wren closed her eyes again, briefly, and this time it looked like simple exhaustion rather than the beginning of another read. "I'm not going to pretend it wasn't worth it, because I think it was, this once. But I'm also not going to pretend I could survive doing that every week until we've got a face to match the hands. Whatever this costs me, it adds up. I felt that this time in a way I haven't before."

Caleb helped her to her feet slowly, one arm braced under hers, and Mara watched them move toward the door together, Wren leaning more heavily on him than she likely wanted to admit, and felt something settle cold and certain in her own chest.

They were closer now than they'd been an hour ago. A woman's hands, moving fast, working something small between her fingers. It matched nothing conclusively, not yet, not enough to bring to anyone outside this room. But it was a piece where before there had only been theory, and pieces, Mara had learned over the last month, had a way of accumulating into something undeniable long before anyone wanted them to.

She sat alone at the table after they'd gone, the old pocket watch still lying where Wren had left it, ticking steadily on toward whatever hour it had been faithfully counting down for forty years, indifferent to what it had just been asked to remember.

Mara picked it up carefully, turned it once in her palm, and set it back down without daring to touch it any longer than that.

Some doors, she thought, were better left closed until you were ready to walk all the way through them.

Priya arrived an hour later, summoned by a call Mara had made the moment Wren and Caleb pulled out of the driveway, and listened to the account without interrupting, her expression sliding from concern into something more focused as the details settled into place.

"Hands," she repeated, once Mara had finished. "Not old, not young. Working something small and fast between her fingers."

"That's all she got. The face wouldn't come."

Priya was quiet for a moment, turning that over. "Denise's hands are recognizable. Arthritis in the left one, from what I've seen at the co-op, she favors it a little when she's kneading dough. If Wren saw hands without pain in them, without any sign of that stiffness, that's either not Denise, or it's Denise at an age before the arthritis set in, which would put this memory decades in the past rather than anything recent."

Mara sat with that, turning the pocket watch slowly in her fingers again without quite meaning to. "You think it's an old memory. Something the watch has been carrying since before Ruth's husband ever married her."

"I think we don't know yet, and I think that's exactly why a read like this is dangerous to lean on too hard. It's a fragment, not a photograph. It tells us something happened near this watch, at some point, involving hands doing something quick and careful. It doesn't tell us when, and it doesn't tell us whose hands, no matter how badly we want it to." Priya's voice was gentle but firm, the same tone Mara imagined she used on grieving families who wanted more certainty than an investigation could honestly give them. "I don't want Wren doing this again without someone trained standing right there. Not because I don't trust her judgment. Because I've seen what happens to people who push past what their body can survive, and I don't want to watch that happen to a twenty-year-old on my account."

"She's not going to stop," Mara said quietly. "You know that as well as I do."

"I know it. I'm not asking her to stop. I'm asking that the next time, it happens with a plan, with someone who actually understands the physical risk standing beside her instead of you and Caleb improvising with a dish towel." Priya stood, gathering her jacket back around her shoulders against the evening chill already settling into the kitchen. "I'll talk to her tomorrow, once she's slept. In the meantime, I want that watch logged properly, with the date and time of the read written down exactly like we would any other piece of evidence. If this ever needs to hold up in front of the council, it needs a paper trail as careful as everything else we've built."

Mara nodded, already reaching for the notebook she kept for exactly this purpose now, a habit she'd have laughed at three months ago and now couldn't imagine working without. She wrote the date, the time, Wren's own words as close to verbatim as she could recall them, and set the pen down feeling, for the first time in days, like the pattern they were chasing had finally reached out and touched something real.

Outside, the evening had gone fully dark, and somewhere across town a woman with steady, careful hands was almost certainly readying herself for bed, winding some clock of her own, counting down toward whatever hour her own long patience was still faithfully keeping.
