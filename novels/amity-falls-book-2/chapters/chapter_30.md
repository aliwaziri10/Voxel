CHAPTER THIRTY

Denise's kitchen looked exactly the way Wren had described it after the pocket watch, down to the chipped blue teapot sitting cold on the stove and the wooden spoon resting in its same worn groove on the counter, and something about the ordinariness of it, the sheer domestic normalcy of a room where an old woman had spent fifty years quietly stealing pieces of other people's lives, unsettled Mara more than any of the stranger revelations of the last hour.

Denise didn't put the kettle on after all. She sat instead at the head of her own table, hands folded in front of her, and waited for the six of them to find whatever places they could around a kitchen built for a woman who'd lived alone a very long time.

"I need you to understand something before we go any further," Denise said, once everyone had settled. "What I've been carrying isn't a debt that simply sits still between renewals, waiting patiently for me to decide when to pay it again. It's alive, in its own way. It draws. Constantly, quietly, from the moment one renewal ends until the next one begins, and the longer the gap stretches, the harder it pulls."

"Pulls on what," Priya asked, her deputy's instinct for precise language cutting straight to the practical question underneath the strangeness.

"On me, first. Always me, first." Denise's hands tightened slightly against each other. "That's the part Adelaide never told anyone, maybe never fully understood herself before it was too late for her to explain it. The bargain doesn't simply wait for its next payment. It starts trying to collect early, from whoever's carrying it, the moment the gap between renewals runs long enough. That's what aging looks like from the inside. Not years passing normally and catching up all at once the way it did with Kell. Years being taken, slowly, whether I offer anything or not, unless I redirect that pull onto someone else's smaller loss before it finishes collecting the whole of me."

Mara felt something cold settle low in her stomach, the shape of a structural problem coming into focus the way it always did, load and stress finally visible instead of merely suspected. "You're saying the six-to-eight-year interval isn't a choice. It's a deadline."

"It's the point at which the pull becomes strong enough that I can't ignore it any longer without it starting to show," Denise said. "I could delay it, sometimes, a little. Suffer through the early collecting rather than take from someone else. I did that once, in the nineties, tried to hold it off out of guilt after a renewal that cost a young mother her memory of her own daughter's first steps. I lasted four extra months before my hands started shaking so badly I couldn't hold a rolling pin, before people in town started asking careful questions about whether I was well." Her mouth twisted into something that wasn't quite a smile. "I renewed within the week. I've never tried delaying it again."

"And now," Caleb said, quiet and careful, the question underneath his own missing years audible in every word. "Now that we know. Now that this is happening in front of all of us instead of hidden. What happens to that pull, sitting here tonight, this far past your last renewal."

Something crossed Denise's face that looked almost like relief at finally being asked directly. "That's the part I most need you to hear plainly, before any of you decide what happens next. I am already well past due. I've been holding off these last weeks specifically because I could feel all of you watching, because taking from anyone now, with the six of you already circling, felt like exactly the mistake that would end this the way it ended for Kell." She looked around the table, meeting each of their eyes in turn. "Which means the pull hasn't stopped collecting. It's simply been collecting from me instead, faster and harder than it's ever pulled before, for longer than I've ever let it go unanswered."

"How much longer can you hold it," Mara asked.

"I don't know," Denise admitted, and the admission seemed to cost her something physical, her shoulders dropping slightly under the weight of the honesty. "Days, certainly. Perhaps a week, if I'm fortunate. Not longer than that. And when it finally collects in full, from me, unredirected, it won't do what it did to Kell. Kell's collapse was decades catching up all at once because he'd been trying to take without ever properly renewing, stealing the shape of the bargain without understanding how it truly worked. Mine would be different, and in some ways worse. Fifty years of postponed collection, released in one moment, either onto me entirely, which I suspect would simply end me, or, if the mechanism finds it easier, onto whoever happens to be standing closest when it finally breaks free of my ability to hold it."

The kitchen went silent at that, the ordinary tick of a clock somewhere in the next room suddenly the loudest sound in the house.

"That's what Odette's grandmother meant," Wren said slowly, working through it out loud the way she did when a thought was still assembling itself as she spoke. "About it being dangerous to interrupt something mid-cycle. Not dangerous like a trap. Dangerous because stopping it cold doesn't make the debt disappear. It just changes who pays it, and how much, and how suddenly."

"Yes," Denise said. "Exactly that. Whatever any of you were imagining when you decided to confront me tonight, whether you meant to expose me publicly or simply force me to stop, either of those endings, done carelessly, could very easily kill me outright or tear something enormous out of one of you instead of the small, careful pieces I've at least tried to take with some measure of restraint. I am not saying that to defend what I've done. I am saying it because you all deserve to know exactly what you're standing in the middle of before you decide what comes next."

Yusuf, who had said almost nothing since they'd walked through the door, finally spoke, his voice heavy with the exhaustion of a man watching every simple version of justice he'd hoped for collapse into something far more complicated. "Then simply exposing you, formally, publicly, the way the council would normally handle something like this, isn't actually an option anymore. Not without real risk of it costing someone in this room, or someone else in this valley, everything at once."

"Not if you want anyone to walk away from tonight whole," Denise said. "I'm sorry. I know that's not the ending any of you came here hoping to find."

"We didn't come here hoping for an ending that was easy," Mara said, and found, to her own surprise, that she meant it entirely. "We came here because Adelaide couldn't finish what she started, and because whatever this is, it doesn't get to keep going another fifty years just because stopping it properly is hard." She looked at Denise directly, engineer's mind already turning the shape of the problem over, searching for where the load could be redistributed instead of simply removed. "You said the debt could be redirected onto someone else's smaller loss. Is that the only way it's ever worked, one person carrying it, passing it whole to the next when they can't anymore?"

Denise was quiet for a long moment, something wary and hopeful moving behind her tired eyes in equal measure. "There are old stories," she said carefully. "Stories Adelaide herself half believed and half dismissed, about a version of the bargain being carried differently. Split, rather than passed whole. Shared willingly among several people instead of hoarded inside one. I never found anyone willing to test whether it was true. I never had six people standing in my kitchen willing to try, either, until tonight."

"What would it actually require," Priya asked, her deputy's practical instinct reasserting itself even here, in a kitchen full of things no procedure had ever been written for. "If we're seriously considering this. What does splitting it look like, mechanically, if you even know."

"I don't know for certain," Denise admitted. "Only fragments Adelaide passed down, half in warning, half in something closer to wistfulness for a version of this she never got to try herself. She believed it required the same kind of choice she made when she took it from her sister. A willing offering, from each person carrying a share, of something they were prepared to lose. Not taken, the way I've always taken from this valley without truly asking. Given. She thought that distinction mattered enormously, though she could never explain exactly why, only that the two felt entirely different to whatever the bargain actually was underneath the mechanism of it."

"Given by us," Caleb said slowly, and Mara watched him work through the shape of what that meant, the same careful weighing he brought to every decision that mattered. "Six people, each offering something small enough to survive losing, so that no single one of us ends up hollowed out the way you have been."

"That's the theory," Denise said. "I want to be honest that it is only theory. I have never seen it attempted. I have never known anyone brave enough, or foolish enough, to attempt it. It could work exactly as Adelaide believed. It could also fail entirely, and take from all six of you regardless, the full weight instead of a shared fraction, if the old stories were simply an old woman's hopeful invention rather than anything true."

Dev, who had said almost nothing since they'd arrived, finally spoke from where he'd been sitting quietly beside Priya, his weathered hands wrapped around a mug of tea he hadn't touched. "Walt used to say something like that. That the debt wasn't cruel by nature, only lonely. That it had never once been offered a kindness instead of a theft, in all the years he half suspected what Denise was carrying, and he always wondered if that was the real reason it kept behaving the way it did." He looked up, meeting Denise's eyes across the table. "Maybe he was closer to right than any of us understood."

Denise's composure wavered again at that, something raw crossing her face before she managed to steady herself. "Walt was kinder to me than I ever deserved from him, knowing what he half suspected and never once said aloud." She was quiet a moment, then continued. "If the stories are true, each of you would need to choose your own offering. Something real enough to matter, small enough to survive. I cannot choose it for you, and I would not want to, even if I could. That choice has to be yours alone, or the theory falls apart before it ever begins."

"Then that's where we start," Mara said. "Tonight, if we have to. Tomorrow, if there's any time at all to plan it properly instead of guessing." She glanced around the table, at Caleb's careful stillness, at Wren's fierce, exhausted focus, at Priya and Dev sitting closer together than they had in months, at Yusuf's steady, sorrowful resolve, and felt something settle into place that had nothing to do with blueprints or load calculations and everything to do with six people who had decided, one way or another, to finish carrying what Adelaide never could.

"Days," Denise said again, quieter now, almost to herself. "You have days, not weeks, to work out whether an old story half-remembered by an old woman's grandmother is actually true."

"Then we'd better start now," Wren said, already reaching for the notebook she never seemed to travel without, and around the kitchen table, six people and the woman they'd come to stop began, together, the work of trying to end something none of them fully understood yet, against a clock none of them could see but all of them could now, plainly, feel.
