CHAPTER EIGHT

Priya's office at the substation was smaller than the kitchen they'd sat in the day before, half a room really, crowded with a metal desk and a filing cabinet that stuck on the bottom drawer and a corkboard covered in notices nobody ever seemed to take down even after they'd gone stale. She waved them in without looking up from the form she was finishing, and it wasn't until she'd signed it and set her pen down that she really looked at the three of them standing in her doorway and understood, from their faces alone, that this wasn't a social visit.

"Close the door," she said.

Wren did, and then stood there for a moment with her back against it, gathering whatever she'd rehearsed on the walk over, and Mara resisted the urge to start for her, the way she'd resisted it in Denise's kitchen a day before. This was Wren's read. It deserved to be Wren's telling.

"I need you to listen to something," Wren said finally, "and I need you to tell me honestly if it's real or if I've talked myself into it. Not gently. Honestly."

Priya's expression sharpened into the same stillness Mara had come to associate with her working mind, the deputy underneath the friend surfacing without any visible effort. "Go ahead."

Wren laid it out the way Mara had asked her to, in order, without skipping ahead to conclusions. The letter Adelaide had left Mara at the wedding, its warning that the bargain had been renewed since her death, more than once. The name in the 1980s drought file that didn't match any death certificate on record. The pattern of incidents Wren had found stretching back to 1974, every single one traced to land that had once belonged to the Voss family. Ruth Calloway's memory, taken clean, no other symptoms, no explanation anyone in the valley had ever managed to give her. And finally, carefully, without embellishment, the visit to Denise's kitchen the day before: the pause before a story about Caleb as a boy, the scar at her wrist, the way her hand had drifted toward an old wooden spoon a half second before Wren's had reached for it.

Priya didn't interrupt once. She sat with her hands folded on the desk, listening the way she must have learned to listen at every scene she'd ever walked into as a young deputy, gathering the whole shape of something before deciding what any single piece of it meant. When Wren finished, the room stayed quiet for long enough that Mara found herself counting the seconds.

"Say the dates again," Priya said. "The pattern years. Slowly."

Wren recited them from memory, the way she'd recited them to Mara and Caleb a dozen times already. "Nineteen seventy-four. Eighty-one. Eighty-nine. Ninety-six. Two thousand three. Two thousand nine. Twenty sixteen."

Priya was already reaching for a legal pad, writing the years down herself in a column, and beside them, without needing to be asked, the rough interval between each one. "Seven years. Eight. Seven. Seven. Six. Seven." She looked up. "That's not random. Randomness doesn't hold a pattern that tight for forty years. Whatever's driving that cycle, it's deliberate, or it's biological, something with a real mechanism behind it rather than coincidence."

"That's what I thought too," Wren said, something in her posture easing slightly at having the pattern recognized by someone whose whole job was recognizing patterns. "But recognizing a mechanism isn't the same as proving who's behind it."

"No," Priya agreed. "It isn't. And I want to be honest with you, because you asked me to be. Everything you've told me so far, on its own, is the kind of thing that gets a case laughed out of a courtroom before it's even opened. A pattern in old records. A pause in conversation. A scar that could belong to a thousand harmless stories. None of it, individually, would move me to open an investigation if a stranger walked in and told me this."

Wren's face fell slightly, though she nodded, bracing for the rest of it.

"But you're not a stranger," Priya continued, "and I've spent two years watching you develop a gift I don't fully understand and don't need to, in order to trust that when you say something felt wrong in that kitchen, it probably was. The problem isn't whether I believe you. It's whether what you've got would survive contact with people who have every reason not to believe it, starting with a council that loves Denise Voss and will need more than a feeling before they'll let anyone treat her like a suspect."

"So what do we do," Mara said, "to make it survive contact."

Priya turned to a fresh page on the legal pad, and something in the shift of her posture told Mara they'd crossed some invisible line, from a conversation into a working session. "First, we stop calling this a suspicion and start calling it a case file, even informally, even if it's just the four of us who ever see it. That means everything gets dated, sourced, and written down the way I'd write down anything I intended to bring in front of a judge eventually, whether or not it ever actually gets that far. No more carrying pieces of this around in your head, Wren. Everything you know goes on paper, today, while it's fresh."

"That's basically what the file already is," Wren said, gesturing at the folder Mara carried.

"It's a good start, but it's a research file. It reads like someone building a theory. What we need is closer to an incident log. Dates, locations, named witnesses where you have them, direct quotes where you can remember them exactly. Ruth Calloway's memory loss should be its own entry, dated, with everything she's told anyone about it since, in her own words if we can get them. The scar. The pause. All of it, entered the same careful way, whether or not it feels significant on its own."

Caleb, who'd been quiet through most of the conversation, spoke up from where he leaned against the filing cabinet. "You're talking about this like you already believe it."

Priya set her pen down and looked at him directly. "I believe there's something here worth taking seriously. I don't yet believe it's Denise, not with the certainty any of you seem to be circling toward. Those are different things, and I need all three of you to hold onto that difference the way I'm going to hold onto it, or this whole thing collapses into exactly what a skeptical council will accuse us of the moment we bring it forward: three people who loved someone, decided they were being wronged by someone else they also loved, and built a case backward from a conclusion they'd already reached."

The room went quiet again, and Mara felt the truth of it settle uncomfortably in her chest, because it was exactly the fear she'd been sitting with since the night before, phrased more precisely than she'd managed to phrase it herself.

"Then help us not do that," Mara said. "Tell us what would actually change your mind, in either direction. What would make you certain this is nothing. What would make you certain it isn't."

Priya considered that seriously, tapping her pen against the legal pad. "On the nothing side, an honest explanation for the scar that checks out. A hospital record, an old family story that corroborates independently rather than just being told to us by Denise herself. Something outside her own account that closes the question instead of just answering it."

"And on the other side?"

"A second incident. Not historical, not something we're reconstructing forty years later from county paperwork. Something happening now, close enough in time that we can trace it directly to her, not just to a pattern that happens to fit her family's land." Priya's voice had gone quiet and serious in a way Mara hadn't heard from her since the worst weeks of the Kell business last year. "Ruth's memory loss is a start, but it happened before any of you were watching for it, before there was a name attached to who might have caused it. If it happens again, and this time we're close enough to actually see something, that's the difference between a pattern and a case."

"You think it will happen again," Wren said. "Soon."

"If your dates are right, and the interval's held steady this long, we're due, or close to it. Which means from today forward, all four of us need to be watching for exactly the kind of small, deniable loss that wouldn't have registered as strange a month ago. A memory that disappears clean, with no other symptoms. Grief, mostly, you said. Watch for that pattern specifically."

Mara felt something settle inside her that was neither relief nor fear exactly, but closer to the steadiness that came from finally having a plan instead of only a question. "Then that's what we do. We build the log. We watch. And we don't say Denise's name to anyone outside this room until we have something that isn't just a feeling shared by four people who already love her too much to trust their own instincts about her."

Priya nodded once, decisive. "Agreed. And Wren." She waited until Wren met her eyes directly. "For what it's worth, walking an instinct like this into a room and asking someone to try to prove you wrong, instead of just asking them to agree with you, that's not weakness. That's exactly what good investigative work looks like. Most people never learn to do that. You did it on your first try."

Wren managed a small, tired smile, the tension in her shoulders finally easing all the way down. "Doesn't feel like a compliment yet. Feels like homework."

"It's both," Priya said, and almost smiled herself. "Most good work is."

They left the substation a little while later with a plan where they'd had only questions before, the four of them agreeing to meet again in three days with whatever the log had gathered by then. On their way out, they passed Dev in the hallway, mop in hand, finishing the last of his community-service hours for the week under the watchful, careful distance Priya still kept from her brother even now, months into his return. He nodded at Wren, then at Mara, and something in the way his eyes lingered a moment too long on the folder under Mara's arm told her he'd clocked exactly what kind of meeting they'd just come out of, even without being told a word of it.

"You four look like you've been arguing about something serious," he said, trying for lightness and only half managing it.

"Nothing you need to worry about," Priya said, a little too quickly, in the exact tone of a sister protecting a brother still finding his footing.

Dev held up his free hand, unbothered, or pretending to be. "Wasn't going to ask. Just saying, if it's about anything strange happening around here lately, I've been hearing things. Old-timer talk, mostly. The kind of stories people tell each other over coffee and don't expect anyone to take seriously." He shrugged, already moving past them toward the supply closet. "Probably nothing. This town's got more folk stories than it's got people to tell them."

Wren went very still beside Mara, and Mara felt the same stillness settle into her own spine, the two of them exchanging a glance that neither Priya nor Caleb caught in the moment, both of them filing away, without a word spoken, that whatever Dev had heard was a conversation for another day, one they intended to have soon.

As they stepped back out into the square, the ordinary business of the valley moving around them exactly as it had that morning, Mara found herself thinking, not for the first time, that the hardest part of all of this might not be finding proof of what Denise had done.

It might be learning to watch her, patiently and carefully, for a mistake that would cost someone else something irreplaceable before it ever cost Denise anything at all.
