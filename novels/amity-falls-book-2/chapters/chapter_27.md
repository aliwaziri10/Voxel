CHAPTER TWENTY-SEVEN

Caleb didn't say much when Mara brought Wren home, which frightened her more than if he'd shouted.

He met them at the door already moving, some instinct pulling him into action before Mara had even finished explaining, and within minutes the kitchen table had become something closer to a field station than a place they'd eaten breakfast together that same morning: clean towels, a basin of warm water, the good flashlight from the hall closet angled so he could actually see what he was doing while he checked Wren over for anything worse than the nosebleed she kept insisting was the whole of it.

He simply took over, quiet and efficient, cleaning the worst of the blood while Mara held Wren's hand and murmured whatever reassurances she could find, his hands steady even though Mara could see, in the tight set of his jaw, exactly how much that steadiness was costing him. It wasn't until Wren was finally asleep on their couch, wrapped in every blanket the house owned, her breathing settled into something slow and even, that his composure cracked at all.

"She could have died," he said, low, standing at the kitchen counter with his back to Mara, his hands braced against the edge hard enough that his knuckles had gone pale. "Alone. On that floor. And none of us would have known until morning."

"I know."

"Do you." He turned, and the fear in his face had curdled into something sharper, something closer to anger than she was used to seeing directed anywhere near her. "Because from where I'm standing, this whole investigation has slowly convinced all three of us that reckless is just what this fight requires. Wren does a solo read on the letter, we let it go because she got something useful out of it. Wren pushes her limits on the watch weeks later, and now she's nearly bled out over a face we already half-suspected we'd find. At what point does one of us say enough, before she pushes past whatever her body can actually survive."

Mara didn't flinch from it, because she'd been asking herself some version of the same question the entire walk home. "I already told her no more solo reads. Tonight. On the floor of the co-op, before either of us moved her."

"And you believe her."

"I believe she meant it in the moment," Mara said carefully. "I don't know if fear holds the same way exhaustion does, three weeks from now, when we're desperate for one more piece of proof and she's the only one of us who can get it." She sat down heavily at the kitchen table, the adrenaline that had carried her through the last hour finally draining out and leaving something closer to bone-deep tiredness in its place. "I don't have a clean answer for you, Caleb. I wish I did. I wish I could tell you we're past the point where any of us has to risk something this real, but I think we're actually just now arriving at that point, not past it."

Caleb was quiet for a long moment, some of the sharp edge going out of his posture as her honesty settled into the room between them. He looked, for that stretch of silence, older than she'd ever seen him, the steady, plainspoken man she'd married suddenly visible as someone carrying his own fear the way she carried hers, badly hidden underneath a calm neither of them fully believed anymore.

"I'm not angry at her," he said finally, softer. "I want to be clear about that. I'm angry at whatever this is, for making a twenty-year-old girl feel like bleeding on a floor alone at midnight is a reasonable price to pay for a piece of proof none of us can even use officially." He came to sit across from Mara, some of the earlier rigidity finally leaving his shoulders. "I keep thinking about what you said in the co-op parking lot, weeks ago. That we're not walking into this blind, the way Adelaide was. That we know what she didn't." He shook his head slowly. "I don't think that's protecting us the way I hoped it would. I think knowing what's coming and still not being able to stop it from costing us something is its own kind of cruelty."

Mara reached across the table and took his hand, the weight of the night settling into something quieter between them. "I don't have a way to make this safer for any of us. I wish I did. All I can offer you is that we go into whatever comes next together, watching each other more closely than we have been, and that if any one of us starts making the kind of choice Wren made tonight, alone, unsupervised, past what our bodies can safely give, the rest of us stop assuming good intentions are enough and actually intervene."

"That includes you," Caleb said. "And me. Not just Wren."

"That includes all of us," Mara agreed.

They sat with that for a while, the house quiet except for Wren's steady breathing from the next room, the valley outside holding its own stillness that neither of them fully trusted anymore. The kitchen clock ticked on above the stove, an old wedding gift from someone whose name Mara could no longer remember without checking the little card still tucked behind it in a drawer, and she found herself watching the second hand more closely than she needed to, grateful for something so ordinary to fix her attention on while the rest of her mind kept circling back to the sight of Wren on that floor.

"I keep seeing it," she admitted quietly. "Every time I close my eyes for even a second. The blood. How still she was for that first moment before she spoke."

Caleb reached across and took her hand without a word, and they sat like that for a while, two people holding onto each other in a kitchen gone strange with fear, the ordinary shapes of their life together, the chipped mug rack, the calendar still turned to last month, standing witness to something neither of them had ever expected to be true when they'd hung them there.

"What did she actually see," Caleb asked eventually. "You told me the basics on the walk home, but I want the whole thing. Everything she told you before the adrenaline wore off and she started editing herself for our benefit."

Mara told him. The kitchen, older, unfamiliar, a slant of afternoon light through a window facing west. The woman at the counter, thirty years old at most, dark hair without a trace of grey, the same scar at the temple, the same set of the jaw, unmistakably Denise decades before either of them had been born. The ritual motion over an object neither of them could name, small and repeated, murmured words too quiet for even Wren's read to catch clearly. The violence of the vision's ending, the sense, according to Wren, that something on the other end had noticed itself being watched and moved deliberately to shut the connection down, tearing sideways all at once rather than simply fading the way her earlier reads always had.

Caleb absorbed all of it without interrupting, his jaw tightening further with every new detail, and when Mara finished he sat back, staring at some point past her shoulder for a long moment before speaking again.

"Three seconds," he said finally. "That's all she had."

"Long enough to be sure of the face," Mara said. "Not long enough for anything more."

"Three seconds and a face is more than we've had this whole investigation," Caleb said, though his voice carried none of the relief the words should have.

"If it can push back like that," he said slowly, "if it noticed her watching and reacted, then we're not dealing with dead history anymore, the way I think all three of us were quietly hoping. We're not just uncovering something old and finished, the way we were with Kell's crimes once he was already exposed. We're dealing with something active, aware, and apparently capable of defending itself the moment it feels threatened."

"That's what worries me most," Mara admitted. "Not the physical toll on Wren, though God knows that's bad enough on its own. It's the confirmation that whatever we're closing in on is still capable of reacting to us specifically. We're not investigating a static mystery anymore. We're provoking something, and I don't think we fully understand yet what it's capable of doing when it decides it's been provoked enough."

"Do you think Denise knows," Caleb asked. "Right now, tonight, sitting in her own kitchen. Do you think she felt Wren watching her, the way she seemed to feel it happening in the vision itself, or is that only true of the moment being remembered, not the woman remembering it."

"I don't know," Mara admitted. "I don't know if there's a difference, from her side of it. If the memory can defend itself the way Wren described, I'm not sure it matters whether Denise consciously felt anything tonight or not. Something in her, or something attached to her, already knows we're closer than we've ever been."

The silence that followed carried a different weight than the fear from earlier in the night, something colder and more clear-eyed, the shape of a danger neither of them could pretend, any longer, was still theoretical.

"We go to Priya and Yusuf tomorrow," Caleb said finally. "All of it. Not just the proof Wren found, but the warning that came with it. If this thing can fight back when it's watched too closely, everyone moving forward on this needs to understand exactly what they're actually risking, not just what they might find."

Mara nodded, exhaustion and resolve settling into the same tired weight in her chest. "Tomorrow," she agreed. "Tonight, we just watch her breathe, and be grateful it wasn't worse."

She glanced toward the front room, where Wren's shape was just visible under the pile of blankets, small and still in a way that made her look younger than twenty, younger than any of the burdens this investigation had already asked her to carry. "I keep thinking about the letter," Mara said quietly. "Adelaide, alone with all of this, no one to sit up with her the way we're sitting up with Wren tonight. I don't know how she survived it as long as she did, carrying it entirely by herself."

"She didn't have us," Caleb said. "That's the difference. Whatever happens from here, Wren isn't carrying this alone, and neither are you, and neither am I. Adelaide never had that. I think that might matter more than any of us have given it credit for."

They sat together at the kitchen table until the first grey light came up over the orchard, neither of them quite willing to leave Wren alone long enough to sleep themselves. Once, near dawn, Wren stirred and murmured something unintelligible from the couch, and both of them were on their feet before either had consciously decided to move, standing over her together until her breathing evened back out and she settled again, undisturbed, into a sleep that finally looked like rest rather than exhaustion. Neither of them said anything about it afterward. There wasn't anything left to say that hadn't already been said once tonight, and some fears, Mara had learned, were better carried quietly than spoken twice.

Outside, the valley held its breath the way it had for fifty years, waiting to see what this new generation would finally do with a debt nobody living had ever managed to close.

Mara sat a while longer after Caleb finally dozed off in the chair beside her, watching the light change over the orchard, gray easing slowly into something with color in it, and let herself think, for just a few minutes, about nothing more complicated than the ordinary shape of a morning. The kettle needed filling. The chickens would want letting out soon. Somewhere underneath all of it, a woman she'd known her whole life was still sleeping, or not sleeping, in a kitchen not so different from this one, and Mara found she couldn't yet decide whether she hoped Denise had slept easily last night or not at all.
