CHAPTER SIXTEEN

Priya came back the next evening with a legal pad instead of a hat, and that alone told Mara she'd spent the night working rather than sleeping.

"Three shifts," Priya said, dropping the pad on the kitchen table and dragging a chair around so she could sit across from all three of them at once. "Dawn, midday, dusk. Dusk is the one that matters most, because if the pattern from Ruth's case holds, that's when things happen. But I'm not leaving the other two empty, because we don't actually know that for certain, we just know it for the one case we've confirmed."

Wren leaned over the pad, reading upside down out of habit. "So who's on dusk."

"Nobody's on dusk alone, and nobody's on dusk every night." Priya tapped the paper where she'd already blocked out a grid, days across the top, names down the side, each square filled in her small, cramped handwriting. "I rotate you through it. Two people per shift, always. One watching, one logging. You switch jobs halfway through so nobody's eyes glaze over from doing the same thing for two hours straight."

"You're pairing me with Caleb for dusk on Thursday," Mara said, running a finger down the grid. "And with Wren on Saturday."

"I'm not putting Wren on dusk alone with anybody who isn't you or Caleb, not this early." Priya said it plainly, no apology in it, the same flat certainty she used when she'd decided something wasn't up for negotiation. "If something happens out there, I want at least one of you steady enough to make a call under pressure standing next to her."

Caleb had been quiet, arms crossed, reading the schedule from where he stood behind Mara's chair. "What about you."

"I take the shifts nobody else can cover, and I take every single one where legal exposure is highest." Priya's mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "Meaning if this ever goes in front of a judge, I want my own name on the log more than anyone else's, because I'm the one who can testify to what we saw without anybody asking why a civilian was standing on a public road with binoculars."

"How many of those are there," Wren asked. "The high exposure ones."

"More than I'd like. Any shift where I'm off duty officially and out there anyway. Any shift that overlaps with a night I'm supposed to be somewhere else on the record. I've already told my sergeant I'm working a private matter on my own time, which is true enough not to be a lie, and vague enough that nobody's asked a second question yet."

"And when they do," Caleb said.

"Then I answer them honestly, as far as I'm able to, and we deal with whatever comes of that when it comes." Priya said it without drama, the way she said most hard things, like she'd already decided the cost was worth paying and didn't need to relitigate it out loud every time it came up.

"That's not fair to you," Mara said. "You're already carrying the whole legal weight of this."

"It's not about fair. It's about which of us can survive being cross-examined." Priya slid the pad closer to Mara. "Now. Rules. I need every one of you to actually hear this part, because it's the part that keeps us out of trouble and it's also the part everybody wants to skip when they're bored on a cold night."

Wren settled back onto the counter, tea forgotten. "We're listening."

"Rule one. You watch from the road, and only the road. Not the fence line, not the ditch, not the tree that leans out a little farther than the rest even though I know exactly which tree you're all thinking about right now." Priya looked at Wren specifically when she said it, and Wren had the decency to look briefly caught out. "If you can't see clearly from where you're legally allowed to stand, you don't get closer. You note that visibility was limited and you move on."

"Rule two," Caleb said, half a question.

"Rule two. Nobody goes anywhere alone. Not to the car, not to the outhouse, nowhere. If one of you needs to step away for any reason, the shift pauses until you're both back in position. I don't care how ordinary the reason is. Two sets of eyes, every single time, or the log isn't worth the paper it's written on."

Mara wrote that down on her own copy of the schedule, underlining it twice. "Rule three."

"Rule three is the one that matters most, so I'm going to say it slow." Priya waited until she had all three of them looking at her. "If you see Denise doing anything at all near that well, anything that even looks like what Wren felt in her read, you do not approach. You do not call out to her. You do not try to get a better photograph or a clearer angle by stepping one foot past where you're standing. You log the time, you log exactly what you saw in plain factual language, no interpretation, no adjectives, and you get out of there without letting her know you were watching."

"Why does it matter if she knows," Wren asked. "If we've already got proof."

"Because the second she knows we're watching, she stops." Priya's voice had gone quieter, more careful, the tone she used when she wanted Wren especially to hold still and listen rather than argue. "Fifty years of doing this without getting caught means she's spent fifty years learning what it looks like when somebody's paying attention. If we spook her before we have enough, she doesn't renew the bargain this cycle. She waits. She's patient in a way none of us can afford to be, because we're the ones aging on a normal clock and she isn't. If we tip our hand too early, we don't stop her. We just teach her to be better at hiding, and we lose our best chance in six to eight years."

The room went quiet at that. Everyone was doing the same silent math, what six to eight years actually meant for the people they already knew, for Ruth, for whoever came next if this cycle slipped past them.

"So what's the trigger," Mara asked. "You said before there's a line where documenting stops being enough."

Priya nodded slowly, like she'd been waiting for someone to ask it directly. "If we see her actively performing something at the well that matches what Wren described, hands working fast, something small between her fingers, and there's a second person present who looks like they're in distress or being acted upon, that's the trigger. That's not documentation anymore. That's an emergency in progress, and I call it in as one, and whoever's on shift calls me the second it starts, not after."

"And if it's just her," Caleb said. "Alone at the well, doing whatever it is she does."

"Then we document, and we don't move, and it's the hardest thing any of us will do this whole investigation." Priya looked down at her own hands on the table, turning her pen over once. "Because I know exactly how it's going to feel to stand there and watch a woman potentially take something out of somebody who isn't even present to fight for themselves, and do nothing but write down the time. But if we go running down there without a clear victim in active danger, we don't have a case. We have three trespassers and a woman with fifty years of practice at looking harmless."

Wren slid off the counter and walked to the window, looking out at the dark shape of the orchard, the low hill beyond it where the well sat waiting for whichever night the pattern decided to repeat itself. "What if the trigger happens and I'm the one on shift. What if it's just me and Mara, or me and Caleb, and there's no time to wait for you to drive out."

"Then you call it in exactly the same way, and you still don't approach until I'm there or until there's an active life in danger that can't wait one more minute." Priya's answer came without hesitation, like she'd already turned this exact question over more than once on her own last night. "I know that's a hard line to hold when you're standing right there and everything in you wants to move. That's why it's a rule and not a judgment call. Judgment calls are how good people make the exact mistake that gets a case thrown out."

Caleb pulled a chair around and sat across from Priya, elbows on his knees. "What do you need from us before Thursday."

"I need everyone to actually read the shift grid before you show up, not skim it. I need whoever's logging to write in full sentences, not shorthand, because shorthand gets misread later and misread notes are worse than no notes." Priya slid three copies of the schedule across the table, one for each of them. "And I need you all to understand that most nights, probably most of the next several weeks, nothing is going to happen at all. You're going to sit in a cold car on a dark road and watch an empty field, and it's going to feel like a waste of time right up until the one night it isn't."

Mara folded her copy of the schedule and set it beside the plat she'd already marked with red pins, the map and the grid sitting next to each other like two halves of the same problem finally fitted together. "Thursday, then. Caleb and I take the first dusk shift."

"Thursday," Priya agreed, standing and gathering the legal pad back up. "I'll be parked a quarter mile down, close enough to reach you inside three minutes if the trigger happens, far enough that two cars on the same stretch of road don't look like anything worth noticing."

She let herself out the same way she had the night before, and this time Mara watched her taillights disappear in the other direction, toward whatever quiet spot she'd already chosen to wait from, close but not too close, careful in a way that had cost her something to learn.

Wren was still at the window when Mara turned back around, arms wrapped around herself against a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature in the kitchen. "It's really happening now," she said, not quite a question. "We're actually going to sit out there and watch her."

"We're going to sit out there and watch a field," Mara said, coming to stand beside her. "And hope, for once, that absolutely nothing happens tonight, so that something can happen on the one night we're ready for it."

Caleb came up on Wren's other side, and for a moment the three of them just stood there together, looking out at the dark shape of the hill where an old well waited, patient as anything Denise Voss had ever learned to be, for the night the valley's oldest debt came due again.

"Thursday," Wren said, testing the word like it belonged to somebody else's calendar. "That's not even two days."

"It's exactly enough time to be scared and not enough time to talk ourselves out of it," Caleb said, and there was that dry note in his voice that Mara had learned to listen for, the one that meant he was every bit as unsettled as Wren but had decided years ago that saying so plainly rarely helped anything. "Which might be the right amount, honestly."

Mara folded her arms and let herself lean into him, just slightly, just enough that he'd feel it without either of them having to make anything of it in front of Wren. "Priya's right that most of this is going to be nothing. Hours of nothing. We should be prepared for that too, not just for the one night it isn't."

"I can do nothing," Wren said. "I've gotten pretty good at sitting still and waiting for things, this whole book of my life so far."

"That's not a joke I'm going to laugh at," Mara said, but there was no real edge in it, just tiredness, and something close to pride in the girl who'd grown steadier through all of this even as it asked more of her than any of them wanted it to.

They stood there a while longer, none of them in a hurry to be the first to say it was time to sleep, as if staying up a little later kept Thursday a little farther away than it actually was. Eventually Wren went up to her room, and Caleb banked the stove, and Mara sat back down at the table alone with the schedule and the plat side by side, tracing the four red pins around the well one more time, as if repetition alone might make the pattern hold just once more, on command, for the people finally ready to see it.
