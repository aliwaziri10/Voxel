CHAPTER EIGHTEEN

Wren had taken to spreading the drought file across her bedroom floor at night, long after Mara and Caleb had gone to bed, the county paper gone soft and gray at the corners from how many times she'd handled it in the last few weeks.

She wasn't looking for anything new tonight. She told herself that at the start of every one of these sessions, and every time it turned out to be a lie, because there was always some corner of the file she hadn't pressed hard enough on yet, some name or date she'd skimmed past the first ten times because it hadn't seemed to matter.

The name that had never let her rest was the one on the drought committee's original roster. Denise Voss, age thirty one, listed as a volunteer note taker for the county's 1980s emergency water board, her signature small and neat at the bottom of three separate pages, matching stroke for stroke a sample Wren had gotten off an old church bulletin Denise had signed only last spring.

Same slant on the D. Same closed loop on the V. Forty years apart, and the hand hadn't aged a single stroke.

Wren had known that already, in the way she'd known most of this for weeks now, a certainty sitting under her ribs that didn't need any more proof to feel true. But knowing something and being able to say it out loud to Mara and Caleb and Priya, being able to point at a specific document and say here, this proves the maiden name, this proves the woman in this file and the woman who fed us pie are the same person, that was different, and it was the difference she'd been chasing every night for a week without telling anyone she was still chasing it.

She found it a little after midnight.

Tucked into the back of the file, behind the drought board's meeting minutes, was a marriage record she'd overlooked twice before, filed with the county the same year the drought committee disbanded. Denise Voss to Harold Pruitt. Not the Denise everyone in the valley knew now, no last name of Pruitt attached to her anywhere Wren had ever heard, but there in black and white, a Voss woman marrying into a Pruitt family that Wren already knew from somewhere else in this whole tangled mess.

Walt Pruitt. Dev's old timer. The one who'd joked more than once that Denise never aged, whose own grandmother had warned him never to ask her for a favor without expecting to pay it back double.

Wren sat very still on her bedroom floor, the marriage record trembling slightly in her hands, and worked through what that meant with the same careful patience Mara would have used on a blueprint that had just revealed a support beam nobody had accounted for.

If Denise Voss married a Pruitt in the same year the drought committee disbanded, then Walt Pruitt wasn't just some old timer with a folk story. He was family. Denise's own marriage had put her inside the very family whose oldest son would spend the rest of his life half joking about a woman who never seemed to get any older, without ever quite understanding he'd been married into the answer himself, decades removed and one generation too late to recognize what he was looking at.

She didn't sleep that night. She lay on top of her covers with the file spread out beside her, running the same three documents over and over in her head, the drought roster, the marriage record, the church bulletin, until the shapes of the letters started to blur together into one continuous line connecting a thirty one year old volunteer note taker in 1983 to the woman who'd moved a wooden spoon out of Wren's reach at her own kitchen table not two months ago.

She thought about Ruth Calloway, and the sound of a husband's voice erased in a single ordinary night. She thought about Priya, standing at the substation office weeks ago, saying that a name mattering was different from a name proving anything. She thought about Caleb, and what it would mean for him if this whole chain of paper turned out to be exactly as true as it felt lying here in the dark, that whoever had taken his two years hadn't been some stranger passing through, but a woman who'd fed him pie as a boy and hummed while she did it, close enough the whole time that nobody had ever thought to look at her twice.

The thought made her sick in a way none of the abstractions had, the drought file, the pattern of dates, even the Finder read with its nosebleed and its faceless hands. Paper didn't feel like anything until you let yourself imagine the person on the other end of it, ordinary and warm and patient, waiting fifty years for nobody to ever put these three documents side by side.

She almost told Mara at breakfast. She had the marriage record folded in her back pocket, ready to lay it on the table the way she'd laid the drought file down that very first night, and something stopped her before the words came out. It wasn't fear of being wrong exactly, though that was part of it, some old instinct that had learned the hard way that being certain and being right weren't always the same thing. It was something closer to the weight of finally being the one holding the answer instead of just chasing it, the strange loneliness of knowing something before anyone else in the room did, even the people she trusted most in the world.

"You're quiet this morning," Caleb said, sliding a plate toward her that she hadn't touched.

"Just tired." It wasn't even a lie, not really. "Didn't sleep much."

Mara glanced up from her own coffee with the same close attention she always gave anything that sounded even slightly off in someone she loved, and Wren felt the marriage record in her pocket like a stone she'd swallowed. "Anything you want to talk about?"

"Not yet." Wren met her eyes and held them, trying to make the words sound like a promise rather than a dodge. "I found something last night. I want to check it twice more before I bring it to you both, because if I'm wrong about this one, it's not a small kind of wrong."

Caleb set his coffee down. "That bad."

"I don't know yet if it's bad. I know it's big." Wren pushed her plate away, appetite gone the way it always went when her mind was moving too fast to make room for anything as ordinary as breakfast. "Give me until tonight. I promise I'll tell you both everything, but I need to be sure first, not just sure the way I've been sure about everything else so far. Actually sure. The kind that survives someone else poking holes in it."

Mara studied her for a long moment, the same way she studied a stress point before deciding whether to trust it, and Wren watched something settle in her expression, not full acceptance, but a decision to wait, at least for now.

"Tonight," Mara said. "Not tomorrow. Not because I'm rushing you, but because I don't want you carrying whatever this is alone for one hour longer than you have to."

"Tonight," Wren agreed, and meant it, and spent the rest of the day carrying the marriage record folded in her pocket like a live coal, walking the orchard fence line twice just to have somewhere to put her restless energy, running the whole chain of documents through her head again and again until she'd worn the doubt down to something small enough to survive being spoken out loud.

By late afternoon she'd walked to the county clerk's office on her own, a bicycle ride she hadn't told anyone she was taking, and confirmed the last piece herself, standing at the counter with her heart going too fast while a clerk who didn't know her from any other curious teenager pulled the original certificate for her to see with her own eyes. Denise Voss. Harold Pruitt. The same year. The same hand that had signed the drought roster, aged not a single day in the forty years since.

She rode home slower than she'd ridden out, not because she was tired, though she was, but because some part of her wanted to hold the certainty a little longer before she had to share it and watch it become something bigger than just hers to carry.

Mara was in the kitchen when Wren came through the back door, flour on her hands from bread she'd started without really meaning to, the way she always found some task for her hands when she was worried about someone she loved.

"Tonight," Wren said, before Mara could even ask. "I'm ready now. Where's Caleb."

"Out back, splitting wood he doesn't need to split." Mara wiped her hands on her apron, already moving toward the door to call him in, and something in her face told Wren she'd been carrying her own quiet fear all day too, waiting on the other side of a promise she hadn't wanted to press too hard against.

They sat at the same table where all of it had started, the letter, the drought file, the map with its four red pins, and Wren laid the marriage record down in the center of it like the last card in a hand she'd been holding too long.

"Denise Voss married Harold Pruitt," she said, and watched both of their faces change in real time as the shape of it landed. "The same year the drought committee disbanded. Walt Pruitt's family. Dev's folk story lead. It's not a coincidence anybody could have found by accident. It's the same woman, confirmed, in her own hand, married into the very family that's been half joking about her for two generations without ever understanding what they were actually saying."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Outside, the light was going the same gold and gray it had gone the night of the stakeout, and Wren realized with a small, tired ache that she hadn't felt anything like triumph carrying this news home. Only the same cold certainty she'd felt every night this week, finally set down where the others could see it too.

"You did this alone," Caleb said finally, not quite a question, not quite an accusation either.

"I needed to be sure," Wren said. "I didn't want to hand you both a maybe."

Mara reached across the table and took her hand, flour still dusting her knuckles, and Wren let herself be held there for a moment without needing to say anything else at all.
