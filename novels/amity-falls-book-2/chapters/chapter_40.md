CHAPTER FORTY

Caleb woke before dawn, the way he had every morning that week, lying still in the gray early light while two years of returned memory continued settling into place inside him, piece by piece, like sediment finally finding the bottom of a long-disturbed lake.

Mara stirred beside him, half-awake. "Another one?"

"The chapel again," he said quietly. "Different morning than before. I was reading something. I can't tell what, the book itself hasn't come back yet, just the feeling of sitting there with it, needing somewhere quiet that wasn't home."

She reached for his hand under the blankets, saying nothing, simply staying present the way she had every morning since the well, and Caleb found himself grateful all over again for a patience he didn't think he could have offered anyone else in her position.

He got up eventually, made coffee he didn't really want, and sat at the kitchen table with the small leather notebook he'd started keeping the day after the ritual, recording each fragment as it returned before it could slip away again into whatever quiet corner of himself it had been hiding.

The umbrella outside the grocer's. The old woman's voice, warmer even in memory than he'd expected from what he now knew about her. His mother's phone call, the worry in it, missing her before he'd even fully lost the memory of talking to her that day. Nights at this same kitchen table, working through something with Mara before either of them had said the word love out loud yet, though looking back now, he could see it plainly, sitting right there underneath every conversation they'd had.

And underneath all of it, slowly emerging as the days passed, the thing he'd been most afraid to remember: why he'd been grieving in the first place, that year, before Denise had ever found him standing in the rain outside the grocer's with nowhere else to be.

His father.

It came back fully three days after the well, arriving all at once instead of in fragments, and Caleb had to set his coffee down carefully before his hands started shaking too badly to hold it. His father's heart, failing quietly for months before anyone caught it. A hospital room he'd sat in for six days straight. The unbearable silence of the morning after, the house suddenly too large and too quiet, no one left in it who understood what he needed without him having to ask.

"I remember why," he told Mara that evening, his voice rough with two years of grief finally allowed to be felt all the way through instead of held at a strange, unexplained distance. "I remember my dad."

Mara didn't say anything right away. She simply crossed the room and held him, letting him cry the way he hadn't been able to properly cry the first time, eight years ago, grief interrupted halfway through by a woman who'd needed his loss as cover for her own.

"I'm sorry," he said eventually, into her shoulder. "I'm sorry it took this long. I'm sorry I never even knew there was something missing to grieve properly."

"You don't have to apologize for that," Mara said. "None of this was ever your fault."

"I know." He pulled back slightly, wiping his eyes, something steadier settling into his expression even through the rawness. "I think I actually know that now, in a way I didn't before. It's strange. I spent eight years half-wondering if I'd done something wrong, lost something through my own carelessness. Finding out it was taken from me, deliberately, by someone else, it doesn't make the grief hurt less. But it makes it mine again. Uncomplicated by wondering if I somehow failed to hold onto it myself."

They sat together at the kitchen table a while longer, Caleb's notebook open between them, two years of a life slowly reassembling itself into something whole, and Mara found herself, not for the first time since the well, quietly grateful for a version of grief that got to be simple now, honest, no longer tangled up with a stranger's stolen kindness disguised as cover.

"There's something else," Caleb said eventually, closing the notebook gently. "Something I keep thinking about, now that I have all of it back."

"What's that."

"I understand her a little better now," he said slowly, clearly choosing the words with care. "Not forgiving her. I don't think I'm there, and I don't know if I ever will be fully. But I understand, in a way I couldn't before, what it might have felt like to carry someone else's grief for eight years, quietly, alone, the way she carried mine. I have two years of memory that I know now belonged to her too, in some strange way, sitting inside her mind this whole time alongside her own life. I don't know what to do with understanding someone that clearly without forgiving them completely. I don't think those two things have to arrive together."

Mara considered that for a long moment, remembering her own conversation with Denise weeks ago, about the difference between forgiveness and simply choosing to stand beside someone anyway. "Maybe they don't," she said finally. "Maybe understanding gets to come first, and forgiveness, if it ever comes at all, gets to arrive whenever it's actually ready, on its own time, instead of because anyone's waiting for it."

Caleb nodded slowly, something settling in him that looked, for the first time in the eight years Mara had known him, entirely at peace. "I think I'd like to visit her," he said. "Not today. Maybe not for a while yet. But eventually. I think there's something I need to say to her directly, once I know exactly what it is."

"Whenever you're ready," Mara said. "I'll go with you, if you want me there."

"I'd like that," Caleb said, and reached for her hand across the table, two years and eight years and a lifetime of ordinary mornings finally, entirely, his own again.
