CHAPTER SIX

Denise Voss's kitchen smelled the way it had smelled for as long as Mara could remember, which was to say it smelled like every good thing that had ever happened in Amity Falls at once: cinnamon and browned butter and the deep sweetness of stewed apples that hadn't quite finished cooking down. Mara had eaten pie in this kitchen at eleven years old, at sixteen, at her own engagement party, and every single time she'd walked out feeling lighter than when she walked in.

She hated, now, how much that fact worked against her.

"You're not here for the pie," Denise said, setting two plates down anyway, "though I notice you haven't turned it away."

"I never turn away pie," Mara said. "It would be disrespectful to the pie."

Denise laughed, a real laugh, the kind that came from somewhere low in her chest and crinkled the skin at the corners of her eyes. She looked, as she always looked, like somebody's favorite aunt: soft around the middle, hair gone gray in a way that suited her, hands that had spent fifty years kneading dough and canning peaches and patting the shoulders of grieving widows at funerals. She looked like safety. That was the whole problem.

Wren sat at the end of the table with her hands wrapped around a mug of tea she wasn't drinking, watching Denise with the careful, unreadable attention she'd learned from two years of being the valley's only trained Finder. Mara had told her, before they came, to keep her gift quiet. Don't touch anything you don't have to touch. Just watch.

"How's married life treating you?" Denise asked, sliding into the chair across from Mara like she had all the time in the world, like there was nothing on earth she'd rather be doing than sitting in her own kitchen with two guests who'd come, though she didn't know it, to see if she was the reason a woman named Ruth Calloway couldn't remember her dead husband's voice.

"Good," Mara said. "Quiet, mostly. Caleb's still catching up on repairs he put off for the wedding."

"That man could put off breathing if there was a good enough reason." Denise smiled, fond, easy. "I remember him as a boy, you know. Before you ever looked twice at him. Serious little thing, always fixing something that didn't need fixing yet, just so it wouldn't need fixing later. I used to tell his mother, that boy's going to marry an engineer someday, mark my words, because he already thinks like one."

It was such a small, warm memory that Mara almost missed the flicker underneath it: the tiniest hesitation, half a breath too long, right before *before you ever looked twice at him.* As if Denise had started to say something else first, something closer to the truth, and swallowed it in favor of the version that sounded better.

Mara filed it away and kept her face pleasant. "You remember everyone as a child. I think you've told me a story like that about half the valley."

"Living this long in one place, you either remember everyone as a child or you go a little mad from how much you've forgotten." Denise pushed the pie plate an inch closer to Wren. "Eat something, sweetheart. You look like you haven't slept."

"Been reading a lot," Wren said, which was, strictly speaking, true.

"Anything good?"

"County records, mostly." Wren said it lightly, watching Denise's face the way she'd watch a held breath for the moment it broke. "Old weather stuff. Drought years."

Denise's expression didn't change at all, which was, in its own way, more unsettling than if it had. She simply nodded, unbothered, and said, "Lord, the eighties drought. I remember that one clear as anything. Whole valley prayed for rain like we'd forgotten how to do anything else." She said it with the easy fondness of someone remembering a hard year that had, in the end, turned out fine. "Your grandfather worked himself half to death trying to figure out why it hit us and not the next county over, Mara. Never did find an answer he was happy with, God rest him."

"No," Mara agreed carefully. "He never did."

There was a version of this conversation, Mara realized, that could go on forever, warm and circling and utterly without edges, because Denise had fifty years of practice turning every question into a story that ended in comfort. It was, Mara thought, probably how she'd survived this long doing whatever she'd been doing. Not through secrecy exactly. Through sheer, overwhelming, generous charm, so total that suspicion felt like an act of cruelty against her.

Wren shifted in her seat, and Mara caught the small, deliberate movement of her hand toward the edge of the table, close to where Denise's own hand rested near an old wooden spoon, worn smooth with decades of use. Mara gave the smallest shake of her head. Not yet. Not here.

Wren pulled her hand back into her lap.

"You two didn't come all this way just to ask an old woman about the weather," Denise said, and there was nothing sharp in it, nothing accusing, just the easy observation of someone who'd been reading people over pie for half a century. "What's actually on your mind?"

Mara had rehearsed three different versions of this moment on the walk over, and in the end she went with the truest one she was willing to spend. "Wren's been putting together a project on valley history. The old families. I thought you'd have stories nobody's written down."

"Oh, I've got stories nobody's written down on purpose," Denise said, and laughed again, and for one unguarded second Mara let herself simply enjoy it, the warmth of the kitchen and the smell of apples and the sound of a woman who had, by every visible measure, given the valley nothing but comfort for as long as anyone could remember.

Then Denise reached across the table to refill Wren's tea, and her sleeve pulled back an inch, and Mara saw, just below the crease of her wrist, a thin white scar shaped almost like a closed circle. It was the kind of old scar that could have come from anything: a burn, a childhood accident, a hundred harmless explanations. But Wren's eyes had gone to it too, and something in her posture changed, some small tightening Mara had learned to recognize over the last two years as the moment before her gift wanted to reach for something whether she gave it permission or not.

"Wren," Mara said, quiet but firm.

Wren blinked, and the moment passed, and Denise pulled her sleeve back down without seeming to notice anything had happened at all.

"You'll have to come back another day when I've got more time," Denise said, standing to clear the plates though neither of them had finished. "I could talk your ears off about the old families till Christmas. Right now I promised Odette I'd bring her supper, and that woman does not forgive lateness."

"Of course," Mara said, standing too, matching Denise's easy tone with an effort that cost her more than she wanted to admit. "Thank you for the pie."

"Thank you for eating it. Nothing sadder than a pie nobody wants." Denise walked them to the door, one hand briefly resting on Wren's shoulder in a gesture that looked, to anyone watching, like nothing more than simple affection. "You take care of yourself, sweetheart. You look tired in a way that worries me a little."

"I'm fine," Wren said.

"I'm sure you are." Denise smiled, and it was warm, and it was kind, and Mara could not, for the life of her, find one single thing wrong with it, and that was exactly what frightened her most as she and Wren walked back down the porch steps into the cooling evening air.

Neither of them spoke until they were well past the edge of the Voss property, the old fence line and the dark shape of the well beyond it fading behind them into the dusk.

"You saw it," Wren said finally.

"I saw a scar."

"You saw the pause. Before she talked about Caleb." Wren's voice had lost its careful lightness, gone taut and quick the way it did when she was working something out in real time. "She almost said something else. I don't know what. But it wasn't the version she gave us."

Mara kept walking, her thoughts already arranging themselves into columns the way they always did when the world got too uncertain to hold any other way. A pause. A scar. Fifty years of stories with no hard edges. None of it was proof. All of it, together, in the wrong light, could look like exactly what Wren's readings had been pointing toward for weeks.

"It's not enough," Mara said, more to herself than to Wren.

"I know it's not enough." Wren pulled her jacket tighter against the evening chill. "But it's not nothing either. And she felt it. When I almost reached for that spoon. She felt me want to, even before I did anything. Like she's used to people almost finding out and stopping themselves."

Mara thought about the warmth of that kitchen, the easy laugh, the hand resting so lightly and so naturally on Wren's shoulder, and felt something cold settle low in her stomach that no amount of pie could ever have been sweet enough to chase away.

"Let's get home," she said. "Caleb will want to hear all of this. Every piece of it, exactly as it happened, before either of us starts deciding what it means."

They walked the rest of the way in silence, the valley settling into evening around them, ordinary and quiet and utterly unaware that somewhere behind a warm kitchen window, a woman who had fed half the town at one point or another was clearing two plates of unfinished pie and thinking her own private, unreadable thoughts.

Caleb was on the porch when they got back, sleeves rolled to the elbow, halfway through resetting a hinge on the screen door that had been sagging since before the wedding. He looked up once, took in both their faces, and set the screwdriver down without being asked.

"That bad," he said.

"That nothing," Mara said, climbing the steps. "Which might be worse."

He held the door for them and followed them inside, and Mara told it the way she always told him things, in order, without embellishment, starting with the pie and ending with the scar. Wren filled in the parts Mara skimmed past too quickly, the exact wording of the almost-sentence about Caleb as a boy, the precise way Denise's hand had drifted to the spoon a half second before Wren's had.

Caleb listened the whole way through without interrupting, which was its own kind of answer. When Mara finished, he leaned back against the counter and rubbed a hand over his jaw.

"She talked about me," he said finally. "As a kid."

"She did. It sounded real. It sounded exactly like every other story she's ever told about half this valley." Mara sat down heavily at the table. "That's what makes it so hard to hold onto. Nothing she said was wrong. Nothing she did was strange. If I described this afternoon to anyone else in town, it would sound like an old woman being kind to two young people who came looking for stories."

"But you don't think that's all it was."

"I think," Mara said slowly, "that fifty years of being loved by an entire valley is the best possible place to hide something. Nobody looks hard at the person everybody already trusts. We don't. We never have. That's not an accident. I think she's built exactly the kind of life that makes this conversation feel absurd even while we're having it."

Caleb was quiet for a moment, working something over. "The scar," he said. "Could be anything."

"It could be anything," Wren agreed. "Burn, old surgery, a hundred things. I've got a scar on my knee from falling off a fence when I was nine. Doesn't mean anything happened to me that I'm hiding."

"But you wanted to touch it."

Wren didn't answer right away, and when she did, her voice had gone smaller than Mara had heard it in weeks. "I wanted to touch the spoon. Old, worn thing, sitting right there by her hand. Something that old has been held a thousand times, it would have given me something. And the second I thought about reaching for it, before I moved at all, she pulled it closer to herself. Not fast. Not obvious. Just enough that I couldn't have gotten to it without asking."

"Maybe she was just tidying," Caleb offered, though without much conviction behind it.

"Maybe," Wren said. "But I've spent two years learning to read rooms even when I'm not reading objects. And that room felt like it was being managed. Every question we asked got turned into a story with a soft landing. Every silence got filled before it could stretch long enough to feel uncomfortable. I don't know if that's guilt. Could just be a woman who's spent her whole life being the person who smooths things over. But it felt practiced in a way I didn't expect."

Mara thought about that word, practiced, and found she couldn't shake it loose from where it had lodged. Denise had practiced kindness the way some people practiced a trade, until it stopped looking like effort at all and started looking like nature. That alone wasn't evidence of anything. People who ran bakeries and sat with the grieving and remembered every child's name in three generations of families weren't automatically suspects. But if you needed somewhere to hide something for half a century, you could not design a better hiding place than that.

"So where does this leave us," Caleb said. "We can't walk up to the council with a pause in a sentence and an old scar."

"No," Mara agreed. "We can't. We need something that isn't a feeling. Something with a date on it, or a name, or a document that doesn't move when you push on it." She looked at Wren. "You said you weren't ready to tell anyone else what you found in the records. Priya included."

Wren's jaw tightened, just slightly. "I wasn't sure it meant anything yet."

"I think it's time it did," Mara said. "Not because I doubt what you found. Because we need someone in this who thinks about evidence for a living, someone who isn't already halfway convinced the way the three of us are. If Priya looks at this and sees nothing worth pursuing, that tells us something too."

Caleb nodded slowly. "And if she sees what we see?"

"Then we're not three people with a feeling anymore," Mara said. "We're four people building a case. That's a different thing entirely, and it's the only kind of thing that's ever going to survive being said out loud in front of Yusuf."

Outside, the last of the light was draining out of the sky over the orchard, and somewhere down the road, in a warm kitchen that smelled like cinnamon and browned butter, a woman who had spent fifty years being loved without question went on quietly living the only life anyone in Amity Falls had ever known her to have.
