CHAPTER TWO

Wren didn't tell Mara what she was planning to do with the envelope until she'd already done it.

It happened on a Thursday, three days after the kitchen table conversation neither of them had finished, in the back room of the co-op that had become, without anyone officially deciding it should, the closest thing the valley had to a Finder's workshop. A single table. A locked cabinet Yusuf had approved without asking too many questions about what went in it. A window that faced the orchard, so that on clear afternoons the light came in gold and made the whole cramped little room feel less like what it actually was, which was a place where a twenty-year-old girl taught herself to survive touching things that didn't want to be touched.

She had the envelope in front of her for twenty minutes before she picked it up.

Adelaide Whitlock's handwriting stared back at her from the single folded page, the ink faded to a brown that was almost the same color as the paper itself, and Wren had read the words on it enough times now that she could have recited them without looking. That wasn't what she wanted from it. Words were easy. Words were what Adelaide had chosen to leave behind, filtered and controlled and written for a stranger she'd never meet, decades of careful editing behind every sentence. What Wren wanted was underneath that, the thing objects held onto that people couldn't help leaving in them, the part that didn't get edited because the person who left it there never knew it was still there to find.

She'd done small reads for months now. An inherited ring. A church pew. A child's shoe found in the woods that had turned out to belong to nobody frightening at all, just a boy who'd lost it running from his own dog. Ordinary things, worked carefully, always with someone else in the room, always with a hard stop the moment her nose started to run.

There was nobody else in the room today. She'd made sure of that.

She closed her hand around the letter and let herself go under.

It came fast and wrong, the way the bad ones always did, less like memory and more like weather, something that happened to her rather than something she witnessed. Cold first. A cold that had nothing to do with the valley's actual winters, a cold that lived in the chest instead of the skin. Then a kitchen, older than any kitchen Wren had ever stood in, low ceiling, a woodstove instead of the co-op's electric range, and a woman's hands, shaking, folding a page along a crease Wren could feel forming under her own fingers eighty years too late to matter.

*I don't know if this is the right thing to do.* Not words exactly. Closer to the shape a thought makes right before it becomes words, raw and unfinished. *I don't know if telling anyone makes it worse for them or better. I only know that not telling anyone has already made it worse for me, every year, a little more, and I can't carry the rest of it alone anymore. If I'm wrong to leave this, God forgive me. If I'm right, I hope whoever finds it is braver than I was.*

Fear under all of it, threaded so deep into the paper it had outlasted the woman who'd folded it. Not fear of dying. Wren understood that much clearly, the one thing she could pull whole and certain out of the fog. Fear of being believed too late. Fear of a name she wasn't writing down, deliberately, protectively, a name Wren reached for and couldn't quite close her hand around before the whole vision buckled sideways and let her go.

She came back to herself on the floor of the co-op's back room, the letter still clutched in one fist, her nose already bleeding freely onto her shirt, and Yusuf's assistant Marisol standing in the doorway with an expression somewhere between alarm and the particular weary patience the valley had developed over the last year toward anyone who did what Wren did for a living.

"How long was I out," Wren managed.

"Ninety seconds. Maybe two minutes." Marisol was already crouching beside her with the little emergency kit that lived permanently in the cabinet now, gauze and water and the specific brand of electrolyte drink that had turned out, through trial and error nobody wanted to repeat, to help more than anything else. "You know you're not supposed to do these alone."

"I know." Wren let her tip her head back, let the gauze do its work. "I got a name. Almost. It was right there."

"Whose?"

"I don't know yet. She was protecting it, even in the memory, even eighty years after the fact. Whoever she was scared of, she didn't want them found by accident." Wren closed her eyes against the ache building behind them, the particular exhausted hollowness a hard read always left, worse this time than she'd expected for a piece of paper instead of a person's actual possession. "But I got the fear. I got that it wasn't fear of dying. It was fear that nobody would believe her in time."

She thought about Denise's face at the wedding, warm and unbothered and entirely, convincingly ordinary, and felt something cold settle into the space the vision had left behind.

---

Mara found out about it that evening, not from Wren, who would have downplayed the whole thing out of some combination of pride and not wanting to be told she'd done something reckless, but from Marisol, who caught her outside the diner and asked, with careful casualness, whether Wren was doing okay after that read this afternoon.

She was at the co-op within ten minutes.

Wren was still there, working through a second cup of the electrolyte drink with the particular sullen patience of someone who knew a lecture was coming and had decided to get ahead of it by looking as pathetic as possible. It didn't work.

"You did a solo read. On the letter. Without telling me."

"I got something," Wren said, instead of arguing the point, which was itself an admission. "Not a name. Fear, though. Real fear, and not the kind you'd expect from someone who thought this was going to be a clean, noble sacrifice. She was scared someone was going to hurt her before anyone believed what she was trying to say. That's not the fear of a woman about to make a bargain she thought would end things. That's the fear of a woman who already knew, going in, that whatever she was up against had power to spare and time on its side."

Mara sat down across from her, some of the anger draining out into something closer to the exhaustion that had become her default state since the wedding. "That doesn't sound like it's describing Kell. Kell didn't have decades of runway. He was found out inside a year of the drought file even existing."

"It's not describing Kell." Wren met her eyes, steady despite the dried blood still faintly visible at one nostril. "I think it's describing whoever came before him. Or whoever's still coming after."

Caleb found them there twenty minutes later, having apparently deduced from Mara's absence at dinner and a single text that said *co-op, come when you can* that whatever the two of them had been circling for the past week had finally come to a head. He didn't ask what he'd missed. He pulled up a third chair, set a paper bag of sandwiches from the diner on the table between them, the kind of gesture Mara had learned meant he was settling in for however long this took rather than expecting a summary.

"Tell me the whole thing," he said. "Not the careful version. The whole thing."

So Mara did. The letter. The drought file. Denise Voss, age thirty-one in 1981, alive and unaged and beloved in a valley that had no idea what it might actually be looking at every time it accepted a slice of pie from her hands.

Caleb was quiet for a long moment after she finished, turning his coffee cup slowly on the table, and when he finally spoke his voice had none of the shock Mara had braced for.

"I've known Denise my whole life," he said. "She taught me to swim. She was at my parents' funeral, both of them, years apart, and she brought the same casserole dish both times because she said it was bad luck to buy a new one for grief." He shook his head slowly. "If you'd told me a year ago that this valley was capable of hiding something like Kell inside a man I respected, I'd have said the same thing about him that I'm about to say about her. That it's not possible. That I'd know."

"You didn't know about Kell," Mara said gently.

"No," he agreed. "I didn't." He looked up, and there was something new in his expression now, something harder than the careful patience he'd been offering her all week. "Which is exactly why I think we have to actually look this time, instead of trusting what we think we already know about someone we love. We did that once already. I'm not doing it twice."

Wren pushed the drought file across the table toward him, the last piece of caution any of them had been holding onto finally, quietly, giving way.

"Then let's look," she said.
