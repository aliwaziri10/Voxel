CHAPTER THIRTY-SEVEN

The golden light steadied into something almost gentle, pulsing evenly now around the circle instead of lurching toward whichever hand it found weakest, and Mara felt the strange, sorrowful weight settling into her own mind finally slow to something she could actually bear, foreign grief taking up quiet residence alongside her own thoughts rather than crashing through them.

"It's holding," Yusuf said, wonder cutting through the strain still audible in his voice. "It's actually holding steady."

"Don't relax yet," Denise warned, though even she sounded less frantic now, her own face easing from its earlier rictus of concentration into something closer to careful hope. "We're not finished. There's still more to release, and I don't know what happens in the final moment, when the last of it lets go."

Mara held on, feeling the light pulse warm and steady against her palms, and beside her she felt Caleb go suddenly, sharply rigid.

"Caleb?" Her voice cracked with fresh fear, but when she glanced sideways at him, his face wasn't twisted in pain the way Wren's had been. It was something else entirely, something closer to shock, his eyes wide and unfocused, fixed on some middle distance none of the rest of them could see.

"I remember," he said, barely above a whisper, his hand trembling against the stone rim but not from strain. "Mara, I remember. A rainstorm. Standing outside the grocer's, soaked through, and a stranger, an old woman, handing me her umbrella and telling me it would pass, that everything always passes eventually, even the worst of it." His breath caught. "I remember crying in the truck afterward. I remember why."

"Let it come," Denise said gently, her voice steady despite everything still happening around them. "That's exactly what this is supposed to do. What was taken from you specifically is finding its way back to you specifically, now that it's not locked inside just me anymore."

More came, slowly, in pieces, Caleb's breath catching again and again as fragments of two lost years surfaced one by one: a late night at the kitchen table with Mara before they were even officially courting, working through grief neither of them had fully named yet. A phone call from his mother, her voice steady with worry, asking if he was eating enough, sleeping enough, being kind enough to himself in a season that clearly wasn't kind to him. A quiet Sunday morning at the old chapel, sitting alone in the back pew, not quite praying, just sitting with something too heavy to carry standing up.

"They're mine again," Caleb said, and this time his voice broke entirely, tears finally spilling over as two years of scattered, quietly grieved life settled back into the place they'd always belonged. "Mara, they're actually mine again."

Mara wanted desperately to reach for him, to pull him into her arms the way every instinct screamed at her to, but her hand stayed locked against the stone, the ritual not yet finished, the golden light still pulsing steady and warm around all six of them.

"Stay with it," she said instead, her own voice thick with emotion she didn't have room to fully feel yet. "We finish this together. Then I'll hold you as long as you need."

Around the circle, the others were experiencing their own quieter versions of the same thing, Mara realized, watching Priya's face soften with something like recognition, watching Dev's jaw tighten around some private grief he'd apparently just been handed to carry, watching Yusuf's eyes close briefly around what looked, for just a moment, like his own mother's memory settling somewhere gentle inside him.

"It's nearly finished," Denise said, her voice growing fainter now, not with strain but with something closer to relief, the light around her thinning steadily as the weight she'd carried alone for fifty years continued its slow, deliberate migration outward into six willing hands. "I can feel it. The last of it, letting go."

"What happens to you," Wren asked, her own voice still shaky but steadier now, "once it's all released. What happens to the part that was never a memory at all. The part that just kept you alive this long."

Denise's smile, when it came, held something Mara had never seen on her face before, not once, in all the years of pie and warmth and careful, practiced charm: something that looked, unmistakably, like peace.

"I suppose we're about to find out," she said. "Together, the way this was always supposed to be carried, if only Adelaide had known enough people brave enough to ask."

The golden light pulsed one final time, bright and warm and absolute, and then, all at once, it began to fade.
