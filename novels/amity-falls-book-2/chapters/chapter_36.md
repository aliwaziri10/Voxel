CHAPTER THIRTY-SIX

Wren screamed first, a sound torn out of her that Mara had never heard from her before, not during the pocket watch, not during any of the readings that had left her nose bleeding and her hands shaking, and Mara's whole body went cold with a fear sharper than anything she'd felt since this began.

"It's pulling toward her," Yusuf shouted over the rising roar of golden light, his own arm visibly straining against the well's rim, as though something invisible had hold of his hand and was trying to drag it somewhere he didn't want it to go. "It's not dividing evenly, it's finding whoever's weakest and pulling everything toward them!"

"Weakest how," Priya demanded, her own voice tight with strain, sweat beading on her forehead despite the cool night air.

"Physically," Denise gasped, her eyes still fixed on Wren with something close to horror. "She's the youngest. Her body's already spent more of itself on this than any of yours, the reads, the nosebleeds, everything she's already given trying to understand this before tonight. The well doesn't care about fairness. It only cares about the easiest channel, and we made her the easiest channel without meaning to."

"Wren, let go!" Caleb's voice cracked with terror, but Wren's hand stayed locked against the stone, whether from her own stubborn refusal or something the well itself had done to hold her there, Mara couldn't tell, and the golden light around her thinned and darkened at the edges, concentrating, gathering, the exact failure Mara had feared and never managed to solve.

"I can't," Wren gasped, tears streaming down her face, her whole body shaking now with the effort of something Mara could only half understand. "It's not letting me, it's already inside, I can feel all of it, Mara, all of it, fifty years, it's trying to put fifty years into me at once."

"No." Mara's mind raced, the shape of the failure suddenly, horribly clear, the exact structural collapse she'd warned about in Denise's kitchen two nights ago, the load finding its path of least resistance regardless of how many channels they'd dug to prevent it. "It's not evenly distributed because we never built anything to force it to be. We just hoped."

"Denise!" Priya's voice, sharp with command even through her own visible strain. "Fix this, now, whatever it takes!"

Denise's eyes were open now, wide with horror, her own hand pressed flat against the stone as she fought visibly against a current none of the rest of them could see directly. "I'm trying. I've never done this. I don't know how to redirect what's already redirecting itself."

"Then give it something else to pull toward," Mara said, thinking faster than she'd ever thought in her life, the engineer in her screaming at the obvious, brutal solution even as every other part of her recoiled from it. "If it's finding the weakest point in the system, we make ourselves the strongest point instead. Everyone, hold tighter, not looser. Fight for your share instead of just standing here letting it choose for you."

"That's not how consent works," Dev said, his voice tight with fear even as his hand tightened obediently against the stone.

"It's not about consent anymore, it's about physics," Mara snapped, gripping the well's edge with both hands now, feeling the golden light surge up her arms in response, foreign grief slamming into her chest hard enough to steal her breath. "Wren already consented. She's just the weakest link in the chain right now, and if we don't actively pull our share away from her, this collapses exactly the way Denise warned us it could."

Priya's grip on the stone shifted, her knuckles white, and Mara watched her deliberately widen her stance, bracing like someone about to take a physical blow rather than an emotional one. "Tell me how. Tell me what pulling actually means, because I'm not a Finder, I don't know how to reach for something I can't see."

"Want it," Denise said, her voice ragged but suddenly urgent, some old certainty breaking through the panic. "That's all any of this has ever been. Wanting something enough that it comes to you instead of being taken. Priya, think of the thing you offered at my table. Reach for it the way you'd reach for someone falling. Claim it."

Priya's jaw tightened, and Mara watched something shift in her posture, a visible act of will settling over her shoulders, and the golden light around the circle flickered, brightened unevenly, a fresh thread of it breaking off from the mass still hammering into Wren and arcing instead toward Priya's outstretched hand.

Yusuf followed a half second later, his eyes screwed shut, lips moving around words Mara couldn't hear, and another thread peeled away from Wren's overwhelmed share, settling into him with a visible shudder that ran the length of his whole body.

Caleb moved before anyone else could, releasing one hand from the well only long enough to grab Wren's shoulder, physically bracing her upright as her knees began to buckle under the weight pouring into her. "Wren, look at me. You are not carrying this alone, do you hear me? Pull back. Actively pull back, the way Mara said. Fight it."

Wren's eyes, wild and terrified, found his, and something in her seemed to steady, some old, familiar stubbornness surfacing even through the pain. "Fight it," she repeated, gritted through clenched teeth, and Mara watched her visibly brace, shoulders squaring despite everything, hand gripping the stone with sudden, fierce intention instead of helpless endurance.

The golden light shuddered, flickered, and for one terrible moment seemed to gather itself for something worse.

Then, slowly, agonizingly, it began to redistribute.

Mara felt it happening inside her own chest, a fresh wave of foreign grief settling hard and sudden into spaces that had been empty a moment before, and around the circle she heard the others gasp in unison, felt rather than saw six people simultaneously absorbing pieces of weight that had, seconds before, been rushing entirely toward one girl who couldn't bear it alone.

"That's it," Denise gasped, her own strain visibly easing as the light around her began, at last, to thin toward something manageable. "That's exactly it. You're forcing it to divide instead of hoping it would."

"Keep pulling," Mara ground out through her own gathering pain, foreign memory after foreign memory settling into her mind like stones dropped into still water, each one someone else's precious, irreplaceable loss now becoming, impossibly, partly her own to carry. "Everyone, keep pulling your own share. Don't let it choose the easy path again."

Around the well, six people held on, fighting now instead of simply offering, forcing a fifty-year weight to divide the way it had never once, in a hundred years, actually been divided before, while Wren, shaking but upright, Caleb's hand still steady on her shoulder, found her own strength returning piece by piece as the crushing flood finally, mercifully, began to ease.

The golden light dimmed, slowly, unevenly, settling at last into six separate threads instead of one overwhelming torrent, each thread pulsing faintly against its own bearer's chest like something finally finding the place it was meant to rest.

Mara felt hers settle last, a quiet, sorrowful weight sinking into the space behind her ribs, gentler now than the first violent wave had been, almost peaceful in comparison. She could feel it clearly, a stranger's Sunday afternoon, the exact slant of light through a kitchen window that wasn't her own, and beneath it, fainter, something that felt unmistakably like Denise's own private grief over what she'd spent fifty years being forced to become.

Beside her, Caleb had gone very still, his free hand pressed flat against his own chest, eyes wide with something between disbelief and wonder. "I feel them," he said, voice cracking. "The two years. Not all of them, not clearly, but pieces. A summer. A conversation I don't fully remember having. Something's coming back."

"It's not everything," Denise said quietly, her own voice hoarse with exhaustion, the golden light around her finally gone entirely, leaving only ordinary dusk and an old woman standing weak-kneed at the edge of a well that had, for the first time in a century, released more than it had ever managed to take back. "I don't think it can be everything, not all at once, not safely. But it's a beginning. More than I ever managed to give any of them before tonight."

Wren sagged against Caleb's supporting arm, breathing hard, blood trickling faintly from one nostril but nothing like the collapse Mara had feared moments before. "I felt it try to take all of it," she said, voice shaking. "For a second I thought I was going to disappear under it. And then all of you were just there, pulling, and it stopped being just mine to survive."

"That was never supposed to happen to you alone," Mara said, crouching beside her, checking her pulse out of old habit even though there was nothing practical left for her hands to do. "I'm sorry. I should have seen that coming. I should have built something into the plan to protect you specifically, instead of assuming even distribution would simply happen because we wanted it to."

"You couldn't have known," Wren said, managing something close to a tired, crooked smile. "None of us could. That's what tonight was. The first time. Nobody gets to know the shape of a first time in advance."

Priya knelt beside Dev, both of them breathing hard, their hands still loosely intertwined where they'd gripped the stone together through the worst of it. "I feel her," Priya said quietly, wonder plain in her voice despite the exhaustion. "Not all of it. Just a thread. Something warm and worried, a woman standing at her own kitchen window every single morning of her life, terrified someone would finally see what she was carrying. I think that's the piece I offered to carry. Her fear, instead of one specific loss."

Yusuf had sunk down onto the grass entirely, head bowed, shoulders shaking with something that might have been grief or relief or both at once. "My mother," he said, so quietly Mara almost missed it. "I can feel the shape of what Denise carried for her, those six weeks. Not the dying itself. The love underneath it. The reason Denise never once complained about sitting there every night while I hid outside." He looked up, eyes wet, meeting Denise's exhausted gaze across the well. "Thank you. For finally letting me carry some part of that instead of just resenting you for having carried it alone."

Denise's legs gave out then, slowly, and Caleb caught her before she could fall, lowering her carefully onto the grass beside the well she'd stood at for fifty years of solitary renewals. Up close, in the fading light, Mara thought she looked different somehow, not younger, nothing so simple as that, but lighter, the old heaviness that had always sat behind her eyes finally, visibly, eased.

"Is it done," Mara asked quietly, kneeling beside her. "Truly done. No more renewals, no more six-to-eight-year debts waiting to collect."

"I don't fully know," Denise admitted, her voice barely above a whisper now, utterly spent. "I can feel that something fundamental has changed. The pull that's driven every decision of my life for fifty years, the constant, quiet demand at the edge of everything I've ever done, it's gone quiet in a way I've never once felt before, not even in the calmest months between renewals." She managed a small, exhausted smile. "Whether that means it's finished, or simply resting, differently than it's ever rested before, I think only time is going to tell us that."

"Then we watch," Priya said, still catching her own breath, Dev's hand still loosely folded in hers. "Together. However long it takes to be sure."

Around the well, six people and one old woman sat in the gathering dark, each of them carrying, now, some small honest piece of a weight that had once belonged to only one person alone, and for the first time in longer than any of them could easily measure, the valley around them felt, however briefly, entirely at peace.
