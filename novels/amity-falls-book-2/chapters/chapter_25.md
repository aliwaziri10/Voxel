CHAPTER TWENTY-FIVE

The grange hall was mostly dark by the time they reached it, only Yusuf's office window still lit, a single amber square against the building's long black outline. Mara had walked past this building her whole life, potlucks and harvest meetings and the occasional wedding reception spilling warm and loud out of it into the parking lot, and she couldn't remember ever once seeing it look like this: hollow, waiting, a place built for gatherings standing empty except for the one man inside who now had to decide, alone, what kind of gathering it might need to hold next.

Yusuf received them in his office at the grange hall that evening, and Mara noticed immediately that he'd aged in the weeks since their first visit, not in any way she could point to specifically, just a heaviness around his eyes that hadn't been there before, the look of a man who'd spent too many nights arguing with himself in a room with no one else to answer him. His desk was cluttered in a way she'd never seen it, ledgers and old meeting minutes stacked in uneven towers, as if he'd spent the last several days trying to bury the question in front of him under every other piece of council business he could find.

"Priya told me what happened," he said, before any of them had sat down. "This morning. The missing hour."

"Then you understand why we're here," Priya said, standing straighter than usual, official in a way she hadn't allowed herself to be in this office before. "This isn't circumstantial anymore, Yusuf. This isn't a hesitation in a story or a birth certificate that doesn't add up. Someone reached into my mind and removed something specific, deliberately, to protect themselves from exactly the kind of scrutiny this council exists to provide. That's not a suspicion. That's an active threat against a sitting deputy, carried out in the middle of an ongoing investigation."

Yusuf sank into the chair behind his desk, and for a long moment he said nothing at all, his hands pressed flat against the worn wood, staring down at them as if they might offer some answer his own mind hadn't managed to produce. Mara watched him and thought, not for the first time since this had begun, how much easier it would be if the people at the center of a thing like this were simply cruel, simply easy to hate. Yusuf wasn't. He was a tired man in a small office trying to weigh one kind of loyalty against another, and there was no version of tonight that let him walk out having betrayed nothing at all.

"I went to see her again," he said finally. "Two nights ago. I didn't tell any of you, because I wasn't sure yet what I was looking for, or what I'd do if I found it."

"And," Caleb said.

"And she was exactly who she's always been. Warm. Funny. Asked after my sister's new baby by name, remembered details about my mother's last weeks that I'd forgotten myself." Yusuf's voice had gone rough. "I sat in that kitchen for two hours, and every single minute of it, I wanted to believe there was some other explanation. A woman who's carried this town through every hard season for fifty years doesn't just turn out to be the thing we've all been afraid of in the dark. That's not how the story is supposed to go."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, a truck passed on the county road, its headlights sweeping briefly across the office ceiling before the room settled back into its low amber quiet, and Mara found herself grateful for the small, ordinary interruption, a reminder that the rest of the valley was still out there, still moving through its evening, entirely unaware of what was being decided in this one lit window.

"But you don't believe that anymore," Mara said quietly.

"I believe Priya lost an hour of her own mind, and I believe it happened the day after Denise warned you off directly, and I believe if I keep waiting for a version of this that lets me avoid the choice in front of me, I'm going to keep waiting until someone gets hurt worse than an hour they can't get back." Yusuf looked up, and something in his face had finally settled, the exhausted uncertainty replaced by something harder, sadder, and more resolved. "I owe her a debt I can never repay, sitting with my mother the way she did. I've spent every day since you first came to me trying to find a way to honor that debt without betraying everything else I owe this valley at the same time. I don't think that's possible anymore. I think I have to choose."

The room went still, waiting. Wren, who had been standing near the door with her arms crossed since they'd come in, shifted her weight but said nothing, some instinct telling her this decision needed to arrive at its own pace rather than be pushed toward it. Priya kept her eyes on Yusuf, steady and patient in a way Mara suspected took real effort, given everything sitting unresolved in the hole where an hour of her own memory used to be.

"I'm authorizing a full inquiry," Yusuf said. "Formal. On the record. Not because I've stopped loving the woman who sat with my mother, but because loving her doesn't excuse me from protecting the rest of this valley from whatever she's actually doing, however kindly she means it." He pulled a sheet of paper toward himself, already reaching for a pen. "I want everything. The letter. The drought file. The marriage record. The stakeout log. Priya's missing hour, documented as an incident in its own right. Whatever Adelaide's letter says about redirection, whatever Odette told you about Whitlock. All of it, compiled properly, so that if this ever needs to survive a challenge, it will."

"That could take days," Wren said, finally moving away from the door, restless energy she hadn't spent since they arrived spilling into her voice now. "Days we're not sure we have, if she's already willing to do what she did to Priya."

"I know the cost of moving slowly," Yusuf said, meeting her eyes directly for the first time since they'd sat down. "I also know the cost of moving fast on something that falls apart the moment somebody who loves her asks a single hard question we didn't think to answer first. I'm not choosing slow because I'm comfortable. I'm choosing it because it's the only version of this that survives contact with people who aren't already convinced."

"So we work through the night," Wren said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Then we work through the night," Yusuf agreed. "But I won't authorize the council to act on anything less than a complete record, properly assembled. I've watched this valley move too fast on too little evidence once already, with Kell, and I watched what that recklessness cost us even when we turned out to be right. I won't repeat that mistake for Denise's sake or for yours."

"What does authorization actually let us do," Mara asked. "Practically. Tonight, tomorrow."

Yusuf considered that, weighing his words carefully. "It lets Priya request additional deputies from the county if she believes the situation warrants it, though I'd advise against bringing in anyone unfamiliar with what we're actually facing. It lets the four of you, plus Dev, act with the council's knowledge and tacit protection rather than as private citizens taking matters into your own hands, which matters a great deal if anything about tonight or tomorrow night ends up being questioned later. And it means that when you're ready to confront her, whatever that confrontation looks like, you do it with this office standing behind you, not exposed and alone."

"When we're ready," Caleb repeated. "You're not asking us to wait for the council to vote on this first."

"There's no time for a full council vote, and you know it as well as I do," Yusuf said grimly. "I'm exercising the emergency authority this chair carries for exactly this kind of situation, the same authority my predecessor used the night Kell finally collapsed. I'll answer for that decision afterward, to the full council, whatever the consequences turn out to be. But I won't sit behind procedure while someone in this valley loses pieces of themselves one careful theft at a time."

Caleb, who had said little through most of the conversation, spoke up from where he stood near the window. "And if it comes out that you authorized this alone, without a vote. What happens to you."

"Nothing good," Yusuf admitted. "Some on the council will say I overstepped. Some will say I should have moved faster, given what's already happened to Priya. I've made peace with both criticisms being possible at once. It's not a comfortable place to stand, but I don't think comfort is what this chair is actually for, not on nights like this one."

Priya reached across the desk and set her hand briefly over his, an old gesture between two people who'd clearly weathered difficult decisions together before. "Thank you," she said. "I know what this costs you."

"It costs less than the alternative," Yusuf said, though his voice carried the weight of a man who didn't fully believe his own reassurance yet. "Go home. All of you. Get whatever sleep you can manage. Tomorrow we start building the record properly, and once it's built, we decide together exactly how and when to go to her."

They left his office a little before midnight, the grange hall dark and empty around them, the valley outside settled into the same deceptive ordinariness it always wore no matter what was happening underneath it. The air had gone properly cold, the kind of late-night chill that came early in this part of the valley once the season started to turn, and Mara pulled her jacket tighter as they crossed the empty parking lot, gravel crunching underfoot in the only sound for a long stretch of road. Behind them, a light went out in Yusuf's office window, and she wondered whether he was finally heading home himself or simply sitting a while longer in the dark, alone with a decision he'd have to live inside for as long as this took to resolve.

Mara walked between Caleb and Wren without speaking for a long while, feeling the shape of the last several hours settle into something that finally, after weeks of careful uncertainty, resembled the beginning of an ending rather than another delay.

"He chose us," Wren said eventually, something like disbelief still coloring her voice. "After everything. After the debt he owes her. He actually chose us."

"He chose the valley," Mara said. "We're just the ones standing closest to it right now." She looked up at the dark shapes of the fields stretching out on either side of the road, familiar and unfamiliar at once, the way everything in this valley had started to feel since the letter first changed hands at her own wedding. "Tomorrow we start building something that has to survive being questioned by people who love her as much as he does. That's not going to be easy, whatever authorization we've got behind us now."

"No," Caleb agreed. "But it's finally moving. After everything, it's finally moving."

Wren fell into step beside them, quiet for once, her earlier restlessness settled into something more thoughtful. "Priya's going to want to start the moment we walk in the door," she said. "You know that. She won't sleep either way, so she might as well be building the record while she's awake."

"Then we let her," Mara said. "But not alone. Whatever we build tonight, we build it together, all of us in one room, so nobody's carrying a piece of this by themselves the way Priya was this morning when Dev found her."

They walked the rest of the way home in a silence that felt, for the first time in weeks, less like dread and more like the quiet before something necessary, the valley around them holding its breath along with all of them, waiting to see what the next few days would finally bring to a debt fifty years in the making.

Mara glanced back once, toward the grange hall's dark silhouette against the ridge, and thought of every ordinary meeting she'd ever sat through in that building, every potluck and budget dispute and small-town squabble that had once seemed like the most complicated thing this valley could produce. She wondered, not for the first time, how many of those evenings Denise had sat somewhere in that same hall, laughing at the right moments, bringing the right dish, carrying all the while a secret so large it should have bent the room around her, and yet somehow never had.
