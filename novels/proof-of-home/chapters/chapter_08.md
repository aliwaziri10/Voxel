Jack carried the evening order to the bakery’s back door because the front street was crowded with a cart unloading turnips and a pair of boys arguing over a hoop. The parcel rested against his forearm, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. Sarah had asked him to bring it over after the last crossing. He had said yes before asking himself why she had not sent Nell.

The parcel had sat on the wheelhouse table through the last crossing, its string damp from the mist that rose off the lake. Jack had moved it twice to clear space for the fare ledger, then set it back exactly where it had been. He could have handed it to Nell at the landing. She had been on deck, ready to take the evening line, and Sarah knew that. Instead he had carried the parcel himself and told himself it was quicker. The explanation would have held if he had not spent the whole crossing noticing how often the bakery door opened onto the street. By the time he reached Lantern Street, he had rehearsed three ordinary greetings and forgotten all of them.

The alley behind the shop was narrow, shadowed by the bakery wall on one side and the stone boundary of the Lamplight Inn on the other. A smell of warm crust drifted through the open back door. Jack stopped at the threshold and heard Sarah humming to herself as she worked. The tune was low and tuneless, a few notes repeated while metal touched metal inside. He shifted the parcel from one arm to the other and wiped his palm on his coat. He could hear the scrape of a peel against the oven brick, then the soft knock of a loaf being set down. The ordinary sounds made him conscious of standing outside instead of carrying on with his evening rounds. He could still turn away and leave the parcel on the step. Sarah had asked him to bring it, though, and he had agreed. He lifted his hand before he could talk himself out of the visit.

He knocked once.

“Come in,” she called, then appeared in the doorway before he could move.

She had flour on her cheek. A pale streak lay just below her left eye, caught in the soft crease beside her nose. Her hair was pinned up, but the heat had loosened a strand near her ear. She looked tired from work, her sleeves pushed up and her apron dusted white. Her gaze went to the parcel, then to his face.

“You brought it.”

“You asked.”

“I did.” She stepped back to make room. “Thank you.”

He set the parcel on the low crate beside the door. The string had tightened in the damp, and he worked a finger beneath the knot to loosen it. Sarah reached for the package at the same moment. Their hands did not meet. He pulled his fingers away and let her take it.

The flour on her cheek was close enough to touch.

He saw it. He thought of saying that she had missed a spot, or that the sack must have split while she worked. He could have kept his hands at his sides and let her find it in the mirror later. Instead, before he had settled on any of those choices, he lifted his thumb and brushed the flour from her skin.

The touch was brief. His thumb passed over the warm curve of her cheek, soft skin beneath a trace of powder. He felt the fine movement of her breath against his wrist. Sarah went still. Her lips parted, and her eyes stayed on his hand as he drew it away.

Jack stopped with his thumb raised between them.

Neither of them spoke.

The alley seemed to contract around the narrow space separating their bodies. He could hear the cart wheels on the street, a horse stamping against the stones, the faint tick of a cooling oven inside the bakery. Sarah held the parcel against her apron. Her fingers had tightened around the paper until it creased.

“Thank you,” she said.

Her voice was quieter than before. Not a whisper, not quite. The words barely carried beyond the doorway.

Jack put his hand into his pocket. The movement was too fast. His knuckles struck the seam, and he had to curl his fingers to keep them still. He wanted to apologize, but an apology would make the touch sound like a mistake. He wanted to explain that he had not planned it, but that would only tell her how long he had been thinking about it.

“I saw the flour,” he said.

“I know.”

He looked at her cheek. The pale streak was gone. He wished it had not been.

Behind her, the kitchen gave a small wooden pop as the oven cooled. Sarah shifted the parcel from one arm to the other. A warm loaf lay on the counter inside, its crust still shining faintly where she had brushed it with water. She reached back and lifted it, holding it with both hands through a folded cloth.

Heat seeped through the folded cloth. Sarah adjusted her grip, then looked down at the loaf as if it had surprised her. Jack watched the color climb from her collar to her cheeks.

Jack saw her fingers adjust around the cloth. The heat should have been ordinary, the stored warmth of an oven. But the loaf grew warmer under her palms, enough that she looked down at it and then back at him. A flush rose along her neck. She did not put the bread down.

For a moment neither seemed able to decide what to do with the loaf. Sarah shifted the cloth beneath it, protecting her palms, and the warmth spread through the folds. Jack could smell the crust now, toasted grain and a trace of rosemary. It was the same bread he had eaten before, but the scent caught at him in a way he could not explain. He looked from the loaf to her face. She held his gaze, waiting for him to say what he had noticed.

“Is it meant to do that?” he asked.

Sarah glanced at the bread again. “Not usually.” She set it on the counter at last, but kept one hand on the cloth. “It’s still warm from the oven.”

He knew that was not the whole answer. He also knew she had given him all she intended to give. He nodded, accepting the limit, and stepped back.

“I should get back,” Jack said.

The parcel had been delivered, and the crossing was over for the evening. Jack lingered by the door, trying to think of a practical reason to stay. He found none. Sarah held the warm loaf between them. He looked at it, then at her, but said nothing.

Instead he stepped away from the door.

Sarah moved aside to give him room. Her shoulder did not touch his. The gap between them was narrow enough for him to see the small flour marks on her sleeve, wide enough for him to pass without brushing her again.

“Good night, Jack.”

“Good night.”

He turned before she could see what the words cost him. The back door remained open behind him, a square of amber light on the stones. He walked to the end of the alley and did not look back until he reached the corner. Sarah was still in the doorway, the loaf held against her apron. She watched him for a moment, then went inside.

Jack kept walking.

The harbor had quieted. A few lamps glowed in windows along Lantern Street, and the last of the market carts had turned uphill. The Heron waited at the landing, rocking gently against the ropes. Jack stepped aboard and went straight to the wheelhouse, though there was no work left that required him there. He placed both hands on the rail and looked out at the darkening lake.

He had touched her face.

He could make the act sound smaller if he listed the facts. Flour had marked her cheek. He had brushed it away. The touch had lasted less than a second. She had thanked him. He had left. No promise had been made, no word spoken that could not be explained as ordinary courtesy.

The facts did not help.

He rubbed his thumb against the seam of his pocket, then rested his hand on the rail. The wood was cold from the night air. He kept his palm there until the ferry reached the next marker.

Nell came up from the stern with the coil of line over one shoulder. She stopped when she saw him at the rail.

“You delivered the parcel?”

“Yes.”

“Was the door difficult to find?”

Jack glanced at her. “I’ve been to that alley before.”

“Of course.” Nell set the coil down and began checking the knot. “I only wondered why it took you so long to return.”

“I walked.”

“You do that.” Nell tightened the line and stood. She glanced toward the bakery, where the light still showed in the window, then at the heel of bread on the bench.

“Did she say anything?”

“About what?”

Nell gave him a look that made the question pointless. “The parcel, then.”

“She thanked me.”

“Good. That’s what parcels are for.” She took the empty coil and headed below before he could answer. Jack watched her go, annoyed by the ease with which she had found the edge of the truth. Nell never pushed hard. She only left him room to hear himself.

She did not look at him. Her fingers worked the line through the cleat, pulling it taut and laying the slack in a neat loop. Jack watched her hands because it was safer than looking toward the bakery.

“Anything else?” he asked.

“Nothing I can think of.”

Nell lifted the coil and went below deck. Jack stayed by the rail, watching the dark water and the bakery window across the lane. The wind had begun to turn, but he waited until the light in the shop went out before checking the bow line.

The Heron had drifted a little off the landing, though the lines were taut. Jack pulled the bow rope in and laid it over the cleat again. The hull moved back, then eased away as if the current had found a gap under the boards. He checked the stern line and found it secure. There was no practical reason for the boat to keep straining against its moorings. He loosened the bow line by half a turn and gave the hull room to rise with the small waves. The Heron eased toward the landing, then drifted away again, the rope pulling taut in his hand. Jack checked the knot and the current, then tried the line once more. Nothing had changed in the water. The trouble was in him, and he knew it. He had called the touch an impulse, as if the flour had moved his hand on its own. It had not. He had wanted to touch her, and he had chosen to do it. That did not tell him what Sarah had felt, and it gave him no right to guess. He let the line rest against the cleat without tightening it. After a moment the Heron settled against the dock, still and quiet beneath his hand. He rested his hand on the rail until the Heron settled, then looked across at the dark bakery window and withdrew his hand.

He had not meant to touch Sarah, but the wish had come before the movement. The flour on her cheek had seemed an easy thing to brush away. He had acted before caution could stop him, and now he could not tell whether she had welcomed the touch or stood still from surprise.

Jack sat on the bench beside the wheelhouse and took the heel of bread from the cloth in his coat pocket. Sarah had given it to him after the morning crossing. The edges had dried, but the center was still soft. He broke off a corner and chewed. The taste was familiar; the memory it brought back was not. He set the bread beside him, then picked it up again, unable to settle on whether he wanted to eat or keep it.

He crossed to the wheelhouse and looked at his reflection in the lantern glass. He could go back to the bakery and apologize if he had startled her. That would be fair. But it would leave out the part he had no right to ask her to manage: he had wanted to touch her before he found the flour, and the excuse had only made the want easier to act on. He rested his forehead against the cool frame. The bakery light had gone out. He would not invent what Sarah was thinking, or go back and demand an answer from her tonight.

He wrapped the heel in its cloth and tied the corners securely. Tomorrow he would take the usual channel, collect the fares, and keep his attention on the water. He had made that decision before and broken it without meaning to, so he did not mistake it for a promise. The Heron shifted against the ropes. He checked the bow line, then the stern, pulling each taut and laying the slack flat against the cleats. Both knots held. Across the lane, the bakery stood dark; he could see the back door and the worn step, but nothing beyond them. He went into the wheelhouse, set the lamp low, and laid the route chart on the table. At first light, he would take the straight route to the landing. The ropes creaked once as the ferry settled against the dock.
