Lanterns were already lit as they moved down the lane. Each one glowed like a small white eye in the darkness, and the town seemed to pour light toward the water. Sarah walked beside him, her steps steady, and he felt the press of her shoulder against his chest. He kept his eyes forward, counting the lanterns behind them one by one. The numbers rose in his thoughts like bubbles in clear water: twenty‑three, twenty‑four. The rhythm steadied something inside him.  

Her breath came short. He noticed it the way one notices a shift in temperature or a change in air pressure. She held herself very still, arms wrapped around her torso, and he realized she was afraid—not of the cold or the dark, but of being seen, of standing too close and having the space between them disappear.  

He kept his gaze ahead. The crowd pressed close, bodies leaning in, and he felt the warmth of strangers brushing past. The lanterns caught the edge of her hair, turning it silver. Somewhere ahead the Heron waited at the far end of the lane, its dark hull set against the glowing path.  

They reached the narrowest stretch of the lane. The crowd thinned a little here, the lane itself a corridor of shoulders and coats, with the occasional gasp. Sarah stopped. Her hand found his arm, fingers threading through his, and he did not pull away. Instead he gripped the iron railing of the pier post and counted again: one, two, three, four, five. His knuckles ached from the tension, a dull pulse that matched the beating of her heart he could almost hear beneath her clothes.  

She did not move. She did not step closer. The line of people behind them shifted, forming a wall of bodies that separated them from the rest of the crowd. He could see faces above them—some laughing, some whispering, none looking directly at him. The lantern light fell on their features, and he saw the way they watched him without quite knowing why.  

He thought about the Heron waiting. He thought about the water beyond the lane, how it moved slow and patient, how it carried things that wanted to cross. The bread in his pocket was quiet that night; he had not touched it since the walk began. The dough inside was soft, waiting, and he knew that tomorrow morning it would speak for the first time since he had inherited the shop. It would carry something he had been keeping hidden for weeks, something that needed to be shaped into bread before it could be shared.  

The secret settled into him like the lanterns settling into their posts. He had seen Nell at the inn that afternoon, her face sharp with something she could not name. She had spoken to him in low words, and he had nodded, because that was what you did. You listened. You did not push. Now he understood that she had known. That she had been guiding the Heron all along, pushing the baker onto the boat, making sure he would arrive at the pier at the right moment. She had been steering him toward this walk, this night, this pressure against his chest.  

He did not say anything. There was no need. The closeness was enough. He felt her body's weight press against him, a kind of prayer he offered without question. Like water around stones, the crowd moved around them, smoothing edges and leaving them alone in the center of their own small circle.  

The walk continued. The lanterns burned brighter as the night deepened. Sarah's breath remained short, and he could feel the heat radiating from her, though she tried to keep it contained. He wondered what she was hiding. What she needed to show him that day. The bread in his pocket seemed to respond to his thoughts, swelling slightly, as if it recognized the intention building inside him.  

When they reached the dock, the Heron sat motionless in the water, its oars resting alongside. The crew was gone, the men who usually worked the lines had retreated to their homes. Only the captain remained, a silhouette against the lantern light, and he looked up at them with an expression that might have been gratitude or might have been something else entirely.  

Sarah stood beside him. Her hand was still on his arm, and he could feel the warmth of her skin through the thin wool of her jacket. The night air was cool, but the heat between them was different. It had nothing to do with temperature. It was the ache of wanting something you could not name, and the knowledge that the thing you wanted was sitting on the dock, waiting to be taken. The bread inside him was ready. It had been waiting since the inheritance, since the funeral, since the moment Fenna had handed him the keys to the shop and the old recipe book bound in worn leather.  

He did not open his mouth. He could not. The lanterns blazed overhead, and the crowd pressed close, and somewhere in the middle of all that, the distance between them shrank to nothing. He stood rigid, his grip on the railing barely loosening, and watched her face. The fear was there, yes, but underneath it was something else. Something that made him want to reach for her and fail.  

The Heron creaked softly as the tide shifted. The sound traveled through the wood and into his bones. He thought of the water, of the way it moved slow and deliberate, carrying secrets in its depths. The bread in his pocket rose another inch, responding to the desire he was not yet willing to name. Tomorrow it would taste of longing. Tonight it would simply exist, patient and silent, waiting for the first bite.  

The next morning the bread gave her away. Odalys took a slice from the first loaf and bit down, and the moment the crust cracked, the flavor spilled out like a confession. It carried the ache that lived in Sarah's hands while she shaped it, the sorrow she had held since the funeral, and the grief that had turned sweeter and sharper as she kneaded the dough. The bread spoke in words she could not unhear. It said *I am sorry* and *I miss you* and *stay*, all tangled together in the texture of wheat and salt.  

Sarah stood in the bakery doorway, watching Odalys eat. The woman's face showed nothing, but her eyes tracked the movement of the bread, the way the crumb fell. Sarah felt the loss of control in her chest, a sudden opening that made her throat tight. She had hoped the bread would remain a secret, a private language between her and the town. Instead it had become public, and she was the only witness to the public declaration of everything she had been trying to contain.  

She went back inside. The oven was hot, and the smell of rising dough filled the small space. She pulled the loaves from the heat and laid them on the cooling rack, each one a vessel for the feeling she had been carrying. The first loaf, the one she had baked for the funeral, was still soft in the center. She broke it in half and placed one piece on the counter, where it waited for the morning customers.  

The crowd outside the bakery had grown. People gathered on the steps, some with cups in hand, others with children clutching them. The lanterns from the harbor reflected in the windows, casting patterns of gold and shadow across the walls. Sarah could hear the murmur of conversation, the clink of dishes, and beneath it all, the distant sound of the Heron pulling away from the shore. The ferry was moving on its regular route, carrying its usual passengers, indifferent to the small drama unfolding inside the shop.  

She thought about Nell. About the way she had watched her from the corner of the lamplight inn, the way she had leaned close to Jack without speaking, the way she had smiled when the captain of the Heron passed by. The secret was not new. It had been building since the beginning, a thread pulled tighter with each passing day. Nell had known about the Heron, about the promise Sarah had made to herself of one season, about the bread that carried feelings and the danger of sharing them too soon. She had been the one to steer the boat, to make sure the timing was right, to guide Sarah toward the moment when the bread would speak and the truth would come out.  

It was care. It was not betrayal. Nell had always cared for Sarah, had always seen the ache in her hands and tried to fill it with small gestures. The secret was a gift. No one took it. And now Sarah understood that the closeness she felt with Jack was not the same as the closeness she had been avoiding. This was something else. This was the result of years of watching, of waiting, of letting the bread teach her what she had been too afraid to learn.  

The door opened and a customer entered, and Sarah stepped aside to greet them. The moment passed. She returned to her work, her hands moving through the familiar motions of kneading and folding. The dough responded to her touch, yielding and rising, and she felt the shape taking form. It was not the bread she had intended to bake that morning. It was the bread of this moment, the bread of the secret she had been carrying, the bread that would tell the town what she had been hiding.  

Outside, the lanterns continued to burn. The Heron drifted slowly across the water, its presence a constant reminder of the journey that had brought Sarah here. The night was ending, but the ache in her chest had not diminished. It had only changed shape, becoming something larger, something that would require more than a single loaf to express. She would have to find a way to share it, to let it flow into the world without breaking the fragile balance she had maintained.  

The bread on the counter waited. It was still soft, still promising. And somewhere in the distance, the sound of the ferry grew fainter as it crossed the lake, carrying its passengers toward whatever came next. Sarah closed her eyes and let the sensation wash over her, the weight of the secret and the hope it contained, and she began to think about what she would do with the feeling she had been holding for so long.  

The ache was still there. It had only grown. And she would not stop it.
