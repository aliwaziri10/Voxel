By the late-summer lantern night Jack had asked Sarah Quill nineteen questions, and none of them was the one.

He kept them in his head the way he kept soundings. Did she take sugar in her tea. Had the widow’s gate been mended. Was the new oven drawing properly now that the wind had gone round to the north. She answered each one with her whole face, as if he had handed her a hot cup, and then she asked him one back, and they stood on the dock or the step or the narrow strip of counter and traded small plain things across the space between them. It was the slowest courtship ever conducted on Larkmere Water, and he knew it, and he could not hurry it by an inch. The big question sat at the bottom of everything like a stone on a lakebed. He could see it. He did not yet have the line long enough to reach it.

It was cooler. That was the first thing the town had noticed. The storm had broken the heat and the heat had not come back, and the evenings now closed in a little sooner and smelled of cut hay and chimney smoke. The dragonflies were gone from the pier. The Heron rode lower in the morning mist with a new dull sheen on her brass, and he had begun to take his coat on the early crossings without being told. The last run of the season stood on the harbor clerk’s board, six weeks off, in the clerk’s square chalk, and Jack had stopped looking at the board. She had said she would tell him to his face when she knew. She had not said it yet, and he had not asked.

The lantern night came down on the harbor like a festival that had been waiting all summer for permission. Every household in Larkmere carried a small lantern to the pier at dusk, a tin one or a glass one or a paper one stiffened with wax, and hung it from the long iron rail that ran the length of the boards, so that by full dark the whole waterfront stood outlined in small flames and the lake took them all and doubled them. Gideon Teague lit the first from his taper at the head of the lane. After that the town did the rest itself. It was the oldest custom in Larkmere after midsummer, and it had no story to it that Jack had ever been told. You carried your light down to the water. That was the whole of it.

By the time he came up from the Heron, the lanes were packed to the walls.

The harbor lanes had been built for carts, and they were not built for this. They ran down the hill to the water in three narrow channels, and every channel was a slow river of coats and shawls and children carried on shoulders and old men being helped by their grandsons. The lamps swayed. The shuffle of a thousand boots made one long soft sound like surf on gravel. He stood at the top, taller than the crowd by a head, and he saw her in the second lane before she saw him: a small figure in Fenna’s long coat, carrying a tin lantern in both hands, being carried along by the flow with her chin lifted and her eyes on the water.

He moved before he thought. A man did not have to think to follow a line he had already made fast. He cut sideways through a knot of fishermen, and a woman with a baby, and a boy with a stick, and somebody’s elbow caught him under the ribs and he did not feel it.

“Jack.” She had seen him. Her face opened, quickly, and closed again to a quieter thing. “I did not think you would be here. Is the Heron not meant to be lit?”

“Nell has her.”

“Then you are all mine for the night.”

She had not meant it as it sounded. He watched her hear it, and the color came up her throat, and she turned and looked very hard at a drain.

“There is a jam at the bottom,” said Nell Mercer, who had not been there a moment ago.

She stood at his elbow with her cap pushed back and a coil of cord on her shoulder and the open, helpful, perfectly blank face of a woman who had never schemed in her life. “The main lane is blocked solid. A cart wheel gone. Nobody is going anywhere down there before midnight. You want the Net Sheds stair. Come on. It is quicker.” She put a hand flat against the back of each of them, not pushing, only indicating, and turned them like a pair of boats into a gap between two walls that Jack had walked past a thousand times and never counted as a lane. “Go on. Straight down. I will find you at the bottom.”

She did not. He did not see her again that night.

The Net Sheds stair was the narrowest passage in the harbor. It went down between the tarred walls of the net lofts in a steep slant of worn stone, no wider than a man’s outstretched arms, with an iron handrail bolted to the left wall at the height of his hip. The lamps did not reach it. There was only the grey light coming down from the strip of sky between the roofs, and the glow at the bottom where the pier began. And the crowd came with them. The people who had been squeezed out of the main lane saw an opening and poured into it behind, a thick warm pressing weight at their backs, and in the space of ten steps the stair was full from wall to wall and nobody could have turned round if he had wanted to.

Sarah was in front of him. She had come in first, with the lantern, and she was on the step below him now, and the crowd pushed down, and she was not carried away from him. She was pressed back.

Her back met his chest.

He had expected the shock of it. He had not expected the way it settled. It was not an accident any longer; it was a state of affairs. She stood against the whole length of his front, her shoulders under his breastbone, the line of her spine against his ribs, the small of her back against his belt, and the coat between them was too thin, and the crowd behind him shoved again, and the pressure came home, step by step, until there was no daylight between them anywhere.

He reached for the rail. It was the only thing in the world that he could reach. His left hand found the iron and closed on it and he held it as a man holds a cleat in a gale.

Neither of them said a word.

They went down one step. They stopped. The line ahead had halted for a child. They went down another. The crowd breathed all around them, a great slow animal of wool and lantern-oil and warm skin, and in the middle of it she stood and he stood, and the only things that moved were the muscles in his forearm and the breath in her back.

He could feel her breathing. That was the cruelty of it. It came through the thin coat and through his shirt, a little fast, a little shallow, each intake lifting her against his chest and each exhale letting her sink. He knew the rhythm of it as well as he knew the cycle of the engine. It was short. It was caught. Twice it stopped for the space of a heartbeat and began again with a small shake. The nape of her neck was a hand’s breadth under his chin, bare where her hair had been pinned up, with a few loose strands lifting in his own breath, and he could smell warm flour and cardamom and the cool metal smell of the lantern in her hands.

He fixed his eyes on the lamps below. He counted them.

There were forty-one on the first stretch of the pier rail. He counted them left to right. Then he counted them right to left, and got forty-three, and counted again. The numbers held him up. He gripped the iron and let the knuckles whiten and the bones in his hand begin to ache, a long bright ache, and welcomed it. It was an honest pain. It was a post set in the current. It kept him from the other thing, the plain terrible fact of her warmth at his chest, the weight of her just beginning to lean, a quarter inch, so small that he might have invented it.

She did not move away. There was room, once. The stair eased for the length of two steps and a gap opened in front of her, a hand’s width of open stone. She could have gone into it. She stood where she was. She kept her eyes straight ahead, on the glow at the bottom of the stair, and her breath came shorter, and he felt the back of her head come to rest, very lightly, against the front of his shoulder.

He closed his eyes for exactly one breath. Then he opened them and counted the lanterns again.

Forty-three. Forty-three. He could not make it come out otherwise.

Neither of them turned. It was the whole discipline of it. If she turned her head she would see his face, and he did not know what it would show. If he bent his, his mouth would come down to the soft place under her ear, and the rail would not hold him then. They went down the Net Sheds stair as two people made of the same stillness, and every step was an hour, and the pier came up toward them out of the dusk like a coast.

The crowd let go of them all at once. They came out onto the boards into the light of a hundred hanging flames, and the pressure was simply gone, and cool air struck his chest where she had been.

She stepped forward. She did not look back. She raised the tin lantern in both hands and hung it on the rail, carefully, on the hook between two others, and stood with her hands still lifted around it, steadying a flame that did not need steadying.

“Goodnight, Jack,” she said, to the flame.

“Goodnight.”

She walked away along the rail. He stood where he was and opened his left hand. It would not open at first. The groove of the iron ran across his palm in four bright bars, and it was a long time before the blood came back to them.

It was past midnight when Nell came aboard the Heron. He sat in the wheelhouse with the lamp low, and she came across the deck in her stocking feet, which she did only when she wished to be unheard, and leaned in the doorway with her cap in her hand.

“You steered us,” Jack said.

“I did.”

“The main lane was not blocked.”

“It was not.” Nell looked at the ceiling. “There was a cart wheel. I pulled the pin. I put it back an hour later, in case anyone was cross.”

He turned in his seat. She had the grace to look a little ashamed, which was a thing he had seen on her twice in her life.

“How long?” he said.

“Since the first loaf.” She said it very simply. “Since the morning she sent it across and you took it from her hands and went and sat on the bench with your eyes shut. I was coiling the stern line. I have never seen a man look like that, Uncle, not in my life, not at a funeral. So I made a few changes.” She ticked them off on her fingers. “I told the chandler to send the flour by the Heron instead of the cart. I moved the early crossing so we would pass Lantern Street when her ovens were opening. I tied the stern line short, the day you took her as a passenger, so the plank would put her beside you. I may have told Gideon where to stand at midsummer.”

“You may have.”

“I may have been very thorough.” She turned the cap in her hands. “You were going to sit on this boat until you were old and call it a life. I would sooner be a nuisance than watch it.”

He opened his mouth, and shut it, and found that there was nothing in him to say but the one thing.

“Thank you.”

“Oh, do not,” said Nell, and her voice cracked in the middle. “Do not be gracious. I cannot bear it.” She went out, and the deck complained under her, and then it was quiet.

Dawn came grey over the water. The rail of the pier was still hung with lanterns, burned down to their last stubs, and the wax had run in white tears over the iron. Odalys Pike came along the boards with her basket and the walk of a woman on a mission, and stopped at the wheelhouse door and held out a roll, still warm, in a cloth.

“Her first batch of the morning,” she said. “I took a bite at the back door and it gave her away at once. I do not need to ask what she was thinking of. Go on.”

He took it. He tasted a stair in the dark, and a breath held so long that it had turned into the shape of a name, and the faint tremble of a lantern lifted in two steady hands.

He ate every crumb of it. When he had done, he looked down at his palm, where the four bars of the rail still lay red across the skin, and he did not close it.
