The engine did not die. It lost interest.

Jack felt it go the way a man feels a stair that is not there in the dark. The low steady note that had carried him across Larkmere Water ten thousand times thinned, hesitated, and ran out into a quiet so complete that he could hear the lake lapping at the hull like a cat at a saucer. The Heron coasted. She lost a little way, and a little more, and then she lay still in the very middle of the water with her bow toward the lights of the home pier and her stern toward the dead spark of the far landing, and the dark came up on every side and held her there.

He tried the engine anyway. A ferryman does. He turned the key and checked the fuel cock and laid his palm on the casing to feel for heat, and he did each thing in order by the grey light of the wheelhouse lamp while Sarah Quill sat on the bench behind him and watched him do it. The fuel was good. The casing was cool and sound. The wheel moved freely in its lashing and the rudder answered it. When he turned the key again the starter spun and spun and the engine gave him back nothing, not a cough or a catch, like a horse that has heard its name and chooses to go on standing where it is.

“Is it the fuel?” said Sarah.

“It is not the fuel.”

“Then what is it?”

He took his fingers off the key. The fishermen and the women with baskets had gone up the far landing road long since; he had seen the last of them off himself. Nell was ashore with her cough. The standing loaf had been handed over to the landing keeper, and Sarah had come back aboard for the run home as plainly as a woman comes back to her own front step, and neither of them had remarked on it. There was nobody else on the lake. There was nobody else within a mile in any direction.

“She sulks,” he said. “You have seen her do it. She drags her feet and the crossing runs long. She has never done this.”

“Done what?”

“Stopped.” He looked out through the glass at the seam of pier lights, small and steady and a long way off, and entirely indifferent to him. “I think she means to stay here.”

Sarah did not startle. She held her basket on her knees with her coat buttoned to the throat, a small straight figure under the lamp, and he waited for alarm, because any sensible woman would have shown it. She looked at the engine. She looked at him. “Until when?” she said.

“Dawn, if the old talk is right.”

“What does the old talk say?”

He had never said it aloud to anyone. It was the sort of thing a boy was told on the deck by his elders and then spent his life declining to believe. “That she will not carry a lie across the water. That when there is a thing aboard that has not been said, she holds still until it is, or until the light comes and makes the question foolish.”

The lamp hissed. Outside, a fish rose and left a ring on the black glass of the lake, and the ring spread and went out.

“And what has not been said?” said Sarah, very quietly.

He did not answer. He could not have told her which of the answers was the true one, and the Heron, who knew a good deal more than he did, went on lying still.

He lit the signal lamp. It was what the rules asked, and he followed rules when he had nothing else to follow. He hung it on the mast hook and shuttered it three long and three short, three long and three short, and watched the pier. Nothing answered. The harbor lamps burned on in their row, and up on the hill a window showed yellow and went dark. A person with good eyes and no cough at all might have seen the signal from the end of the boards. He did not let himself finish the thought.

“Nobody is coming,” said Sarah.

“No.”

“Then we had better be comfortable.” She said it to her knees. She was shivering, in fine quick shudders that she was trying to hide in her shoulders, and he saw it and was on his feet before he had decided to be.

The blanket lived in the locker under her bench. He had to ask her to lift her feet. It was grey wool, coarse and heavy and smelling of tar and old rain, and when he shook it out the color went through him for the length of one breath: a gangplank slick with wet, a brass button hanging by a thread. Then he laid it round her shoulders and the gangplank was gone. She drew one edge across her front. She held up the other without a word, the way a person holds open a door.

He sat.

The bench had been built for one man and his dinner. There was no room on it to be careful. The whole of her left side lay along the whole of his right, shoulder to ankle, a warm line through two layers of cloth, and the grey wool closed over both of them like a hatch. He kept his back straight and his eyes on the glass. His free hand went to his knee and took hold of it.

Under the blanket she found his other hand. She did not look for it. She found it as a person finds a rail on a dark stair, and her fingers went in between his and closed, and held.

Neither of them said a word about it.

He had meant to sit very still. That had been the whole plan. He learned that stillness was a thing a man could manage only when there was nothing in him to move. He felt every breath she took. It came to him through his arm and his ribs and the long bone of his thigh, a small steady swell under the hull of him, rising and ebbing, and he knew its rhythm as well as he knew the lift of his own boat at her mooring. She was breathing slowly on purpose. He could tell. He was breathing slowly on purpose too, and the two of them breathed in the middle of a lake, measuring out air like people carrying brimming buckets across a floor.

The Heron lay without a tremor. The water slapped at her once, twice, and was quiet. He looked at it through the glass, black and flat and endless, with the pier lamps laid across it in a long wavering road that did not reach them, and he thought: I am happy.

It was a plain fact, and it frightened him. He was stranded in the dark with a dead engine and a night to get through, and he had not been so glad of anything since he was a boy. He knew the reason. It was not the dark or the cold or the wool. The boat had stopped, and the one person he wanted to keep was being kept, and he was glad of it down to the keel.

That was the thing. That was the whole of Gideon’s warning, and every reason he had ever given himself for not asking. The Heron kept her people. She had kept him. And here was the water, doing what water did, holding a woman where she could not leave, and his heart was glad.

“I am sorry,” he said to the glass. “For the boat.”

“Are you.”

“You did not choose to be out here.”

He felt her turn her head. He did not turn his. “Jack Mercer,” she said, in the voice she kept for customers who had made a foolish remark about the weather, “I walked down a pier and climbed aboard a ferry and took a bench. I have done it every week since spring. Nobody has put a finger on me.” Her grip tightened, very slightly, on his. “Nobody is keeping me. I am sitting here.”

He said nothing. There was nothing in him that could be said. But for the length of a long breath the old fear loosened, as a line goes slack when the current eases, and he let it. It was not gone. It would come back with the light. For now it let him sit in the dark beside a woman who had just told him she was there because she had chosen to be.

Near midnight she freed her other hand from the blanket and opened the basket on her knees.

“I do not go anywhere without bread,” she said. “It is a failing.” She drew out a small round loaf in a clean cloth, no bigger than two fists, and held it a moment against her coat. “Oh,” she said, in a different voice. “It is warm.”

It had no business being warm. It had been out of the oven since morning. She looked at it, and then, very briefly, at him, and then at the loaf again, and her ears went pink in the lamplight. She broke it. The crust parted with a small dry crack that sounded loud over the water, and she gave him the larger half and kept the smaller, and they ate.

It tasted of nothing grand. It tasted of the end of a long day, of a lamp in a window and a door shut against the weather by a person who meant to stay on the right side of it. It tasted exactly like the bench felt. He ate it slowly, and she ate hers, and neither of them spoke, and when they were finished she set the last heel on the basket lid and the two of them forgot it.

The moon came up after midnight, late and thin and the color of tin, and laid its own road across the water straight to the Heron’s bow. He watched it climb. The lake turned from black to slate. The lamp burned down to a thumb of flame, and he did not trim it.

Her head came to rest against his shoulder in the long middle of the night. He did not feel it arrive. One moment there was air, and the next there was the weight of her, and he sat with his chin an inch above her hair and did not breathe for ten beats of his own heart. Her hair smelled of woodsmoke and flour and the cool lake. A few strands of it lifted in his breath and settled again. He fixed his eyes on a point where the moon road broke on a ripple, and he held them there as he would have held a bearing in fog. His free fist had closed on his knee until the knuckles ached. His jaw ached. All of him ached, evenly, from the soles of his boots to his scalp, the way a rope aches that is holding a load it was never meant to hold.

He knew she was not asleep. Her breath was too even and too careful. Every so often it caught, and she let it go slowly, as one lets go of a thing one hopes will not be noticed. Her thumb moved against the side of his, a quarter of an inch one way and a quarter of an inch back, so small a motion that a man less cursed with attention would never have felt it. He felt it. He felt it in the roots of his teeth.

The last run of the season stood on the clerk’s board in square chalk, and the board was nearer than it had been. He thought of it once and put it down. The question lay at the bottom where it had always lain, and his line was longer than it had been in the spring, and still not long enough. He did not ask it. He had no right to ask it on a boat that would not let her go.

It was past the deepest part of the night, in the hour when the lake seems to have stopped breathing, that he said her name.

“Sarah.”

He said it low, hardly more than a breath, to the glass and the road of moon. He had not known he was going to. It was not a question and it did not hold one. It was only her name, laid down on the dark like a palm on a rail, because it was the one true thing he could say that asked for nothing.

She did not answer. She did not lift her head. But her fingers closed on his, hard, until he felt the small bones shift, and her forehead pressed once against the side of his shoulder, and stayed. That was all. It was enough to carry across any water in the world.

Grey came up out of the east so slowly that he did not see it begin. The moon road paled and went. The black of the lake turned to pewter, and the mist lay on it in low rags that moved in no wind, and a pair of gulls came out of the whiteness and settled on the water, two old men waiting for a ferry that was already late. On the basket lid the last heel of the loaf lay forgotten, cold as a stone from the shore. Neither of them had given it a glance in hours.

Sarah lifted her head. She blinked at the light. Her hair was flat on one side, and there was a crease from his collar printed on her cheek, and she looked at him and did not smile, and did not let go.

He did not let go either.

He rose, and she rose with him, because neither of them had loosened a finger. He did not decide to reach for the key. His free hand went to it from long habit, and he turned it, and the engine caught on the first turn, clean and loud and ordinary. It settled at once to its low steady note as though it had never stopped, as though nothing at all had happened on the water that night, and the wheelhouse filled with the plain sound of a boat doing her work.

He took the wheel with his left hand and kept his right exactly where it was, laced into hers beneath the grey wool, and the Heron swung her bow toward the pier and went, steady and unhurried, down the long pale road the sunrise was laying across the lake. He steered her home with one hand. Not once on the whole crossing did he offer the other back.
