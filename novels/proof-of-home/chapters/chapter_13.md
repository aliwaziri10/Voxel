The morning light lay thin over Larkmere Water, a pale silver that caught the edge of the ferry’s hull and turned the ripples into tiny mirrors. Sarah stood at the edge of Lantern Street, flour dusting the front of her apron like a soft snowfall, and watched the queue for the Heron form itself. People shifted from foot to foot, shoulders brushing, voices low, the scent of lake water mingling with the smell of fresh bread from her oven behind her. She could feel the weight of the dough in her hands even before she touched it, a quiet pressure that settled in her palms whenever she thought of the crossing.

She had spent the night shaping loaves that held a quiet longing, the kind that made the crust crack open just a little too wide when it baked, releasing a scent that made anyone who passed the shop pause and look toward the water. The feeling had settled in her chest after yesterday’s chapter, when Jack’s voice had lingered in the air like an invitation that never quite arrived. She had felt it then, that almost‑invited‑in sensation, a warmth that brushed her skin and then slipped away, leaving her aware of how close she had come to something she dared not name.

Now, as she waited, the line moved slowly. A fisherman in a soaked coat stood near the front, his hands gripping a battered bucket, his eyes fixed on the water as if he could read the day’s catch in its ripple. Jack Mercer stood beside him, the ferry’s tiller resting loosely in his grip, his posture easy but his attention split between the fisherman’s words and the horizon. Sarah could see the line of his jaw, the way his throat worked when he spoke, the faint rise and fall of his collar as he breathed.

Without thinking, she stepped forward, the flour on her fingertips catching the morning light. Her hand moved to the edge of his coat, fingers finding the small fold of fabric just above his collarbone. She brushed the fabric smooth, her knuckles grazing the warm skin of his throat. The contact was brief, a deliberate press of bone against flesh, a touch meant only to steady the fabric.

Jack’s voice stopped mid‑sentence. The fisherman’s words trailed off, caught in the sudden silence. Jack’s eyes flicked to hers for a heartbeat, then dropped to the water, his breath catching as if he had forgotten how to draw air. His shoulders tightened, the muscles in his neck locking, his gaze fixed on a point far out on the lake where the light broke into shards.

Heat rose in Sarah’s cheeks, a flush spreading from collar to temples. A tremor began in her fingers, a tiny vibration that threatened to become a shake if she lingered. She forced herself to step back, pulling her hand away before the shaking could start. Turning on her heel, she walked away as the queue closed behind her; passengers shifted, whispers low, glances flicking between her and Jack as if they sensed something unseen passing between them.

She did not look back. Moving quickly toward Lantern Street, she entered as the bakery door swung open, the scent of yeast and warm wheat wrapping around her like a familiar hug. Her basket went down on the counter, her hands still tingling from the brief contact, and she began to work.

She took a lump of dough from the morning’s batch, feeling its cool, yielding surface under her palms. She pressed her weight into it, feeling the resistance give way as she folded the dough over itself, again and again. Each fold was a small confession: she had touched him on purpose, she wanted to feel the pulse beneath his skin, and she wanted to see if he would notice.

The dough responded to her intent. As she shaped the first loaf, she infused it with a mixture of hope and the faint, aching curiosity that had lived in her chest since the almost‑invited‑in feeling of yesterday. She rolled the loaf tight, sealed the seam, and set it on the baking stone. The oven’s heat rose, wrapping the loaf in a steady glow.

While the first loaf baked, she moved to the second. This time she allowed a softer feeling to guide her hands—a tenderness that reminded her of the way Jack’s voice had softened when he spoke of the ferry keeping its people. She shaped the loaf with a gentler press, her fingers moving slowly, feeling the dough stretch under her touch. She thought of the way his collar had felt under her knuckles, the way his breath had halted, and she let that memory settle into the dough like a quiet promise.

The third loaf came last. Her hands moved with a mixture of resolve and vulnerability, the kind that made her heart beat a little faster against her ribs. She pressed the dough firmly, then eased her touch, feeling the rise and fall of her own breath match the rhythm of her kneading. Shaping the loaf with care, she let the intention of the touch she had given him settle deep into its core.

When the timer chimed, she opened the oven door. The first loaf emerged, crust golden brown, a thin line of steam curling upward. She set it on the cooling rack and watched as Odalys Pike, who had been wiping tables in the inn’s common room, paused in her work, eyes drawn to the loaf. Odalys lifted a slice, inhaled deeply, and then a soft laugh escaped her, bright and sudden, followed immediately by a tear that traced a path down her cheek. She wiped the tear away with the back of her hand, laughing again, the sound warm and unguarded.

Sarah felt a swell in her chest, a mixture of relief and wonder. She turned to the second loaf, pulling it from the oven. Its crust was darker, almost caramel at the edges, the scent richer, tinged with something like lake mist and sun‑warmed stone. Odalys approached again, this time taking a bite more slowly. She closed her eyes, a smile spreading across her face, then opened them to find Sarah watching. Her laughter rose again, softer this time, mingling with a quiet sigh that seemed to carry both joy and a hint of sorrow. She reached out, squeezed Sarah’s forearm briefly, and said, “It’s like hearing a song you forgot you knew.”

Sarah smiled, feeling the heat of the bakery wrap around her, the flour on her cheeks, the echo of Jack’s halted breath still lingering in her mind. She turned to the third loaf, pulling it free. Its surface was speckled with flecks of herbs she had sprinkled without thinking, a tiny green that caught the light. She set it down, and Odalys came forward once more, her hands trembling just slightly as she broke the loaf open. The interior was soft, steam rising in delicate curls. Odalys took a piece, chewed slowly, then looked up at Sarah, eyes glistening. “It makes me want to cry and laugh at the same time,” she said, voice thick. “It feels like… like remembering a promise you didn’t know you made.”

Sarah watched her friend, feeling the truth of the words settle in her own bones. She had shaped three loaves in a row, each one carrying a different shade of the feeling she had given Jack that morning, a blend of deliberate touch and held breath, while the almost‑invited‑in sensation lingered since yesterday’s chapter. Bread had turned that feeling into something tangible that could be shared and that made Odalys react with both tears and laughter.

She wiped her hands on her apron, feeling the faint dust of flour settle into the creases of her skin. The bakery was quiet now, the oven’s glow fading to a soft ember. She leaned against the counter, shoulders relaxed. Her mind replayed the moment at the ferry queue: she recalled how Jack’s throat moved under her knuckles, how his breath stopped, and how she had walked away before her hands could shake.

She thought of the harbor lanterns Gideon Teague would light at dusk, each one a steady point of flame that seemed to hold a thought or a wish, a silent conversation across the water. She imagined standing beside one of those lanterns. The flame caught on her coat’s edge while the light reflected in the water. She wondered if Jack would ever feel the same pull toward her that she felt toward the lake’s horizon.

The morning light grew stronger, spilling through the bakery window and painting the floor in warm stripes. Sarah breathed in, feeling the scent of yeast and flour fill her lungs, feeling the quiet certainty that she had touched him on purpose, that she had let her hands speak what her voice could not yet say. She turned back to the counter, picked up a fresh lump of dough, and began to shape again, her palms ready to receive whatever feeling came next, her heart open to the quiet rhythm of the lake and the bread that carried it.
