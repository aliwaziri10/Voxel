Sarah found the note because the page would not lie flat.

She had taken Fenna’s recipe journal from the high shelf to check the proportions for a loaf with barley flour. The cover had softened at the corners, and the spine gave a small complaint when she opened it. Fenna had written in a firm hand, the letters leaning forward as if they were late for an appointment. Recipes filled the first half, each with corrections in the margins: less salt in damp weather, longer rest when the kitchen stayed cold, never trust the oven by sound alone.

Sarah had been learning those notes slowly. Fenna had rarely explained a mark while she was alive. She would tap a margin with one floury finger and say, “You’ll know when it matters.” Sarah had always assumed the old woman meant the bread.

The page for barley loaves had curled near the binding. Sarah pressed it flat, and a thin slip of paper caught against her thumb. It was not a loose scrap. The writing had been tucked into the inner margin, nearly hidden beneath the fold, with three dots and a short angled stroke beside it.

She knew the marks. Or she knew enough to know that they were not recipe instructions.

Sarah sat at the worktable. The ovens had not yet been lit. Beyond the front window, the street was still quiet, the shutters of the tailor’s shop closed and the Lamplight Inn’s lamps fading in the morning. She spread the journal open beneath the light and reached for the small lens Fenna had used when her eyes tired.

The note began with a name crossed out so thoroughly that the nib had torn the paper. Under it, Fenna had written: *The ferryman came back before the last lantern was dark.*

Sarah read the sentence again, slower this time, as if the words might alter under a closer look.

There were more words below, but the margin language twisted through them, dots grouped in threes, slashes set at odd angles, a little hooked mark repeated at the ends. Fenna had used the same signs in the recipes, though there they seemed to guide the hand: rest, turn, wait, cover. Sarah had learned a few meanings by watching the dough respond. She had never learned how the signs worked together when they were not about bread.

She bent closer. One mark might mean *left*. Another could mean *asked*, or perhaps *waited*. The line beneath it looked like *before the last run*, but the final word was smudged. Sarah traced the air above the paper, careful not to touch the ink.

A chill gathered along her arms despite the mild room.

Fenna had loved a man before Sarah was born. That much she knew. There had been a photograph once, a young woman in a pale dress standing at the harbor beside a man whose face had been blurred by sunlight. Fenna had put it away before Sarah could ask who he was. When Sarah had asked later, Fenna had said, “A person can be dear to you and still belong to a part of your life you do not tell.”

Sarah had thought it was an answer meant to end the conversation. Now the old words came back with a different weight.

She turned the page. The next note was shorter, written close to the binding. *He thought asking her to stay would make a cage of love.*

Her breath caught. The room seemed to contract around the page.

The sentence was clear enough. The marks around it were not. A line of dots continued beneath, but Fenna’s pen had grown faint, and the paper had been rubbed thin by the page opposite. Sarah lifted the journal toward the window. The morning light passed through the worn fibres, revealing a few strokes and then hiding them again.

She set it down with care.

The words had no name attached. No date, no place, no clue that could turn the note into a story she understood. The ferryman might have belonged to a different shore, a different time, a life Fenna had never described. Larkmere had been a ferry town for generations. There had been many men at the tiller, many women who watched from the landing, many departures made for reasons that seemed sensible to the person leaving.

Sarah knew all of that. Still, one thought had arrived before she could stop it.

Please do not let him be related to Jack.

Her palm came down flat on the page.

The gesture surprised her. She held the paper beneath her hand as if the words might lift away and find their own way to the harbor. Her fingers had begun to tremble. She pressed harder until the page stopped moving, then drew a slow breath through her nose.

It was absurd. She had no evidence that the ferryman in Fenna’s note had any connection to Jack Mercer. The handwriting could have been decades old, and Fenna had known people from beyond Larkmere. A shared occupation did not make a shared history. She could list every sensible reason not to let the thought settle.

Her hand stayed where it was.

The bakery bell rang outside as a cart passed the window. Sarah lifted her palm from the journal. A faint print of flour remained across the paper, whitening the edge of Fenna’s careful line. She brushed it away with the corner of a cloth, taking care not to smear the ink.

The ovens needed lighting. The starter needed feeding. The shop would open soon, and the town would expect bread whether Sarah had found a mystery in the margins or not.

She closed the journal, then opened it again.

The note was still there. The crossed-out name remained unreadable. The ferryman had come back before the last lantern was dark, and had believed that asking a woman to stay would make a cage of love. Sarah looked at the sentence until the letters lost their shape, then shut the cover and placed the journal beneath the counter.

She lit the ovens and measured the barley flour.

The morning work began with its usual demands. Water warmed in the bowl. The starter smelled sharp and alive. Sarah sifted the barley flour through her fingers, feeling the coarser grains catch at the creases of her palms. She added white flour, salt, and a spoonful of honey, then worked the mixture until it came together in a rough mass.

She had planned a simple loaf for the shelf. Now the dough carried the taste of honey against the earthy barley, sweetness held close to a darker flavour. She tried to keep her mind on the proportions. A little more water would soften the crumb. A longer rest would let the barley take it in. Her hands moved carefully, yet the dough tightened beneath her fingers each time her thoughts returned to the note.

*He thought asking her to stay would make a cage of love.*

Sarah folded the dough over itself. The surface split along one side. She pressed the tear closed, but the seam showed through the flour. She turned the dough and tried again. The next fold held, though the loaf remained uneven, its crown lifting at one end and sinking at the other.

She could start over. There was enough flour, and the morning was not yet busy. She could scrape the bowl clean and make a second batch with a clearer mind. Instead she covered the dough and let it rest as it was.

Fenna had taught her that bread did not improve when a baker bullied it. Certain dough needs time to accept what has already been done to it. Sarah had once thought the lesson was only about gluten. She was less sure now.

She wiped the table and opened the shop.

The first customers came for their usual purchases. A fisherman bought rye and asked whether the wind would hold. Sarah said she thought it would, though she had barely looked outside. A woman from the upper lane bought two rolls for her children. An old man stood by the window to eat a heel of crust, taking small bites and watching the street with the patient expression of a person who had nowhere he needed to be.

Sarah wrapped the bread, made change, and answered each greeting. The work kept her voice steady. Beneath the counter, Fenna’s journal waited with its hidden line of writing. She could feel its presence without looking down.

When the loaf had risen, she uncovered it. The dough had grown, but not evenly. Its surface leaned toward the high side, and the seam had opened a little near the base. Sarah dusted the board and turned the loaf out. She tried to shape the crown into a smooth round, drawing the outer skin tight with her palms. It held for a moment, then relaxed into its earlier slant.

She left it that way.

The blade made a shallow cut across the top. Sarah slid the loaf into the oven and watched the door close. Heat gathered in the room, carrying the smell of barley and honey. She stood with her hands on the edge of the counter until the warmth reached her face.

The bread would tell the truth whether she wanted it to or not.

By late morning, the loaf had turned deep gold. The honey darkened the crust, and the barley gave it a faint, nutty scent. Sarah tapped the bottom and heard the hollow note she wanted. She set it on the rack and waited for the crust to stop singing as it cooled.

Odalys Pike arrived from the Lamplight Inn with a basket hooked over one arm. She was in a brisk mood, talking about a guest who had mistaken the pantry door for the washroom and apologised to a shelf of preserves. Sarah laughed at the right places and packed the inn’s order. Odalys noticed the barley loaf on the rack.

“That one’s new.”

“I was trying a different proportion.”

“May I?”

Sarah cut a slice. The crust broke in a crisp line, and the inside showed a warm amber crumb, close and tender. Odalys took the plate to the window. She ate half the slice, then slowed. Her eyes shifted from the bread to the harbor street beyond the glass.

Sarah watched her shoulders settle. Odalys did not sigh or smile. She held the remaining piece between two fingers and seemed to have forgotten it was there.

“Is it too sweet?” Sarah asked.

“No.”

“Too heavy?”

Odalys shook her head. She placed the slice on the plate, though she had eaten only half. “It begins with comfort,” she said after a while. “Then there’s a shadow under it. A sweetness that knows it may not last.”

Sarah looked down at the knife in her hand. The edge caught the light. “I didn’t mean to make it sad.”

“I don’t think it’s sad.” Odalys turned back toward the harbor. “It feels like hoping for a door to open while already preparing yourself to hear it close.”

Sarah set the knife on the board.

Odalys did not ask what had happened. That was her kindness. She had a talent for reading the bread, but she never acted as if it gave her ownership of the person who had made it.

“I found a note of Fenna’s,” Sarah said, before she could decide whether to speak.

Odalys looked at her, waiting.

“It was in the margin of her journal. About a ferryman. I can’t read all of it.”

Odalys’s face changed, not with surprise exactly, but with the care of a person who had reached a familiar turning in a road and did not know whether to point it out. “Fenna kept a great deal in those margins.”

“Did she ever tell you about a man she loved before she came to Larkmere?”

Odalys folded the napkin around the uneaten half slice. “She told me that love could be true and still be badly handled. She didn’t tell me his name.”

Sarah felt her chest tighten. “Was he a ferryman?”

“I don’t know.” Odalys met her eyes. “And I won’t guess at a life she chose not to explain.”

Sarah nodded. She was grateful for the answer and disappointed by it, both at once. The note had offered a door, and now it stood shut again, with only the words visible through the crack.

Odalys wrapped the barley loaf in clean paper. “I’ll take this to the inn. The cook will like it.”

“You’ve only eaten half a slice.”

“That’s enough to know what it says.” She tucked the parcel into her basket. “Keep the rest for yourself, Sarah.”

After Odalys left, the bakery seemed larger and quieter. Sarah carried the journal from beneath the counter and opened it to the marked page. The ink looked no clearer than before. She traced the air above the crossed-out name, following the torn fibres with her eyes.

Sarah took a scrap of clean paper from the drawer and copied the marks as carefully as she could. Three dots, a slanted line, the hooked sign at the end. On a fresh page they looked less like a private language and more like a row of small, deliberate pauses. She tried to arrange them into words, then stopped before she could force a meaning that was not there. Fenna had never liked guesses dressed up as answers. Sarah could almost hear her saying so, dryly, while turning a loaf onto its side to cool. She put the scrap inside the journal, not over the note but behind it, and closed the cover with both hands.

The ovens ticked softly as they cooled. Outside, a gull landed on the harbor wall and folded its wings. Sarah set her palm beside the sentence, not on top of it this time. The words remained where Fenna had left them, narrow and dark in the margin.

She did not turn the page.
