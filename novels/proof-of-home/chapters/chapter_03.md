The basket was heavier on the walk down than it had been on the table.

Sarah had packed it before the oven cooled: cinnamon buns under a folded cloth, a rye loaf tucked in beside them, a small jar of honey for the Lamplight Inn. The standing order was ordinary. Odalys had asked for the same things every week since Sarah reopened the shop, and the basket had gone down to the dock so often that the route lived in her feet and needed no help from her head.

Her head was the trouble. At the corner of Lantern Street she checked the knot on the cloth. At the old pump she checked it again. By the time she reached the harbor wall she had undone the twine and tied it twice over, and she stood a moment with her thumb pressed to the bow, annoyed at herself, as if the knot had been the one to start this.

The morning had opened clear after a night of mist. Sunlight lay along the damp planks in bright strips, and the dock was crowded: a cart stacked with apple crates, two fishermen disagreeing over a net, a woman hauling a small boy back from a puddle by the hood of his coat, and a line of passengers waiting for the Heron with their collars turned up against the wind.

She slowed at the head of the gangway. She had come here before with bread for Jack Mercer, but the first loaf had been a custom, and the second had been a thank-you, and this basket belonged to the inn. It should have been easier than either of them. It was not.

Nell Mercer was coiling a line near the stern. She looked up, and her whole face brightened before she wrestled it into a businesslike frown.

“Morning, Sarah.”

“Morning. Is your uncle aboard?”

“Wheelhouse.” Nell tugged the rope into a tidy circle. “You can leave the basket with me, if you like.”

“I can carry it the last few steps.”

“Of course you can.”

Nell’s eyes dropped to the knot Sarah had retied three times and rested there. She said nothing about it, which was worse than anything she might have said. She lifted the coil and carried it off toward the cleat, taking a great deal of time over the loop.

The deck moved gently under Sarah’s shoes. The Heron smelled of damp timber and oil and cold water, with yesterday’s smoke caught in the canvas around the wheelhouse. She knew the boat better than she had in her first week, though she had only ever seen it from the dock or through the bakery window, and she noticed now what she had not before: that every rope had its worn place, that the brass was polished by hands that never stopped to think about it. Everything aboard bore the quiet evidence of being used and kept.

Jack stood at the wheelhouse door with one hand on the frame. He wore a dark wool sweater under his canvas jacket, and a loose strand of hair had come down over his forehead, and he had not pushed it back. He was looking at the far shore when she stepped aboard.

Then he looked at her.

The noise of the dock did not stop. A gull cried above the roofline. A crate hit the boards. The little boy protested as his mother lifted him clear of the puddle. All of it went on exactly as before, and Sarah could not find anywhere to put her eyes except his.

There was no smile in his face to soften it, and no polite glance to make it easier. He only looked, steadily, as if the distance between them had become a question and he meant to wait out the answer.

The heat climbed from her collar to her cheeks. She had stood at an open oven in a summer kitchen without turning red. She had argued suppliers out of charging her twice for flour, bargained over butter, refused a bad delivery, and told a grown man that his loaf needed another hour. She could not hold the gaze of a man who had not said a word.

Her eyes dropped to the planks.

A dark nail head sat near the toe of her shoe, and beside it a thin line of tar had dried in the grain. She studied both with a concentration she had never given a surface in her life.

“Standing order,” she said, and her voice came out level enough, which surprised her. “Cinnamon buns for the inn, rye for the table. Odalys asked me to bring the honey as well.”

She held the basket out.

He came forward and took it by the handle, putting his hands beneath hers with room left between his fingers and her own. No skin touched skin. The carefulness of it ought to have made the exchange simpler. Instead she noticed everything: the broad span of his palm, a small pale scar near the base of his thumb, the way his fingers closed over the wicker after hers let go.

“Thank you,” he said.

“You’re welcome.”

Neither of them moved.

A passenger with a leather satchel squeezed past, murmuring an apology, and Jack shifted back to let him through. Sarah stepped toward the rail, and the gap between them widened by a hand’s breadth. She should have taken it. She had handed over the order. She could go back to the shop and see to the ovens and begin the next batch. There was always work, and she had always been good at finding it.

His eyes came back to her face.

“The rye was good,” he said. “The last loaf.”

The words were plain, almost practical, and she felt them all the way down. She had heard compliments. Customers praised the crust and the crumb and the butter in a bun, and told her a loaf had reminded them of a kitchen they had not stood in for years. Jack did not speak like a man praising a baker. He spoke like a man who had carried the taste about with him for days and had only now found a place to set it down.

“I’m glad,” she said.

He seemed about to add more. His lips parted and closed. His hand moved on the handle of the basket.

Sarah waited. She could not have said what for, only that leaving before he finished would feel like walking away from a door at the exact moment it began to open.

Behind him Nell cleared her throat with tremendous force.

“Passengers are ready, Uncle.”

Jack glanced toward the gangway. The moment loosened like a held breath, and Sarah let out her own, though she had not known she was keeping it.

“I should get back,” she said.

“Yes.”

It was the wrong answer, or only the answer he could manage. She smiled at the planks, because the alternative was to smile at him and she was not ready for him to see that, and then she walked down the gangway with her pace held even until the dock gave way to cobbles.

At the corner she looked back.

He had turned to the wheelhouse and was setting the basket on the bench, arranging the cloth so it would not slip. His hands moved with their usual care. The sun lay along one side of his face and left the other in the cool shade of the roof. He did not look up.

Sarah faced the street and walked home with the whole length of it burning on her face.

The bakery was warm when she opened the door. The first trays had cooled, the starter crock rested under its cloth, and a faint buttery smell hung in the air. Everything stood where she had left it. The dough in the covered bowl had risen at exactly the right pace, and the oven held its heat, and the flour bin gaped open with a white dusting along its rim.

She tied on her apron and washed her hands.

Flour, water, starter, salt. She measured each with great care and set the bowl square to the edge of the table. She would make a plain loaf for the afternoon shelf, she told herself. No honey. No spice. Nothing to disturb the work.

The dough was cool and slack under her palms. She pressed, folded, turned, and the rhythm began to settle her, the familiar resistance giving way as the flour drank the water. She ought to have been thinking about the shape, the tension across the surface, the way the seam must close beneath. Instead she saw his hand around the basket handle. She heard the low roughness of his voice on the word good.

She folded the dough too far and tore the skin.

She stopped. A small tear, easy to mend. She pinched the edges together and dusted the place with flour and began again, and her hands were warm now, and the dough went from yielding to clinging. She pushed harder than she needed to, as if force would keep her thoughts where she put them.

The loaf took shape unevenly. One side rounded; the other sloped toward the table. She tried to correct it, turning the dough and drawing the skin taut, and each correction pulled the seam crooked. In the end she laid it in its basket to rise with one shoulder tilted, like a person listening from a doorway.

When she lifted the cloth, the dough had spread wider than she meant. Its surface was warm under her fingertips, though the room had cooled. She scored the crown, one clean line down the middle, and the blade caught near the end and dragged sideways. She looked at the crooked mark for a moment and slid the loaf into the oven before she could start over.

It came out golden, with one shoulder risen high and the other sunk toward the cut. It was warm through the cloth when she lifted it. She held it against her for a moment, the heat soaking into her palms, and understood that it carried the muddle of her thoughts as plainly as a face carries a blush. It had not gone bitter. There was warmth in it, and a pull toward the water, and the awkward lift of a hope that had not yet found its feet.

She set it on the rack to cool.

The bell over the door chimed. Odalys Pike came in from the inn with her coat unbuttoned and her dark hair pinned up in a hurry, a folded napkin over one arm and the look of a woman who had come for bread and would not leave without a conversation.

“Your delivery made it,” she said. “The buns survived the crossing. The cook has already claimed the honey.”

“Good.”

“And the rye is on the table. I thought I’d come and see what you were making for the afternoon.”

Sarah turned to the rack. “A loaf that refused to be round.”

Odalys came to stand beside her and studied it. She did not touch it at once. Her eyes followed the lopsided shoulder, the slanted score, the place where the crust had opened in a thin dark seam.

“Looks warm.”

“It is.”

“May I?”

Sarah found the bread knife and a plate. Odalys waited while she cut a slice from the middle, and steam rose from the crumb in a faint breath. The inside was soft and open, though one side had risen higher than the other. Sarah set the slice on the plate with a small curl of butter and watched it begin to melt into the pores.

Odalys took her first bite at the front window.

For a moment she said nothing. Her gaze drifted through the glass toward the harbor, where the Heron was easing away from the dock with passengers aboard. She chewed slowly. Then she let out a sigh that began low in her chest and left her shoulders slack, and the plate dipped a little in her hand.

Sarah went very still.

Odalys took another bite, her eyes on the water. “Oh, that is a lonely little loaf.”

“It’s only misshapen.”

“I wasn’t talking about its shape.” The innkeeper turned the slice in her fingers and looked at the uneven crumb. “It’s warm. Nearly cheerful, to start. Then you reach the middle and there’s a tug, as if you’ve heard a door open and can’t decide whether to go through it.”

Sarah kept her hands on the counter. She could feel flour caught in the lines of her palms. “Perhaps it wanted another minute in the oven.”

“It wanted you to stop thinking about the dock.”

Sarah looked at her.

Odalys lifted the bread in a small, helpless gesture. “I have no wish to embarrass you. But I’ve eaten your bread long enough to know when you’ve put a feeling in it. This one makes me want to stand at the window until the ferry comes back.”

Out on the water the Heron had cleared the first buoy, a small dark shape drawing a white line behind it. Jack stood at the wheelhouse, too far off to see through the bakery glass. Sarah watched him all the same.

“Does it make you sad?” she asked.

“No.” Odalys took one more bite and shook her head. “Not sad. It makes me hopeful, which is a far more inconvenient thing to be before lunch.”

Sarah laughed despite herself. It came out too bright and stopped too fast. She picked up the knife and scraped a crumb from the board.

Odalys set the plate on the sill. “You might have said he was a fine-looking man.”

“He isn’t the subject.”

“I didn’t say he was.”

Sarah folded the towel over the knife. “He was only being polite.”

“Was he?”

“He thanked me for the order. He said the rye was good.”

“That’s what he said aloud.”

Sarah looked down at the towel. She could not tell Odalys what had passed between the words. It had not been a promise, or even an invitation. It had been a look that held longer than courtesy required, and the effort of standing still beneath it, and she could not make it sound sensible even inside her own head.

Odalys finished the slice and did not reach for another. She stood with her palms on the sill and watched the ferry until it slipped behind the curve of the shore.

“Fenna used to say bread told the truth before people were ready to,” she said.

“Fenna said a great many things.”

“She did.” Odalys turned, gentler now. “You don’t have to tell me what this is. You don’t have to decide what it means. But don’t punish yourself for having a feeling before you’ve found a place to put it.”

Sarah felt the words land and disliked how easily they found their mark. “I have work to do.”

“I know. You always do.”

Odalys wrapped the rest of the crust in the napkin she had brought. At the door she paused and looked back at the loaf on the rack. “Save me the heel, will you?”

“You’ve already had a slice.”

“Then save me the heel as well.”

The bell chimed behind her. Sarah watched her cross the street toward the inn with her coat swinging at her knees, and then she turned back to the kitchen.

The loaf had cooled enough to handle. She lifted it and felt its weight settle into both palms, the high shoulder pressing into her left hand. It was an imperfect thing. It would still feed a person. It would be good with butter, and with soup, and with the last of the honey in the jar, and the feeling inside it did not make it any less bread.

She set it beside the window where the afternoon light reached the rack. It sat there with its one shoulder raised, listening.
