The Heron ran true again in the morning, and Jack did not trust it.

He had come down the water road in the black before dawn with his collar up and his mind a long way behind him, still on a bow rail, still on a woman’s gloved fists shut in his coat. He had not slept. He had lain on top of the blankets in the cottage with the stove cold and watched the window go from black to pewter, and when it was grey enough to count as day he put the kettle on and forgot it until it screamed. Then he went down to the boat. It was the only place he knew how to be.

She caught on the first turn of the key. The engine settled to its note, low and level, and the wheel met his hands as if it had been waiting to be asked. He took her out past the harbor wall with frost on the rail and a band of cold light along the east shore, and he found he was steering with the care of a man carrying a full cup through a crowded room. The lake lay flat to the horizon. Nothing in the whole breadth of it pushed back.

The passengers came aboard with loaves.

He knew the loaves before he knew the faces. They rode in cloth or paper or bare under an arm, and he could feel the warmth of them from the wheelhouse door, and hear a small dry crackling, like a fire deciding whether to start. The fishermen had bought them. So had the twins from the north lane, and the woman in the green scarf, and the old man with the canvas satchel, who took his place by the stern with the loaf in his lap and looked at the water with a face Jack had never seen on him in all the years of the crossing.

Nobody spoke. Halfway over, the woman in the green scarf broke off a piece and ate it, and her face softened, and she glanced at the wheelhouse and then very quickly at the sky. One by one the others followed. He watched it travel down the deck as he had once watched a squall travel down the lake, a darkening on the water that moved from bench to bench. Joy first, loud and plain. Then under it, patient as the swell beneath a calm, the want.

Every person aboard was looking at him. Not one of them was looking at him. He kept his eyes on the lake, and it did not help.

At the lull between crossings he climbed Lantern Street and told himself he was going about the standing order.

The bell over the shop door was louder than he remembered. She was behind the counter with her sleeves pushed up and flour to the wrist, and when she looked up the whole front of the shop seemed to lean toward him: the racks, the lamp, the window with its iron hook. There was nobody else in the room. For the first time since the lake, nobody could see them.

Neither of them knew what to do about it.

“Good morning,” said Jack, which was what he had said every morning for a season.

“Good morning,” said Sarah, to the third button of his coat. Then she raised her eyes and flushed from collar to hairline, and he felt his own ears answer her. “There is a loaf on the rack for the far landing. I made it with my eyes open.”

“I did not know there was another way.”

“There is. It is how I made all the others.” She laughed, shakily, and pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth. “I am sorry. I have not slept. I think I am a little drunk.”

He crossed the floor in four strides. He had counted them, as he counted fathoms on an unfamiliar shore, and at the counter he put out his hand across the boards, palm up, the way a man holds it out for a line. She looked at it. Then she laid her own in it, flour and all.

That was everything. A hand, small and white in his, and the pulse going in her wrist against the base of his thumb. It was nothing a man could be arrested for. His composure drained out through the soles of his boots like water through a sprung plank.

The bell rang.

The grocer’s wife was in the doorway with her basket on her arm, and Jack had let go before the bell stopped swinging and clasped both hands behind his back like a boy caught at a window. “Good morning, Jack!” she said, much too brightly. “Lovely day for it.” She looked at the floor. She studied the ceiling. She regarded the rack of rolls with a concentration that threatened the rolls. Her whole face worked to keep a thing in, and she bought one roll, which she did not need, and left with the tact of a woman who meant to tell her sister by noon.

Sarah laid her forehead on her folded arms. Her shoulders shook.

“It is not funny,” said Jack.

“It is very funny.” Her voice came out muffled. “Go and cross your lake, Captain.”

By noon there was nobody in Larkmere who needed to be told.

He walked down the street with Sarah beside him, the two of them carrying the standing loaf between them in its blue cloth like a christening gift, her sleeve against his sleeve the whole way down. On the steps of the Lamplight Inn Odalys Pike rose from her stool in full view of the harbor, put two fingers in her mouth, and whistled. He had last heard that whistle stop a fight in the net sheds. Then she flung both arms into the air and shouted, “About time!”

Every head on the quay turned. A gull lifted off a piling in alarm. Gideon Teague, halfway up the street on his ladder, where he had no business to be at noon, took off his cap and held it over his heart. Jack found that he wanted very much to go below the waterline and stay there until spring.

“Let them,” said Sarah. Her face was scarlet, and she was laughing, and in front of the fishermen and the clerk and the length of the quay she took his hand and held it. He held on. It was the one steady thing on the pier. Her palm was warm against his, and the warmth climbed his arm and settled behind his ribs, where for years there had been a cold place the size of a closed fist.

Then the noon crossing began to fill.

They came down the pier by twos and threes, with baskets, with parcels, with a crate of cabbages. The planks drummed. “Morning, Jack!” “Miss Quill!” “Fine weather!” Each greeting was kind. Each one wore a face that had tasted her bread and was trying with all its might to look as though it had not. He felt the crowd press in on the narrow boards, and with it a hundred friendly eyes, and he let his fingers open. She did not cling. She raised her face to him with her lips tucked in at one corner and her fingers trailing away through his, and he saw that she understood, and that it cost her, and that she would not say so.

He counted. It was a habit of the water: when you could not change the weather, you reckoned the hours of it. Six hours to the last lamp.

The afternoon crossing was worse, because the loaves had begun to sing.

It started aboard, a thin crackling that rose from the benches as the crusts cooled, and it was louder than it had been that morning. It carried over the water. He saw people at the far landing turn their heads before the Heron was halfway in. By the time she nosed to the dock the landing keeper had come out to the end of the boards in his shirtsleeves, and three old men sat in a row on a wall with their faces turned toward the sound, like men on a bank listening for a boat. Every one of them smiled at him as he threw the line. The smiles were warm, delighted and entirely without mercy.

“Uncle,” said Nell from the stern, in a voice of deep patience, “you have coiled that rope the wrong way. Three times.”

He looked down. He had. He uncoiled it. She returned to her work with her braid swinging and her spine very straight, a girl who had decided to be tactful and was paying heavily for it.

Between interruptions his fingers kept finding hers: a brush at the gangway, her knuckles as she passed him the rolls, there and gone before anyone could count it. He tried three times that afternoon to be alone with the baker for the length of one sentence. At the rail of the pier, where she had come down with the evening rolls, he got as far as her name before the twins from the north lane arrived in a rush, shouting, “Hello, Mr. Mercer! Hello, Miss Quill!” and stood there beaming up at them both. At the head of the gangway a fisherman asked him whether he thought the frost would hold, and was not to be moved until Jack had given an opinion on frost. And at the very end of the day she came to the wheelhouse door with a word ready, and Gideon Teague said, “A word, Jack, if I may,” and the word was about a lamp.

Not one of them meant any harm. That was the worst of it. He felt like a line paid out and snubbed short, and paid out, and snubbed again, until the whole length of him sang with the strain.

The shop was closing when he got there at dusk. She had the shutter half down and the lamp turned low, and she glanced up from a flour sack at his step and said, in a voice that was almost casual, “Would you carry those down to the cellar for me? They are heavy.”

They were not heavy. He carried one on each shoulder. He understood perfectly.

The cellar stair was the narrowest in Larkmere, steep and stone-sided and turning once in the dark. It had one lamp at the head and none below. He went first, with the sacks bending him over, and he felt her follow, close enough that the light threw their two shadows into one on the wall. At the bottom the air was warm and smelled of yeast and old wood. The eight grey loaves sat on their shelf where they had sat all summer, and the cellar closed its warmth around them and around him.

He set the sacks down. When he turned she was on the third stair, so that for once her eyes were level with his. She held the lamp low. She did not speak. She set it on the stair beside her, very carefully, and laid her free palm flat on the front of his coat.

He had thought, on the lake, that he understood it. He had not. This was a mouth he had time to learn. He caught the stair rail in his left hand and felt the old rope scar press into the wood, and her fingers closed on his collar and drew him down by it, and he went. She tasted of salt and cold air and faintly of woodsmoke. She made a low sound against his mouth and he lost the thread of every plain thing he knew: the depth, the draft, the set of the current, his own name. The stone was cool against his shoulder. The warmth ran all one way. He felt her heart through two layers of wool, quick and hard, and he gave up the rail and slid his fingers into the hair at the back of her head, and she rose up the last inch of the stair to meet him.

A door banged overhead.

“Sarah?” It was Odalys, in the shop, in a carrying voice. “Are you down there? I have come about the order and your back door was unlatched, so I let myself in. Hello!”

They came apart like a hull sliding off a slip. Sarah’s hair had come down on one side and her collar stood crooked and her mouth was bruised and bright. He had never seen anything so beautiful, and it was going up a flight of stairs without him.

“Coming!” she called, in a voice an octave too high. She snatched the light from the stair. She looked back once, and her eyes said everything a woman can say with her lips shut, and then she was gone up the stair and had left him the dark.

He stood in it a long time with his forehead against the stone. The wanting did not ease. He had thought a kiss was an answer, a thing that would draw off the pressure the way a sluice draws a flood. It was nothing of the kind. It was a gate opened a hand’s width on a river that wanted the whole valley. Overhead the two women laughed in the shop, and a chair scraped, and the world went on being kind.

When he could trust his legs he climbed the stair and let himself out by the back and did not meet anyone at all.

The cottage at the end of the water road was cold. He lit the stove and did not eat. He sat on the edge of the bench with his boots on and the taste of her still in his mouth, and he counted the hours as a man counts the marks on a sounding line. Nine to the first run. Nine hours in which a woman would sleep or not sleep a mile off, and he would sit with a palm that remembered the shape of her head.

He wanted her where no one was looking. He wanted a door, and a latch, and a night with no lamps in it. He wanted it so plainly that it frightened him, and under the wanting lay the other thing, older and smaller: a boy at a pier rail with a grandfather’s hand on his shoulder, watching a grey coat go up a plank.

Stay. He turned the word over. It was one syllable. He had never said it to anyone in his life, and it had the weight of an anchor.

She had told him she would give him her answer to his face when she knew. She had not given it. He would not ask. A man who asked before then had no business at a rail, and whatever she decided she would decide with her eyes open, in her own time, on her own side of the water. He could wait. He had waited on the lake for most of a lifetime. That was what he was good at.

He crossed to the window. The harbor lay below him in a long black curve, with the pier lamps strung along it like beads on a wet line, and above them, on Lantern Street, one square of light on an iron hook, small and steady, hung where the water could see it.

He turned his own lamp up a little. He set it on the sill where it could be seen from the lake.

Neither of them put out a light.
