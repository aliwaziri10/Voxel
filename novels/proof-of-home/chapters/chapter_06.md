Jack woke before the light had fully slipped over the horizon, the narrow bunk of the ferry cabin still holding the cool press of night. He lay with his eyes open, the memory of Sarah’s fingers brushing his beneath the flour crate yesterday returning in thin waves. It had been only a second, the tip of her thumb grazing the ridge of his hand, but it lingered like a current that kept finding the same gap in the rocks. He could feel the echo of that contact in the pads of his own fingers, a faint pressure that seemed to settle into the bone and refuse to leave.

He turned toward the small window. Spring light had begun to silver the water, and a fish broke the surface near the reeds. Jack pressed his palm against the bunk rail, then sat up and reached for his trousers. There was work to do before the first passengers boarded: the engine to check, the lines to inspect, the channel markers to read. He dressed and went on deck before the memory could pull him back into it.

By the time the island showed against the paling sky, Jack had dressed and started the engine. He took the longer route around the island, adding several minutes to the crossing. The cliffs caught the first light in narrow gold bands. He watched the markers and kept the Heron steady, trying not to replay the previous evening.

He guided the ferry out of the harbor, the engine’s low thrum a steady counterpoint to the quiet of his thoughts. As the Heron moved away from the dock, he ran the sentence over in his mind, adjusting its course, trying to find a line he could hold. He wanted to say, “I thought about your hand.” It was too plain to disguise and too honest to risk. He tried instead, “The flour got aboard safely.” That sounded like a report from a man who had forgotten how to speak. He abandoned it and watched the water ahead.

The boat rounded the island’s northern tip, the shoreline pulling away in a curve of pine and stone. A crosscurrent caught the stern just beyond the marker and pushed the Heron off her line. Jack eased the throttle, corrected the tiller, and waited until the bow answered before increasing speed. A man near the rail shifted his basket to keep it from sliding. Jack nodded to him without taking his eyes from the water. He had crossed this stretch in fog, rain, and the first hard wind of autumn; the current was ordinary, and the correction should have been easy. Yet he had to check the marker twice before he trusted the boat’s angle. He tightened his grip, then deliberately loosened it. The Heron held her course. He had no excuse to blame the water for his distraction. As the sun climbed, warmth seeped through his coat and reached the back of his neck. He let the rehearsed sentence sit unanswered until the Lantern Street landing came into view. Sarah stood on the dock, her apron tied loosely around her waist, watching the boat approach.

He had planned to ask whether she needed anything carried ashore. When Sarah looked at him, the question seemed foolishly formal. She tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear and waited for the ferry to touch the dock. Jack secured the line, checked the cleat, and stepped aside for the passengers to disembark. A woman thanked him for taking the longer route around the island; the water had been smoother there, she said. He answered with a nod. Sarah had heard her, too. Her gaze moved from him to the empty stretch of water behind the ferry, then returned to his face. Jack had the odd impression that she could tell he had chosen the route for no reason he was prepared to name.

After the last passenger had left, he tied the Heron in and checked the knot twice. The first check was enough. The second was an excuse to remain near the bakery door without admitting that was where he meant to go. He crossed the lane when the dock had cleared, hands empty, his rehearsed sentence still set in his mind.

Sarah was at the counter when he entered. She had flour along one cheek and a loaf resting on the peel. Her gaze rose to his, then moved briefly to his hands. He felt the glance as plainly as a shift in wind.

“Morning,” she said.

“Morning.”

He had come to tell her he had thought about the touch. He had come to say that it had followed him through the night and onto the water. Instead, he looked at the loaf, at the paper folded beside it, at the narrow gap between the counter and the door.

“Did you need bread?” she asked.

“No.” The answer came too quickly. He tried to soften it. “I mean, not today.”

“All right.”

She did not look offended. That made it worse. She turned back to the loaf and slid the peel beneath it with practiced care. Jack watched her hands move, steady and sure, and wondered if they had been steady when she walked home after the crate. He had no right to ask. He had no right to make a brief accidental touch into a question she had to answer.

“I took the long route around the island,” he said.

Sarah glanced up again. “Was the channel blocked?”

“No. The water was clear.”

The sentence sounded thin as soon as he said it. Sarah waited, as if she knew he had more to say. Jack looked toward the open door. He could not tell her that he had been thinking about her all night, so he let the silence stand.

“Good,” she said at last. “It’s a fine morning for it.”

“Yes.”

The oven door opened behind her. Heat moved through the room and touched his face. Sarah lifted the loaf from the peel and set it on the rack. The small movement gave him a way out, and he took it.

“I should get back to the ferry.”

“Of course.”

She did not ask him to stay. He had not given her a reason to. Jack stepped outside and let the door close between them. The bell made its light, brief sound. He stood on the stones until a cart rattled past, then turned toward the dock with the sentence still unspoken.

The walk back seemed shorter than the walk there. Jack kept his gaze on the uneven stones and counted the places where rain had darkened the mortar. At the dock, the Heron rocked against her lines, the bow lifting each time a small wave reached the pilings. He checked the knot at the cleat, tested the stern line, and moved a loose coil away from the gangway so it would not catch a passenger’s boot. The work required no thought, only the sequence he had followed for years. He tightened the bow line by one turn, then loosened it when the hull settled too close to the piling. The boat shifted free. Jack stepped aboard, took up the brush, and began again where he had stopped.

Later, after the morning rush had passed and the shop had settled into a quieter rhythm, Jack found himself back on the ferry’s empty deck. The dawn had deepened, the sky a clear blue that stretched over the lake like a wide‑eyed gaze. He moved to the starboard side, the planks worn smooth by years of feet and salt, and began to scrub the deck with a stiff brush. The bristles scraped against the wood, sending up a faint spray of water and the occasional fleck of old paint. Each stroke was deliberate, each push of his arms a way to wear down the thought that kept circling in his mind like a line that kept slipping its cleat.

He thought of the crate on the gangplank, the weight pulling both their shoulders forward, the hidden space beneath the canvas where their fingers met. The contact had been light, barely more than a brush, yet he could still feel the rough edge of the crate under his palm and the warmth of her fingers against his. He could not tell whether she had paused on purpose. The uncertainty stayed with him.

He scrubbed harder, the brush digging into the grain, his shoulders beginning to ache from the effort. The ache was a welcome distraction, a physical counterpoint to the mental loop that refused to unwind. He focused on the rhythm of his arms, the way the brush moved back and forth, the sound of bristles against wood, the smell of damp pine and lake water rising from the deck. Each stroke was a small act of containment, a way to keep the feeling from spilling over into action.

Nell came up from the stern with a coil of line over one shoulder. She stopped beside the wet patch he had scrubbed pale. “Planning to take the boards off next?”

“They needed cleaning.”

“They were clean yesterday.”

“Then they needed cleaning again.”

She set the coil down and crouched to inspect the rope’s frayed end. She teased two loose strands free, tested the splice, and tied a short whipping around the damaged place. Jack went back to the brush. He could feel her watching him, but she did not ask what had put him in such a mood. Nell had a way of leaving a question hanging in the air until a person answered it by mistake, without asking twice.

After a while she picked up the heel of bread from the bench and held it out. “You dropped this.”

“I didn’t.”

“Then you left it where a gull could take it.” She placed it back on the bench, just beyond the reach of the rail. “Eat it before it goes hard.”

Jack set the brush down. “You’ve got lines to check.”

“I’ve checked them.” Nell stood, brushing damp grit from her palms. “Twice, in fact.” She carried the coil toward the bow and left him with the bread and the wet boards.

He knew there was no one to stop him, no rule that barred him from speaking, no external force that held him back. The dock was empty, the ferry silent aside from his own movements, the lake calm and waiting. Yet he stayed still, his hands moving, his eyes fixed on the horizon, because the stubborn part of him chose to hold back, to let the moment sit unspoken rather than risk breaking the quiet that had become shelter between them.

When his arms finally trembled with fatigue, he set the brush down and leaned against the rail, the wood cool against his forearms. He looked out over the water, the sun now high enough to send a bright glitter across the lake’s surface. In his peripheral vision, he noticed a small heel of crust resting on the bench beside him, a piece of Sarah’s bread that had been left there after yesterday’s exchange. It was golden brown, the edges slightly crisp, the interior soft and steaming still in his memory.

He picked up the heel and took another bite. Honey and rosemary lingered on his tongue. He had eaten Sarah’s bread hundreds of times, but this piece made him think of the way she had held the loaf out to him, waiting for him to take it. He stopped chewing and looked toward the bakery before remembering that he was already halfway across the lake.

He set the heel on the bench and brushed the crumbs from his palm. A gull landed on the stern rail and cocked its head toward the bread. Jack shooed it away with a sharp movement of his arm, then looked at the heel as if surprised to find he had protected it. The ferry needed his attention; the next marker was coming up fast. He took the tiller and corrected the course, leaving the bread where it was.

He remembered the crate on the gangplank, the strain in both their arms, and the instant her fingers found his beneath the edge. He had felt her pause. He could not know whether she had meant to stay there or had simply needed a firmer grip. The uncertainty was worse than a clear answer. He corrected the tiller by a fraction and watched the bow return to its line.

Jack stood, the deck still damp from his scrubbing, and moved toward the helm. He placed his hands on the tiller, feeling the familiar grain under his palms, the steady resistance of the boat as it waited for his direction. The lake lay before him, calm and inviting, the sun casting a long silver path that stretched toward the far shore. He took a breath, the air filling his lungs with the scent of water and spring, and gave the tiller a small turn, guiding the Heron back toward the harbor.

The ferry slipped forward, its wake drawing a white seam behind it. Jack kept his eyes on the channel. The heel of bread lay on the bench beside him, one bite missing, its torn crumb exposed to the air. He reached for it, then changed his mind and took the tiller in both hands. At the next marker, he corrected the course by a fraction. The Heron answered at once. The thought of Sarah did not. He loosened his grip, then set it again when the bow met a crosscurrent. He watched the shoreline pass and kept the boat on its usual line, though his fingers tightened whenever the wind shifted from Lantern Street. He did not look back toward the shop, not once. The wake folded over itself behind the Heron and disappeared.
