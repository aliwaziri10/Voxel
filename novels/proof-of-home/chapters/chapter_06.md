Jack woke before the light had fully slipped over the horizon, the narrow bunk of the ferry cabin still holding the cool press of night. He lay with his eyes open, the memory of Sarah’s fingers brushing his knuckles as she passed him a loaf yesterday returning in thin waves. It had been only a second, the tip of her thumb grazing the ridge of his hand, but it lingered like the scent of warm dough in a closed oven, rising each time he breathed. He could feel the echo of that contact in the pads of his own fingers, a faint pressure that seemed to settle into the bone and refuse to leave.

He turned his face toward the small window, watching the first pale streaks of spring light spread across the water. The lake was still, a sheet of mercury broken only by the occasional ripple of a fish rising near the reeds. The air smelled of wet earth and the faint sweetness of budding alder, a scent that always made him think of the bakery on Lantern Street, of the oven’s mouth yawning open and the flour dust that settled on the counter like a soft snow. He pressed his palm flat against the cool wood of the bunk rail, trying to ground himself in the solid, unyielding texture, but the thought of her touch kept slipping back, light as a moth’s wing against his skin.

When the sky had grown enough to show the silhouette of the island that lay mid‑lake, Jack slipped from his bunk and pulled on his boots. He decided to take the long way round the island on the morning run, a route that added extra minutes to the crossing but gave him time to let the night’s replay settle into something quieter. He liked the feeling of the tiller in his hands, the way the boat responded to the slightest shift, the way the water seemed to hold its breath when he turned the Heron toward the far shore. The longer path meant he would see the sunrise from a different angle, the light catching the cliffs on the far side in a thin gold line that made the water look like polished stone.

He guided the ferry out of the harbor, the engine’s low thrum a steady counterpoint to the quiet of his thoughts. As the Heron moved away from the dock, he ran the sentence over in his mind, shaping it like a baker shapes dough, trying to find the right weight and tension. He wanted to say something simple, something that would not sound like a request or a demand, just a statement of fact that sat between them without breaking the quiet. “I thought about the bread you gave me yesterday,” he formed in his head, the words soft and unadorned. He turned them over, feeling the syllable’s texture, the way they would sit on his tongue if he ever let them leave his mouth.

The boat rounded the island’s northern tip, the shoreline pulling away in a curve of pine and stone. Jack kept his eyes on the water, watching the wake fade behind them, and let the sentence sit in his chest, unspoken. He could feel the sun climbing higher, its warmth seeping through his coat and onto the back of his neck. When the Heron finally turned toward the Lantern Street landing, the dock came into view and there she stood, Sarah Quill, her apron tied loosely around her waist, a smile already forming as she watched the boat approach.

He felt the sentence dissolve the moment his eyes met hers. The words he had rehearsed slipped away like flour sifted through loose fingers, leaving only the quiet hum of the engine and the soft lap of water against the hull. He could see the faint lines of concentration around her eyes, the way she tucked a stray strand of hair behind her ear, the small, unconscious lift of her shoulders as she waited for the ferry to nudge the dock. The memory of her fingers brushed his knuckles flashed again, brighter this time, as if the morning light had caught it and held it for him to see.

He stepped onto the dock, the wooden planks familiar under his boots, and moved toward the shop door. The bell above the entrance gave its soft chime as he pushed it open, the warm scent of yeast and baked crust spilling out onto the street. Sarah was behind the counter, her hands dusted with flour, a loaf resting on the peel ready for the oven. She glanced up, her gaze meeting his for a heartbeat before she turned back to her work.

Jack stood just inside the doorway, the cool air of the shop brushing his cheeks. He could feel the memory of her touch still humming in his skin, a low vibration that made the muscles in his forearm tighten without his consent. He wanted to say something, anything, to break the quiet that had settled between them like a thin film of oil on water, but the words he had prepared were gone, replaced by the simple awareness of her presence, the way she moved, the way the light caught the silver in her hair.

He lifted his hand, not to touch her, but to rest it lightly on the edge of the counter, the wood smooth under his palm. The gesture was small, a way to anchor himself, to keep from reaching out and brushing the flour from her cheek. He felt the urge to speak rise, then fall, like a tide that never quite reaches the shore. He swallowed, the motion dry, and let the silence stretch, aware that the space between them was not empty but filled with everything they had not said.

Later, after the morning rush had passed and the shop had settled into a quieter rhythm, Jack found himself back on the ferry’s empty deck. The dawn had deepened, the sky a clear blue that stretched over the lake like a wide‑eyed gaze. He moved to the starboard side, the planks worn smooth by years of feet and salt, and began to scrub the deck with a stiff brush. The bristles scraped against the wood, sending up a faint spray of water and the occasional fleck of old paint. Each stroke was deliberate, each push of his arms a way to wear down the thought that kept circling in his mind like a stubborn knot in dough.

He thought of the brief handover yesterday, the moment Sarah had placed the warm loaf in his palm and his fingers had brushed hers for that instant. The contact had been light, barely more than a whisper, yet it had left a mark that felt deeper than skin. He could still sense the heat of the bread through the thin cloth of her glove, the way her thumb had grazed his knuckle as she let go. The memory was not a picture but a sensation, a faint pressure that lingered in the pads of his fingers and made his pulse throb just a little slower than usual.

He scrubbed harder, the brush digging into the grain, his shoulders beginning to ache from the effort. The ache was a welcome distraction, a physical counterpoint to the mental loop that refused to unwind. He focused on the rhythm of his arms, the way the brush moved back and forth, the sound of bristles against wood, the smell of damp pine and lake water rising from the deck. Each stroke was a small act of containment, a way to keep the feeling from spilling over into action.

He knew there was no one to stop him, no rule that barred him from speaking, no external force that held him back. The dock was empty, the ferry silent aside from his own movements, the lake calm and waiting. Yet he stayed still, his hands moving, his eyes fixed on the horizon, because something inside him chose to hold back, to let the moment sit unspoken rather than risk breaking the quiet that had become a kind of shelter between them.

When his arms finally trembled with fatigue, he set the brush down and leaned against the rail, the wood cool against his forearms. He looked out over the water, the sun now high enough to send a bright glitter across the lake’s surface. In his peripheral vision, he noticed a small heel of crust resting on the bench beside him, a piece of Sarah’s bread that had been left there after yesterday’s exchange. It was golden brown, the edges slightly crisp, the interior soft and steaming still in his memory.

He picked it up, intending to finish it, to let the flavor settle his thoughts. The first bite was warm, the crust giving way under his teeth with a soft crackle, the crumb moist and fragrant with honey and a hint of rosemary. He chewed slowly, feeling the bread’s texture, the way it seemed to carry something beyond taste—a quiet yearning that made his throat tighten, a sensation that tugged at his thoughts like a current pulling a boat off course. He tried to take another bite, but the loaf felt heavier in his hand, the act of chewing suddenly too deliberate, as if each motion required a conscious effort he could not muster.

He set the heel back on the bench, the crumbs falling softly onto the wood. He could not finish it, not because he was full, but because the bread held a feeling he was not ready to swallow. It was the same feeling that had lived in the brush of her fingers, the same quiet ache that had settled in his chest since the night before. He left the piece there, a small offering to the morning light, a silent acknowledgment that some things were meant to linger rather than be consumed.

He thought back to the chapter before, to the way it had ended with the sensation of one second of skin, a fleeting contact that had seemed to stretch into something larger than its brevity. That feeling had not faded; it had settled into the marrow of his days, showing up in the weight of a loaf, the scratch of a brush on deck, the way the light fell on the water at dawn. It was a reminder that even the briefest touch could leave a imprint that lingered long after the moment had passed, shaping the quiet spaces between words and actions.

Jack stood, the deck still damp from his scrubbing, and moved toward the helm. He placed his hands on the tiller, feeling the familiar grain under his palms, the steady resistance of the boat as it waited for his direction. The lake lay before him, calm and inviting, the sun casting a long silver path that stretched toward the far shore. He took a breath, the air filling his lungs with the scent of water and spring, and gave the tiller a small turn, guiding the Heron back toward the harbor.

The ferry slipped forward, the engine’s hum low and steady, the wake trailing behind in a thin white line. He kept his eyes on the water, the light dancing on the ripples, and let the quiet of the morning settle around him, the memory of her fingers still a soft pressure in his skin, the heel of her crust a silent reminder on the bench, the unspoken sentence a shape he carried without needing to speak it. The day unfolded, the light shifting, the water moving, and Jack remained at the helm, his hands steady, his heart holding the quiet ache that had become his constant companion.
