Jack saw her before she reached the landing.

She was coming down Lantern Street with a loaf wrapped in pale cloth and held against her coat, walking as if she had set herself a pace and meant to keep it. The morning had turned mild enough for the mist to lift off Larkmere Water, though the shade under the harbor roofs still held a bite of cold.

He checked the forward line, then the stern line. Both sound. The passengers for the crossing had begun to gather in their usual loose cluster, bags at their feet, talk passing between them in short bursts, and Jack could have told you who would board first by the way each of them stood. The old man with the canvas satchel always waited until everyone else had moved. The woman in the green scarf liked to be near the rail. The twins from the north lane argued about who would sit by the window, though the Heron had no windows on her deck, and had never had any.

Sarah came closer.

He had seen her at the shop door, and at the dock with a basket, and through the wheelhouse glass as the Heron passed the town. He had not seen her come aboard as a passenger. The difference should not have mattered. People crossed the lake for work and errands and visits and reasons they kept to themselves, and he took their fares and watched their footing and brought them to the other side.

He found that he was looking at the coins in her hand before she reached him.

“Morning,” she said.

“Morning.”

She held out the fare. Her fingers were pink with cold, and the loaf rode against her ribs under her other arm. Jack took the coins and dropped them into the tin by the wheelhouse door. It was an ordinary exchange. He had made it thousands of times without remembering one.

“First crossing?” he asked.

“As a passenger.” The corner of her mouth lifted. “I’ve carried enough bread down here to know where the gangway is.”

He almost smiled. The feeling got as far as his face and stopped there. “Mind the second plank. It shifts.”

“I’ll watch it.”

She came aboard carefully, testing the plank before she put her weight on it. At the top she paused to let a family with two baskets go by, and Jack watched her step aside with the loaf tucked close. A woman bumped her shoulder with a basket and Sarah thanked her. There was no sharpness in it and no complaint. She made room the way a person does who learned to do it long before anyone asked her.

The thought stayed with him longer than it should have.

“Where would you like to sit?”

She looked along the deck, past the benches near the wheelhouse to the narrow seat by the rail, and chose the rail, forward, where the wake spread widest. She sat with the loaf in her lap and rested both hands on the cloth.

Jack went back to the tiller.

The others settled around her. The old man took his usual place near the stern. The woman in the green scarf stood at the rail with a paper parcel under her arm. The twins had reached an uneasy treaty over the seat nearest the wheelhouse, and their mother kept a hand on each of their shoulders, like a woman holding two kites.

Nell cast off the lines with the quick certainty of a deckhand who had learned the boards by falling on them and getting up again. Jack gave the signal, eased the throttle forward, and felt the Heron draw away from the landing.

The boat turned into the channel. The dock slid behind them and the harbor opened out, and Lantern Street climbed its slope in a row of pale stone fronts, the bakery window catching the light. Jack kept his eyes ahead. He could feel the loaf’s presence without looking at it. He had felt it before, on the bench beside the wheelhouse, in the quiet after the first bite, in the crumb he had carried in his pocket until it dried and broke apart under his thumb.

He had told himself the taste would fade. It had faded, mostly. What remained was harder to put aside.

He began to count the engine’s strokes under his breath, as he did when he wanted his mind to stay where it was put. One to forty. Forty to eighty. A gust came off the open water and tugged at the edge of Sarah’s cloth, and she laid her palm over the loaf. A strand of hair slipped from under her cap and lifted toward the wheelhouse, caught the light, drifted across her cheek. She tucked it back without looking up.

He lost the count.

The Heron held her line. Beyond the point the water was calm, with a long low swell coming in from the far shore, and he could have taken the straight crossing. The channel was clear and the wind mild and the shoal well marked. He had no reason to alter the route.

He steered around the edge of the shoal.

The turn was gentle enough that the passengers barely noticed it. The old man went on watching the far landing. The twins began a new quarrel over their mother’s parcel of biscuits. Nell looked at the compass, and then at Jack, and said nothing at all, which in Nell was a form of speech.

Sarah looked up when the bow came around, and their eyes met across the deck.

He should have looked away first. He was at work. The tiller wanted attention, and the water beyond the shoal could change without warning. But her gaze stayed on him, steady and a little uncertain, as though she had not expected him to look back, and the loaf lay quiet in her lap with her fingers resting on the cloth, close together, the knuckles pale where she held it.

His chest tightened. He set his grip more firmly on the tiller and watched the line of water ahead.

The Heron slowed.

It was not the engine. The note stayed even and the hull moved cleanly and the current was no stronger than it had been at the landing. Yet the water seemed to lean against the bow, and the ferry lost a little of her easy pace, as if the channel had lengthened under her.

Jack checked the marker at the shoal. The depth was safe. He eased the throttle and brought it back, and the Heron answered with a slow slide.

“Current?” Nell asked, from beside the forward line.

“Maybe.”

She looked over the side. “It’s running out toward the point.”

“I can see that.”

“Just checking.”

He did not turn. He could feel the question she had chosen not to ask, and he had no intention of helping her shape it into words.

A hush had come over the deck. The twins had stopped arguing. Their mother sat with her hands folded over the basket handle. The woman in the green scarf stared at the water with a softness in her face that did not belong to the view, and even the old man had lowered his satchel to the boards and rested a hand on it, his eyes gone far away.

Sarah’s loaf had made them quiet.

Jack had seen the bread work before. Fenna’s loaves had stilled a room without making it feel empty, and drawn a person inward, toward a memory he had not meant to touch that morning. Sarah’s bread did the same, though its feeling was less practiced, more open at the edges, like a door left on the latch. This loaf carried warmth and a pull toward the shore behind them, and he could feel the passengers turning toward it without moving their heads.

He could feel himself doing the same.

The ferry drifted a little farther from the straight route. He corrected and let her carry them around the shoal, where the water ran dark and smooth. It was a longer way, but a safe one. He could explain the turn if anyone asked. The channel was wide enough, the wind was mild, and no passenger had a right to the shortest possible crossing.

He looked at Sarah again.

She had turned toward the shore. The bakery was a small pale mark among the roofs by now, and the harbor lanterns stood in their row along the wall. Her face was turned away from him, but he saw the line of her cheek and the way her fingers curled around the edge of the cloth. She held the loaf as though it might answer a question if she kept her hands still enough.

He wanted to ask what she had put into it. He wanted to ask whether she knew the bread reached people in ways she could not choose. He wanted to ask whether the pull toward the shore belonged to her, or to the part of her that had not yet decided to stay in Larkmere.

None of those questions belonged to a ferryman at his tiller. He kept them behind his teeth.

The Heron passed the last marker and came out into the wider water, with the far landing still a pale strip beyond the green of the shore. He eased the throttle forward. The engine deepened and the hull lifted, and the ferry found her pace for a few lengths before slowing again, like a horse that had seen a snake in the road.

He breathed out through his nose.

The lake was not difficult today. No sudden wind, no hidden snag, no warning in the sky. The boat was sound and the lines were sound and he knew the channel. He had no patience with a crossing that behaved as though it had forgotten its own length.

Sarah turned her face toward the wheelhouse. A loose strand of hair rose again in the breeze and drifted his way and did not reach him, fell back against her cheek, lifted once more as the wind changed.

He kept both hands on the tiller.

The space between them was a few paces. He could cross it in less time than it took to turn the wheel. He could ask if she was warm enough, or whether the loaf was from the morning bake, or make a remark about the weather, which was what he said to passengers when he needed a sentence and had no other one ready.

He said nothing.

The Heron moved slowly across the lake. The passengers had gone quiet enough that he could hear the creak of a basket handle and the little click of the twins’ boots on the boards. Sarah’s hands stayed on the loaf. Her face had softened, but her gaze stayed fixed on the shore ahead, and he could not tell whether the bread had brought her comfort or reminded her of what she had left behind.

He knew better than to decide for her.

It came to him, with no warning, that he was wishing the lake were wider. He looked at the thought as he might have looked at a line gone slack in his hand, and found it perfectly clear, and perfectly foolish. Twenty minutes more. An hour. He would have crossed the whole width of it twice over if she had stayed where she was, hair lifting, with that loaf in her lap and the whole deck holding its breath.

The far landing drew nearer. A pair of gulls wheeled over the roof of the ferry shed, and the water changed from green to a darker blue where the shadow of the shore reached it. He brought the Heron toward the dock with the usual turn, though he began it later than he needed to, and the hull glided in, slow and steady.

Nell caught the line and made it fast. The gangplank came down with a thump. The old man lifted his satchel, the twins leapt up before their mother could stop them, and the woman in the green scarf tucked her parcel away. The spell of the loaf loosened. Voices came back, first one and then another, and then the ordinary bustle of people gathering their things.

Sarah rose from the rail. She wrapped the loaf in its cloth and held it against her side, and at the gangplank she turned toward him.

“Thank you,” she said. “That was a lovely crossing.”

He had heard those words from passengers who meant only that they had arrived safely. Sarah said them as if the length of the crossing had mattered. He felt the answer rise in him, plain and dangerous.

“Glad of it,” he said.

She smiled. Not at the planks, this time. At him.

Then she stepped onto the landing and walked toward Lantern Street, and Jack watched her until a cart of empty barrels rolled between them and took her out of sight. He could have gone back to the wheelhouse. He should have checked the line, counted the fares, readied the next run.

He stayed where he was, one hand resting on the tiller, listening to the water tap against the hull.

Nell came to stand beside him. She followed the line of his gaze up toward the street and then looked at him.

“Wind shifted,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Funny how it does that.”

Jack looked down at the deck. “Check the stern line.”

“I already checked it.”

“Check it again.”

She gave him a long, level look and went aft without another word, her footsteps fading into the working sounds of the boat.

Jack turned the tiller to center it. The Heron rocked once against the dock and settled. The crossing was over, Sarah had gone ashore with the loaf, and the lake had returned to its ordinary width. He looked out across it. The far shore seemed farther off than it had that morning.

He waited until Nell had finished at the stern before he went to check it himself. There was no fault in the knot and no slack in the rope and no reason to put his hands on it twice. He pulled anyway, and ran the line through his fingers until the rough fibers caught on the callus below his thumb. Work was generally enough to return the world to its proper size. Today it only gave him more to do with his hands.

A new group had gathered at the landing. He lifted his head and called them aboard, took their fares without looking toward Lantern Street, and waited until the last of them was settled before he cast off.

The Heron moved into the channel. This time he kept the shortest route, the marker straight ahead and the tiller firm in his hands. He read the water where the current crossed the channel and made the small corrections the boat required. Nothing held the bow back. The engine kept its steady note, the wake opened clean behind her, and the far landing came on at its usual pace.

It should have pleased him.

Instead, when the shore opened up beside him, he looked toward the place where Sarah had sat. The rail was empty. A scrap of pale thread had caught on a splinter near the bench, from her cloth, perhaps, or a passenger’s sleeve. He left it where it was.
