Jack saw her before she reached the landing.

Sarah Quill was coming down Lantern Street with a loaf wrapped in pale cloth, held against her coat. She walked as if she had set herself a pace and meant to keep it. The morning had turned mild enough for the mist to lift off Larkmere Water, though the shade beneath the harbor roofs still held a bite of cold.

He checked the forward line, then the stern line. Both were sound. The passengers waiting for the crossing had begun to gather in their usual loose cluster, bags at their feet, talk passing between them in short bursts. Jack could have told you which of them would board first by the way they stood. The old man with the canvas satchel always waited until everyone else had moved. The woman in the green scarf liked to be near the rail. The twins from the north lane argued about who had to sit beside the window, though there was no window on the deck.

Sarah came closer.

He had seen her at the shop door, at the dock with a basket, through the wheelhouse glass when the Heron passed the town. He had not seen her come aboard as a passenger. That difference should not have mattered. People crossed the lake for work, errands, visits, and reasons they kept to themselves. He took their fares, watched their footing, and brought them to the other side.

He found himself looking at the coins in her hand before she reached him.

“Morning,” she said.

“Morning.”

She held out the fare. Her fingers were pink from the cool air, and the loaf rested against her ribs beneath her other arm. Jack took the coins and put them in the small tin by the wheelhouse door. The exchange was ordinary. He had performed it thousands of times without remembering a single one.

“First crossing?” he asked.

“As a passenger.” Her mouth lifted slightly. “I've delivered enough bread to know where the gangway is.”

He almost smiled. The feeling reached his face and stopped there. “Mind the second plank. It shifts.”

“I'll watch it.”

She stepped aboard carefully, testing the plank before putting her weight on it. When her shoes reached the deck, she paused to let a family with two baskets pass. Jack watched her move aside, keeping the loaf tucked close. She thanked the mother when the woman bumped her shoulder with a basket. There was no complaint, no sharpness. She made room as if she had learned to do it before anyone asked.

The thought stayed with him longer than it should have.

“Where would you like to sit?” he asked.

Sarah looked along the deck. There were benches near the wheelhouse and a narrow seat by the rail. She chose the rail, close to the bow, where the wake spread widest. She sat with the loaf in her lap and rested both hands on the cloth.

Jack returned to the tiller.

The other passengers settled around her. The old man with the satchel took his place near the stern. The woman in the green scarf stood by the rail, holding a paper parcel beneath her arm. The twins had reached an uneasy agreement about the seat nearest the wheelhouse. Their mother kept one hand on each of their shoulders.

Nell cast off the lines. She worked with the quick certainty of a deckhand who had learned the boards by falling on them and getting up again. Jack gave the signal, eased the throttle, and felt the Heron draw away from the landing.

The boat turned into the channel. The dock slid behind them, and the harbor opened across the water. Lantern Street climbed the slope in a row of pale stone fronts, the bakery window catching the light. Jack kept his eyes ahead. He could feel the loaf's presence without looking at it. He had felt it before, on the bench beside the wheelhouse, in the quiet after the first bite, in the crumb he had carried in his pocket until it dried and broke apart beneath his thumb.

He had told himself the taste would fade. It had faded, mostly. What remained was harder to set aside.

A gust came off the open water and tugged at the edge of Sarah's cloth. She pressed her palm over the loaf. A strand of hair slipped free from beneath her cap and lifted toward the wheelhouse. It caught the light, then drifted across her cheek. She tucked it back without looking up.

Jack adjusted the tiller by a fraction.

The Heron held her line. The water was calm beyond the point, with a long low swell moving in from the far shore. He could have taken the straight crossing. The channel was clear, the wind mild, and the shoal well marked. He had no reason to alter the route.

He steered around the edge of the shoal.

The turn was gentle enough that the passengers barely noticed. The old man continued to watch the far landing. The twins began another argument over the parcel of biscuits their mother had brought. Nell glanced at the compass, then at Jack. She said nothing.

Sarah looked up when the bow changed direction. Their eyes met across the deck.

He should have looked away first. He was at work. The tiller needed attention, and the water beyond the shoal could change without warning. But her gaze stayed on him, steady and a little uncertain, as though she had not expected him to look back. The loaf lay quiet in her lap. Her fingers rested against the cloth, close together, the knuckles pale where she held it.

Jack's chest tightened. He set his grip more firmly on the tiller and watched the line of water ahead.

The Heron slowed.

It was not a failure of the engine. The note stayed even, the hull moved cleanly, and the current was no stronger than it had been at the landing. Yet the water seemed to press against the bow. The ferry lost a little of her easy pace, as if the channel had lengthened beneath her.

Jack checked the marker at the shoal. The depth was safe. He eased the throttle, then brought it back. The Heron answered with a slow slide.

“Current?” Nell asked from beside the forward line.

“Maybe.”

She looked over the side. “It's running out toward the point.”

“I can see that.”

“Just checking.”

He did not turn to her. He could feel the question she had chosen not to ask, and he had no intention of helping her shape it into words.

A hush had settled across the deck. The twins had stopped arguing. Their mother sat with her hands folded over the basket handle. The woman in the green scarf stared at the water with a softness in her face that did not belong to the view. Even the old man had lowered his satchel to the boards and rested one hand on it, his eyes gone distant.

Sarah's loaf had made them quiet.

Jack had seen the bread work before. Fenna's loaves had quieted a room without making it feel empty. They could draw a person inward, toward a memory they had not meant to touch that morning. Sarah's bread did the same, though its feelings were less practiced, more open at the edges. This loaf carried warmth and a pull toward the shore behind them. He could feel the passengers turning toward it without moving their heads.

He could feel himself doing the same.

The ferry drifted a little farther from the straight route. Jack corrected the tiller and let the current carry them around the shoal, where the water ran dark and smooth. It was a longer way, but a safe one. He could explain the turn if anyone asked. The channel was wide enough, the wind was mild, and no passenger had a right to the shortest possible crossing.

He looked at Sarah again.

She had turned toward the shore. The bakery had become a small pale mark among the roofs, and the harbor lanterns stood in their row along the wall. Her face was turned away from him, but he saw the line of her cheek and the way her fingers curled around the edge of the cloth. She held the loaf as though it might answer a question if she kept her hands still.

He wanted to ask what she had put into it. He wanted to ask whether she knew the bread reached people in ways she could not choose. He wanted to ask if the pull toward the shore belonged to her, or to the part of her that had not yet decided whether she would remain in Larkmere.

None of those questions belonged to a ferryman at the tiller. He kept them behind his teeth.

The Heron passed the last marker and entered the wider water. The far landing was still a pale strip beyond the green of the shore. Jack eased the throttle forward. The engine deepened, the hull lifted, and the ferry found her usual pace for a few lengths before slowing again.

He exhaled through his nose.

The lake was not difficult today. No sudden wind, no hidden current, no warning in the sky. The boat was sound. The lines were sound. He knew the channel. He had no use for a crossing that behaved as if it had forgotten its own distance.

Sarah looked toward the wheelhouse. A loose strand of hair rose again in the breeze and drifted in his direction. It did not reach him. It fell back against her cheek, then lifted once more as the wind changed.

He kept both hands on the tiller.

The space between them was only a few paces. He could cross it in less time than it took to turn the wheel. He could ask if she was warm enough, or whether the loaf had come from the morning bake. He could make a remark about the weather, which was the sort of thing he said to passengers when he needed a sentence and had no other one ready.

He said nothing.

The Heron moved slowly across the lake. The passengers had gone quiet enough that he could hear the soft creak of a basket handle and the little click of the twins' boots against the boards. Sarah's hands stayed on the loaf. Her face had softened, but her gaze remained fixed on the shore ahead. He could not tell whether the bread had brought her comfort or reminded her of what she had left behind.

He knew better than to decide for her.

The far landing drew nearer. A pair of gulls wheeled above the roof of the ferry shed. The water changed from green to a darker blue where the shadow of the shore reached it. Jack brought the Heron toward the dock with the usual turn, though he began it later than he needed to. The hull glided in, slow and steady.

Nell caught the line and secured it. The gangplank came down with a thump. The old man lifted his satchel, the twins jumped up before their mother could stop them, and the woman in the green scarf tucked her parcel under her arm. The spell of the loaf loosened. Voices returned, first one, then another, then the ordinary bustle of people gathering their things.

Sarah rose from the rail. She wrapped the loaf in its cloth and held it against her side. When she reached the gangplank, she turned toward Jack.

“Thank you,” she said. “That was a lovely crossing.”

He had heard the words from passengers who meant only that they had arrived safely. Sarah spoke as if the length of the crossing had mattered. He felt the answer rise in him, plain and dangerous.

“Glad of it,” he said.

She smiled. Not at the planks this time. At him.

Then she stepped onto the landing and walked toward Lantern Street. Jack watched her until a cart loaded with empty barrels passed between them and blocked her from view. He could have returned to the wheelhouse. He should have checked the line, counted the fares, prepared for the next run.

Instead he stayed where he was, one hand resting on the tiller, listening to the water tap against the hull.

Nell came to stand beside him. She followed the line of his gaze toward the street, then looked at him.

“Wind shifted,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Funny how it does that.”

Jack looked down at the deck. “Check the stern line.”

“I already checked it.”

“Check it again.”

She gave him a long, level look, then went to the stern without another word. Her footsteps faded into the working sounds of the boat.

Jack turned the tiller a little to center it. The Heron rocked once against the dock and settled. The crossing was over. Sarah had gone ashore, the loaf with her, and the lake had returned to its ordinary width.

He looked out across the water. The far shore seemed farther away than it had that morning.

He waited until Nell had finished with the stern line before he went to check it himself. There was no fault in the knot, no slack in the rope, no reason to put his hands on it twice. He pulled anyway, then ran the line through his fingers until the rough fibers caught at the small callus below his thumb. Work was usually enough to return the world to its proper size. Today it only gave him more things to do with his hands.

A new group had gathered at the landing. Jack lifted his head and called them aboard. The first passenger stepped onto the plank, then the next. He took their fares without looking toward Lantern Street. He waited until the last person had settled before he cast off.

The Heron moved into the channel. This time he kept to the shortest route, the marker straight ahead, the tiller firm in his hands. He watched the water where the current crossed the channel and made the small adjustments the boat required. Nothing held the bow back. The engine kept its steady note, the wake opened cleanly, and the far landing approached at its usual pace.

It should have pleased him.

Instead, when the shore opened beside him, he looked toward the place where Sarah had sat. The rail was empty. A scrap of pale thread had caught on a splinter near the bench, perhaps from her cloth, perhaps from a passenger's sleeve. He left it where it was.
