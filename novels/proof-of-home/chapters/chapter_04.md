Morning fog clung low over Larkmere Water, muffling oar locks and reducing the shoreline to a suggestion rather than a promise. Jack had the Heron moving before the lanterns were doused, the engine's rhythm settling into his ribs like a held breath. He knew the channel by heart — every submerged rock and shifting shoal mapped in the muscles of his arms — but today the water felt different. Thicker. Reluctant.

He spotted her before she reached the gangplank. Sarah Quill moved along Lantern Street with the easy gait of someone who has baked before dawn for longer than she would admit, a cloth-wrapped bundle cradled against her hip. The basket on her other arm held nothing he could name, though the scent of rosemary and something sweet drifted ahead of her on the damp air. A coat the color of wet slate covered her, her hair loose beneath a wool cap that had seen generations of Mercer heads.

She climbed aboard without hesitation, paid her fare with coins warmed by her fingers, and claimed the starboard rail near the bow. The spot where the wake fans widest. Where the wind finds you first.

Jack watched her settle. Cloth unwrapped to reveal a round loaf scored deep with a pattern he did not recognize, a spiral that started at the center and worked outward like a fingerprint. She set it on her lap with both hands, palms flat against the crust as if taking a pulse.

Three women with market baskets and a man reading a newspaper folded to the classifieds turned their heads toward the loaf. The man lowered his paper. The women fell silent. Fog swallowed their voices before words could form.

Jack had seen this before. Fenna's bread had done the same. A silence that was not absence but presence, a hush that gathered rather than fell. He gripped the tiller and counted propeller strokes. One. Two. Three. The Heron's hull shuddered, a slow reluctance in her wake.

The crossing should have taken twenty minutes. Channel length and current force were known quantities. Jack had made this run in wind that stripped paint and rain that blurred the shore to charcoal. He knew the timing in his bones.

Today the water resisted. The engine held its steady note but the shoreline slid past slower, buoys drifting by like weary swimmers. He adjusted the throttle. No change. The Heron moved at her own pace, sulking beneath his hands.

Sarah did not look up. Her fingers rested on the loaf's scored spiral, tips moving in tiny presses — a baker's habit of reading what she had written in dough. Wind lifted strands of hair from beneath her cap, dark threads catching the pale light, drifting toward the wheelhouse, toward him, each gust a suggestion.

He kept both hands on the tiller. Counted strokes. Four. Five. Six.

The man with the newspaper folded it completely. The women sat with hands folded in their laps. On Sarah's knees, the loaf rested like a sleeping animal while fog thickened until bow and stern vanished, leaving only the Heron moving through white suspension.

Jack's thumb traced the worn groove in the tiller where generations of Mercer thumbs had rested. His father. His grandfather. The wood knew the shape of him. The water knew the shape of the Heron. Neither knew what to do with a passenger who carried silence in a cloth wrap.

He glanced at her. Once. Twice. The third time, she was already looking back.

Her eyes were the color of the lake at depth, the green that lives where light stops reaching. They held. One second. Two. The space between heartbeats stretched. Her hair lifted again, a longer strand escaping the cap to brush her cheek, and the wind carried it toward the wheelhouse like an offering.

He did not move. Hands stayed locked on the tiller. Boots stayed planted on the deck plates. The Heron's engine kept its slow chant. The crossing lengthened.

Shoreline resolved ahead, the pier emerging from fog like a bone from silt. Nell would be waiting at the lines, her sharp eyes reading everything he would not say. The thought steadied him. The count returned. Seven. Eight. Nine.

The Heron kissed the dock. Lines sang. The gangplank thumped down.

Sarah rose in one fluid motion, the loaf disappearing into its cloth, the basket settling on her arm. She did not look at him again. She walked the plank with the same easy gait, boots quiet on the wood, and fog swallowed her before she reached the shore.

Other passengers stirred. The man unfolded his newspaper. The women resumed their conversation in lower voices. Silence broke like a held breath released.

Jack exhaled. His hands ached from the grip. The Heron settled into her slip, water lapping against hull planks, and he watched the place where she had stood at the rail. Wood held no warmth. Wind had taken that.

He counted the strokes of the return crossing in his head before the engine even turned. The lake felt wider now. He wished it were wider still.
