The clerk had rubbed the chalk down to a single dot that morning, no bigger than a caraway seed, and Sarah had walked past it with her basket and her eyes front and felt it on the back of her neck all the way up Lantern Street.

Tomorrow was the last run of the season.

She had promised to tell Jack to his face when she knew. Every night since the pier she had lit the lantern on its iron hook and lain in Fenna’s narrow bed and tried to know, and every night she had come up with nothing, only the sound of her own heart and the frost ticking on the roof slates.

At closing she sat down at the long table with the week’s orders to total. She ran her pencil down the column, and the pencil stopped.

It was in her own hand. Twelve sacks of rye and wheat from the north shore mill, to be delivered every two weeks through the first week of February. A cord of beech for the oven, paid in full to the chandler. A longer peel, because Fenna’s was cracked at the neck. A second crock for the starter, which had outgrown the first and crept up its cloth every morning like a cat that wants the sill. She remembered signing the miller’s slip and thinking about nothing.

She had never decided. She turned the pages back and saw it laid out in her own handwriting, week on week, dull as bookkeeping. Dough does not decide to rise. There is no minute you can point to. You look away to fetch a cloth, and when you turn back the bowl is full and the cloth is lifting, and the only thing left to do is to take it out and shape it.

She sat with both palms flat on the open pages. Behind her in the dark kitchen the starter heaved against its cloth, one long wet sigh, and she began to laugh. It came out thin and cracked, and she put the back of her wrist to her mouth to stop it, and it did not stop.

Then she got up and went to the flour bin.

She made one loaf. She would not let herself make two, because two would have been a way to hedge, and she had hedged enough for a lifetime. The dough came together in the big wooden bowl cold and slack and shaggy, as it always did, and she turned it out and began the fold and push and quarter turn. For the first minutes she did not think. Then she did, and she let all of it in.

She put in the first morning, the cloth held out in both hands and his palms coming up beneath it, not one finger touching one of hers, so exact that she had known it was exact. She put in the lopsided loaf and the flour on her cheek and his thumb. She put in a lifeboat of a loaf that had weighed as much as a paving stone, and the stone-hard bun he had laid on her counter, which she had not once been able to throw out. The eight gray loaves on the cellar shelf went in, and the stair with its single inch of air. She put in a bench built for one, and a hand’s width between two bodies held all night like a breath. She did not hide the want. She folded it in with the heels of her hands until the dough was warm to the wrist, and it did not flinch. A season of it, and it had only ever been waiting to be used.

At the last she put in the winter. The twelve sacks. The cord of beech. The second crock. She kneaded in a woman signing a slip without reading it, and a man at a window with one palm on the glass.

The dough gave. That was the only word for it. It had fought her through all the spring, tearing at the knuckle, and tonight it drew together under her palms smooth as a held note and began to push back, steady and alive. She set it to rise in the blue cloth and stood over it with her arms wrapped round her ribs, and it lifted the linen as she watched, a slow round breath.

While it proved she went to the tin box beneath the table and took out a blank card. There was no reason to have one. Fenna’s cards were all marked and soft with years, and Sarah had never written one of her own. She found the stub of a pencil. A spiral for a slow rise. The little circle with a line bowed in the middle, the ferry, because every first loaf and every last one went across. Then she held the pencil above the card a long time, and drew a mark that was in nobody’s margin: a square with a roof, and the door left open.

She put the card in the tin beside Fenna’s, and closed the lid on both.

The loaf came out at the dead hour, and the crust sang when the air touched it, a long ringing crackle that went on and on, a hundred small doors, a thousand. It was the color of a lit window. She wrapped it in the blue cloth, twice round, with the twine knotted once. She did not wind it twice. She had stopped counting.

She took Fenna’s coat from the hook, the long waxed one with the horn buttons, and did them up to her chin.

Lantern Street was white. The frost had come down so hard that the cobbles shone like salted crust, and the lamps burned in their glass along the harbor wall. The Lamplight’s upstairs window was lit. A curtain moved. A teacup lifted, and tipped to her an inch, and the curtain fell, and she bit her lip all the way past the chandler’s.

The lamplighter’s ladder leaned against the first post with no one on it. The harbor clerk’s board stood under its shingle by the head of the pier, and she did not look at it.

She walked out along the planks with the loaf against her chest, the way you carry a thing that might wake. The boards rang under her boots. At the end, against the black sky, a man stood by the mooring post with his shoulders squared and his hands locked behind him. He did not turn. The Heron lay at her lines beside him, her paint pale with frost, and rocked very slightly, as if she were trying not to listen.

Sarah stopped a pace off.

“I am not leaving,” she said.

He turned his head. In the lamplight his face looked as if it had not slept properly since the pier. She watched the words travel across it, slowly, as a flame travels along a wick, and arrive.

“I want to say it first,” said Sarah, quickly, before he could draw breath. “Before you ask. Before you rehearse one more way of asking it. I am not leaving. I will be here in winter. I will be here in the winter after, if you will have a woman who gets up before the light and comes home smelling of rye.”

His hands came from behind his back and hung open at his sides. His mouth moved and nothing came.

“I did not decide,” she said. “I have lain awake every night since the pier trying, and I could not. I found it done. I sat down to total the week, and there are twelve sacks of flour ordered through February, in my own writing, and a cord of beech, and a new peel, and a second crock for a starter who has outgrown the first. I have been staying in small pieces since the spring, Jack. I only never looked up.”

He sat down.

He did it all at once, as if a string had been cut. There was a coil of rope on the planks beside the post, and his knees found it, and he folded onto it with his elbows on his knees and his face in both hands. His shoulders shook. She could not tell whether it was laughter or the other thing. She came the last pace and went down on the frozen boards in front of him, the loaf in her lap, and put her gloved hands over his wrists.

“Say it again,” said Jack, into his palms.

“I am not leaving.”

“Again.”

“I am staying. I am staying, you stubborn man, and you may stop counting hours.”

He lowered his hands. His eyes were wet and he did not trouble to hide it, and the sight of that went through her like heat through brick. “I have asked it in every order,” he said hoarsely. “At the cottage. On the lake. In my sleep. I was so afraid of the question that I could not have put it.”

“I know. I wanted you not to spend one more night believing the no.”

He reached into his coat and drew out a paper folded small and soft as cloth. “Fenna wrote it,” he said. “She left it with Gideon. I would like you to read it where I can see your face.”

She took her glove off with her teeth. She held the paper up to the lamp and read it through once, the tight upright hand, the plain words, the courtesy of them. She read to the end and her mouth shook. Then she read one line aloud, softly, and it was not the line he expected.

“A lantern is not a question. Do not hang one in a window and call it patience.”

She laughed, and her eyes stung. “I lit one every night since the pier. I called it waiting.”

“It was waiting.”

“It was a lantern. She is right. She is always right, and she is dead, and it is very irritating.” She folded the paper along its creases and put it back in his hand and shut his fingers over it. “Then ask me. Ask me properly, once, with your whole mouth. I will answer it, and you will already know what I will say, and it will not matter, and I want you to ask it anyway.”

“Tomorrow,” said Jack.

“Why tomorrow?”

He drew a breath, and she watched him find it. “The last run,” he said. “The one he took. I want to put it to you on the water, on that crossing, with the light on it, and have the Heron carry it the other way. Fenna waited years on the pier for a question nobody put.” His voice caught. “I would like the lake to hear one asked out loud.”

She understood it entirely. It settled in her slowly, the way a loaf settles on a board after the oven, and she sat back on her heels. “Then it is tomorrow,” she said.

“Then it is tomorrow.”

Neither of them moved.

The cold came up through her knees. The whole of her was shaking, and had been for a long while, and so were his hands under hers. She took off the other glove. She opened Fenna’s coat and sat down on the coil of rope beside him, and drew the heavy waxed canvas over both of them. It was a tight fit. It was meant for one. Their thighs lay together and their arms were pressed from shoulder to wrist, and she felt him draw one long unsteady breath.

“May I,” said Jack.

“Yes.”

He kissed her. It was slow and warm and shaking and it was not the dock of the last time, nor the stove, nor the cellar stair. It was a man who knew at last the answer and was learning how to carry it. She put her fingers in his collar. She laid her thumb on the pulse under his jaw, and it beat there like a bird against a cloth. His hand went flat to the small of her back through the coat, and the whole width of his palm was heat through wool. She turned toward him. Her breath came short, and he made the sound again, the one from the stove, low, torn out of him, and his mouth went to her cheekbone and her temple and the corner of her eye.

“Jack,” she said, and could not say what she meant by it.

He stopped.

He lifted his head, and his forehead came down against hers, and they stayed so with their breath smoking between them and the lamp above laying a gold circle on the boards. His hands had gone rigid. His fingers dug into the canvas at her back and she could feel the whole of him leaning against the edge of what he would not do. She held his face in both her palms and felt the stubble and the heat and the wet on his lashes.

“Tomorrow,” he said, in a voice she barely knew.

“Tomorrow,” said Sarah. Her own was a thread. “I know.”

“I want it to be asked in the light.”

“I know. So do I.”

She kissed him once more, short, because she could not not, and he made a sound against her mouth like a man set down in a warm room after a long cold, and then they both drew back at once. She sat with her fists balled in the front of his jacket. He sat with his hands clenched at the coat’s edge, white at the scar. They were breathing as though they had climbed the Net Sheds stair. Every inch of her felt the inch between them. It had a weight. It had a heat. It was the last inch, and it was going to be kept until morning, and she had never wanted anything so much, nor been so glad to wait.

She unwound her fingers from his jacket one by one, and laid her hands in her lap on the blue cloth.

“I brought you a loaf,” she said.

He looked down. He took the loaf in both palms, from beneath, with the linen between his skin and hers, and she knew at once that he had done it on purpose, and her heart turned over. He untied the string. He broke off the heel. The crust shattered clean, and the steam went up into the lamplight, and he put a piece on his tongue and shut his eyes.

She watched it reach him. She watched his shoulders drop, not slump, drop, as the weight he had carried through all those crossings came off and was set on the boards. His face worked. He chewed slowly. His throat moved. When he opened his eyes they were bright and black and fixed on her, and she knew what he tasted. Every season of it, and the winter ahead of it, and a door standing open, and under all of that, plain as flour, the word it had been trying to say all along. Yes. And then, in the same breath, the hunger that would not be fed tonight. Tomorrow.

“It is all in there,” said Jack, very low.

“I did not hide it.”

“I can taste that you did not.” He gave a short laugh and wiped his eyes with his wrist. “Sarah. It tastes like walking in at the end of a very long day.”

She did not answer. She could not have. She tucked her head against his shoulder under the coat, and he put the rest of the loaf in her lap and wrapped his arm round her, with his chin resting on her hair. Neither of them spoke again for a long while.

Beside them the Heron lay at her lines, her frosted flank against the pilings, and rocked softly, easy as a bowl of dough proving under a cloth, and did not once pull toward port.
