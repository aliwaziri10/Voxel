Jack did not sleep, and at first light he stopped pretending to.

He lay on the cottage bench under the gray blanket with his boots on and listened to the weather change. The frost had gone out of the night around the fourth hour. A soft wet wind came up off the lake and began to work at the shutters, and under it, steady as a pulse, he heard the first of the rain. He had the rest of her loaf on his chest, still in its blue cloth. He had eaten the heel in the dark and then lain with his palm on the crust until it cooled. It had not told him to wait. It had not asked him anything. It had said, all night long, in a voice as plain as a sounding line paying out, yes.

He got up and shaved with cold water and put on the clean shirt that he kept for funerals. He wrapped the loaf in its cloth and buttoned it inside his coat against his ribs, where Fenna’s letter had ridden, and walked down the water road with the rain on his face.

The Heron started on the first turn.

She did not cough or hang or sulk. The engine caught, found its note, and held it like a good bell, and when he took the wheel she came off the landing as clean as a knife leaving a sheath. He ran the morning crossing with a boatload of grocers and schoolchildren and a goat, and she tracked as if he had drawn the course on the water with a rule. An old man with a canvas satchel took his seat on the forward bench and said, to nobody, “She has been waiting on you to make up your mind, you know,” and Jack said, “So I hear.”

At the head of the pier the clerk had wiped the board. He had put up a square of clean slate in the corner where the chalk had been, and in it, in a clerk’s best capitals, one line: LAST RUN. DUSK.

“Everyone has bought a ticket,” said Nell at noon, to a mooring cleat. She was shining. She coiled the stern line with such vigor that it nearly got away from her. “Everyone. The whole town. To ride across to the far shore and back in the dark. Odalys has closed the inn.”

“Nell.”

“I have a cough,” said Nell, with enormous dignity. “I will be on the pier.” And she went off up the planks with the rope over her shoulder, whistling.

The afternoon crossings went by. At the middle of the lake he put his hand to the pocket where the letter lay and read it from memory, line by line. Ask. Do not wait to be asked. Say it badly, say it once, say it in the rain. Out here, with the gray water rising and the low shores lost in mist, he knew it was not a thing to be charted. A man could not plan his way across a question. He could only put the wheel over and go.

He thought of a pier in the rain, a long time ago. A woman in a gray wool coat going up the Heron’s plank with a suitcase in her hand and a brass button hanging by a thread from her cuff. A boy so small that the pier rail came to his eyes. His grandfather’s large hand on his shoulder, not holding, not pushing, only there. He had always taken it for a weight. Today, with the wet wheel under his palms and the dusk run still ahead of him, it came to him that it had been the other thing all along. It had not kept him from following her. It had kept him from going over the rail.

Nobody had asked her to stay. That was all it had ever been. A woman had wanted to go and no one had said the word, and the water had taken the years.

He put the Heron about at the far landing and brought her home with his jaw set, and he said it over once under his breath to the empty deck, badly, as Fenna had told him to, and it came out as a thing no man would have said to a woman on purpose. That was how he knew it was right.

The light went early. By the time the last chalk-marked run was called, the sky over the harbor had gone the color of a wet slate roof, and the rain came down fine and cold and slantwise. He waited at the head of the gangway with his cap in his hand. The whole of Larkmere came down the pier to him two by two under oilcloth and umbrellas and borrowed coats.

Odalys came first, in a hat that had not been out of its box for a decade, on the arm of the clerk. She paused at the bow, and looked at his face, and covered her mouth. “Oh,” she said. “Oh, you great fool. Go and sit down.” The grocer’s wife came, and her boy, and the woman in the green scarf, who had a roll in each pocket. Fishermen who had never in their lives ridden anyone’s ferry took their caps off at the plank as though it were a church step. Gideon Teague came last of the crowd, with his taper in his belt and no ladder, and he did not say a word. He laid two fingers to his cap, and went and sat in the stern.

She was the last aboard.

She came down the pier in Fenna’s long waxed coat with the horn buttons done to her chin and a covered basket on her arm, and she did not hurry. He watched her come the whole length of the planks. He had kept this rail through every season of his life and watched a great many people walk toward him and away. He had never watched anyone walk toward him as though she meant to arrive.

She stopped at the foot of the gangway and looked up at him. Neither of them spoke.

“Captain,” said Sarah.

“Passenger.”

“Is it a fare?”

“It is not,” said Jack, and held out his hand.

She took it. It was the first time she had put her bare hand in his in the sight of the whole harbor, with no cloth between them, and her fingers closed on his the way a line comes taut when it takes the weight. He did not let go. He drew her up the plank, and he did not let go at the top.

The Heron cast off on the first word. Nell flipped the stern line aboard with a flourish and stayed on the pier with her arms in the air, and the town on the deck went quiet and turned its face toward the far shore as if by agreement, and the engine came up smooth as a cat’s purr.

Sarah went down the deck with her basket. He saw it from the wheel and could not look away. She set it on the forward bench and lifted the cloth and took out a loaf, a great round one, bigger than a wheel, the color of a lit window. She broke it. She gave the first piece to Odalys, and Odalys put it in her mouth and shut her eyes. She gave it to the old man with the canvas satchel, to the grocer’s boy, to the woman in the green scarf, to Gideon, who held it on his palm as though it were a bird. She went from bench to bench. She said nothing. She did not need to.

He watched the town taste it. A man on the aft bench covered his eyes. The grocer’s wife wept without a sound. The boy said, “It tastes like coming in when the light has been left on,” and nobody told him to hush. Not one of them asked what it meant. It tasted of home, and every soul on that deck knew the way.

When she came to the wheelhouse she held out the last piece, and he took it, and his fingers touched hers. It tasted of walking in at the end of a very long day. It tasted of a bench built for one that could, if two people were very careful, hold two. It tasted of yes. It did not taste of waiting, or of fear. It tasted, he thought, as the lake must taste to a hull that has lain aground a long time and feels water come under the keel.

Half the lake behind them, the lights of the harbor had become a band of gold in the rain. He took the Heron to the middle, where the shores were low black lines on either side and the only light was the lamp at her mast. He cut the engine.

This time he lashed the wheel with a turn of line and trusted her with it. She lay still on the gray swell with her bow toward the town. He heard the whole deck stop breathing.

He walked aft along the planks to where Sarah waited by the rail with the empty basket on her arm. Rain ran off his cap. There was no sound on all that water but the long soft hiss of it on the lake.

He took off the cap. He had meant to say it in order. He had meant to start with the first morning and the cloth and the loaf, and go on from there. He opened his mouth and every word he had put in line went out of him, and he held his cap in both hands like a boy at a school door.

“Stay,” said Jack.

She did not move.

“I want you to stay,” he said, and it came out cracked and rough and not at all in the voice he had practiced. “That is all of it. I have run it through every way there is and it comes to that. I am not asking you to be smaller to do it. I am asking for you to take up all the room there is. The shop and the cottage and the winter and all of the lake, and me in it, if you will have me. I do not know how to ask this well. I am asking it badly. I am asking it once, in the rain, as I was told to.” His throat closed. He pushed through it. “Will you stay, Sarah?”

The rain fell between them. Behind him a hundred pairs of eyes were studying the water as if it held a map.

Sarah looked up at him, and the lamp at the mast laid a line of gold along the side of her face, and he saw the tears come, and he saw her smile through them, the whole, wide, unguarded smile that she had spent a season never letting out.

“Jack Mercer,” she said. “I have been staying since the first loaf.”

The Heron moved.

It was not the engine, which was cold. It was a long low pull below them, as of a hand under the keel, and then the great gray back of the lake lifted them, and held them, and let them go forward on its own. She did not drift. She did not wallow or swing to port or sulk. She gathered herself and ran, true as a plumb, with her bow dead on the town and the wake closing silver and clean behind her, and the deck broke into sound.

It was Odalys who started it, and nobody ever agreed who finished. A man shouted. A woman began to clap. The old man with the satchel rose and put his cap to his chest. Gideon Teague sat in the stern with his face turned to the lake and his shoulders shaking. Jack heard none of it. He had Sarah’s hand in both of his and her forehead against his, and neither of them could say a word.

The Heron brought them in on the dusk.

The lamps stood along the harbor wall in their long glass row, and they were not yet lit. At the head of the pier, with the rain bright on his coat, Gideon Teague waited with a taper that had got there ahead of him. Beside him on the planks was the last lantern, the one at the very end of the pier, the one whose light fell straight across the water to the middle of the lake. Jack did not remember getting off the boat. He remembered the plank under his boots and the weight of her hand in his, and one thing he would think of on every last run for all the years of his life: that a woman had come down the Heron’s plank, in the rain, and stayed.

They walked the length of the pier together. The town fell in behind them in the dark, and the boots went quiet.

At the end of the pier Gideon put up the taper.

Sarah stopped under the lantern. Jack stopped beside her. He had passed this lamp in the dusk of every year of his life, a man looking at a light that belonged to another man, and now it hung over the two of them and the rain ran through its glow. He turned and looked at her, and she was looking at him, in the plain light, with the whole town at their backs, and every wall he had built in all the seasons before her was a thing he could not remember the use of.

The wick caught.

The lantern bloomed gold above them, and the light fell on her face, and on the rain in her hair, and on a mouth already open to say his name, and he thought: she is thinking of me. And she was. He saw it. It was the oldest custom in Larkmere, and for once it had no need to be believed.

He kissed her.

There was nothing held back in it. He put his hands into her hair, and she took the front of his coat in both fists and dragged him down, and the whole of what they had denied each other since the first loaf broke out of them at once. It was the lake and the cellar stair and the stove and the frozen pier. It was every night he had lain with his palm open on his chest. She kissed him as if she meant to be let into every season she had spent outside it. He lifted her, and her boots left the planks, and the great waxed coat swung out around them, and he felt her laughing against his mouth and could not have said when the laughing turned. Behind them Odalys let out a shriek that must have carried across the lake. The town roared. He had her, whole and warm and loud against him, in the rain, in plain light, in front of every soul he had ever known. He was not afraid. There was no room in him for it.

The lamps came on.

One after another along the harbor wall they caught, the small soft sound of each flame arriving a breath after the one before, until the whole curve of the pier lay in gold. The rain turned to a net of light. On the black water below them each lamp laid a long bright road toward the middle of the lake, where nobody was going.

At her lines beside the pier the Heron lay in the lamplight with the rain running off her rail, and she did not move at all, not a point to port or a hair to starboard. She lay as level as a rule laid across a table, and as quiet, and as true.
