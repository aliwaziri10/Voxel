On the first morning, Jack kept the Heron on its ordinary route and stayed away from Lantern Street. The bakery was easy to find from the harbor wall, but he watched the channel instead. He checked each marker and made every turn at the usual point. There was no practical reason to pass the shop. He repeated that to himself until the sentence sounded like a rule he had always followed.

Late spring warmed the deck by midday. Jack kept his attention on the wheel, though his thoughts returned to Sarah’s face at the bakery door. He remembered the flour on her cheek and his thumb brushing it away. The touch had lasted less than a second. He had spent the days since treating it as if it were a line he could not cross again, though he had already crossed it once.

He tried to replace the thought with work. He checked the ferry’s ropes, cleaned the rail, inspected the channel markers, and took the longer watch when Nell offered to cover him. At the bow, he found a knot that had worked loose and retied it, pulling each turn snug before testing the line. He scrubbed a dark mark from the deck until the wood showed pale beneath the bristles. Then he walked the length of the ferry and checked the markers on the far shore, though he could have named each one with his eyes closed. None of it made the memory smaller. He could not know whether Sarah wanted him to return or whether his absence had given her relief. He would not choose an answer on her behalf.

In his cabin, the loaf Sarah had sent earlier in the week remained on the shelf, wrapped in a cloth with a blue stripe along the hem. He had taken it down twice and put it back without eating. The crust had dried at the edges. He could still smell the warmth it had carried when she gave it to him, and he knew the feeling inside it would reach him before the taste did.

Sarah’s bread carried what she felt while shaping it. He had seen the effect on passengers, the way a quiet room grew still around a slice, the way people looked toward the shore after tasting it. He did not need another reminder of what the loaf on his shelf might tell him. He was afraid of how clearly it would name the feeling he had kept out of his own mouth.

The Heron slowed again at the island point. The water was calm and the current familiar, yet the bow seemed to resist the turn. Jack checked the rudder and eased the throttle. Nothing was wrong with the boat. He brought her through the channel, but the crossing took longer than it should have, and Nell watched him without comment.

The delay drew no complaint at first. The passengers were used to the Heron’s steady pace, and most watched the shore or talked quietly among themselves. An older man with a fishing basket leaned near the rail and looked down at the water.

“Current turned?” he asked.

“No,” Jack said. “The channel’s clear.”

The man nodded, but kept watching him. Jack adjusted the tiller and brought the bow into the deeper part of the channel. The ferry answered with a small shudder, then moved forward. It was not danger, only reluctance, but the distinction did not comfort him. He knew every sound the Heron made when the water changed. This was the sound of a boat doing exactly what he asked while making him work for every yard.

A child near the stern tugged at his mother’s sleeve. “Is the boat tired?”

“No,” Jack called back. “She’s only taking her time.”

The mother smiled at him in thanks. The child accepted the answer and returned to watching the wake. Jack wished he could accept it as easily. He had spent years telling passengers what the water was doing, what the wind might do next, when they could expect to reach the landing. He had never had to explain a ferry that seemed to be holding a mirror up to its master.

At the far dock, the old man waited until the other passengers had gone ashore. “You’ve brought us safely,” he said. “That’s what matters.”

Jack nodded. He wanted to say that safe was not the same as right, but the words stayed behind his teeth. The man lifted his basket and walked toward the lane, leaving Jack with the line in his hands and the slow rocking of the hull against the posts.

Jack did not know what Sarah had found in Fenna’s journal, and he had no reason to connect her bread with the past. He knew only that he had begun to avoid the bakery after touching her cheek, and that the distance had not made him less aware of her. He had told himself he would never ask her to stay. The words sounded less like a principle now and more like a shelter he had built around himself.

At dusk, the harbor lanterns came on one by one, each flame small against the lake. Jack watched the lights from the wheelhouse and kept his hands on the tiller. The old custom said that two people who stood beneath the same lantern when it caught were thinking of each other. Gideon came along the quay with his ladder over one shoulder and his taper sheltered in a tin cup. He climbed to the nearest lamp and touched flame to wick. Jack could have stepped down and stood beneath it too; the distance was only a few yards. Instead he stayed behind the wheelhouse glass, where the lantern reflected faintly over the chart without reaching his face. When Gideon climbed down, he looked toward the ferry, but Jack pretended to check the weather glass. He stayed at the wheel and let the Heron rock against her lines.

He knew that if he spoke, Sarah’s bread might carry the feeling to the town. He did not want to expose her, and he did not want to make her answer for what he wanted. Yet silence was not neutral. It had made the ferry sluggish, had turned an ordinary crossing into work, and had left the loaf on his shelf untouched. He could not keep pretending the choice cost nothing.

The Heron carried him into the next morning, while Sarah’s loaf remained unopened on the shelf.



By the second afternoon, the Heron’s reluctance had become hard to ignore. She came off the line too slowly, then hung at the island turn as if the channel had narrowed. Jack checked the depth, watched the current, and tried again. The hull moved, but only after he eased the throttle and gave her more room than she needed.

Nell leaned against the rail. “You’re steering like you don’t want to arrive.”

“I’m steering the boat.”

“You’re doing both badly.”

He looked at her, but she had already turned away. She was not cruel. That made her observations harder to dismiss. She had seen him steer through sudden squalls and crowded crossings without losing his calm. She knew the difference between caution and avoidance.

Jack finished the run without answering. When the passengers were gone, he stayed at the wheel and watched the dock empty. He had not gone to the bakery. He had not asked Sarah what she had meant by the loaf or the silence beside the oven. He had managed to avoid her and had gained nothing from it.

That evening he went below and took the old loaf from the shelf. He unwrapped it and held it in both hands. The cloth carried the faint smell of rosemary. The crust had hardened, but the centre was still intact. He broke off a small piece and ate it standing beside the bunk.

He sat on the edge of the bunk and held the remaining loaf in his lap. The feeling it carried was not a clear message. It had no words and offered no answer. It was the memory of a room kept warm after closing, of Sarah staying at the counter while he stood beside the oven, of the way neither of them had moved into the space between their sleeves.

He had wanted to remain there. He had wanted the hour to last without needing to decide what it meant. The moment had been simple enough while it was happening. It was only afterward that he had begun to pull it apart, searching for the danger in every choice. If he stayed, he might make her feel obliged to keep him there. If he left, he might make her think he had regretted coming. He had chosen the second possibility and called it restraint.

The bread warmed slightly beneath his palm. Jack set it on the shelf and closed the cupboard. He could not keep treating every want as a threat. The thought frightened him because it did not tell him what to do next. It only took away the excuse he had been using.

He looked at the door of the cabin. The ferry moved gently at its moorings, and the sound of the water against the hull reached him through the boards. He had spent years learning when to hold a line and when to ease it. He had mistaken the same choice in his own life for a rule that could never change. He could not know what Sarah would say if he went to her. He could only stop deciding for her before she had the chance.

The feeling arrived before he had swallowed. It was warm at first, then lonely, then full of a tenderness that made him lower his eyes. He could see Sarah holding the loaf at the oven door, the heat colouring her cheek, the space between their sleeves held open by effort. He set the piece down and wrapped the rest again.

He would not let the bread decide for him. He would not let the ferry decide either. He could go to the bakery because he chose to, and leave if she asked him to. The thought was plain. Acting on it was harder.

On the third morning, Nell brought him the parcel. “There’s no order,” she said. “I wrapped it myself.”

“Then why carry it?”

“Because you won’t.”

She stood at the rail, waiting. Jack looked at the parcel and then at the landing. He had spent three days making his absence look like respect. He could not tell whether Sarah had noticed the effort, but he knew she had not asked for it.

He turned the parcel in his hands. Nell had tied the twine with her usual square knot, the one she used on spare line. Jack hooked a finger beneath it, then stopped. Nell had given him a destination and expected him to do the job. He tucked the parcel under his arm and went back to the wheelhouse.

Nell waited without helping him choose.

“You keep saying you won’t ask her to stay,” she said. “She hasn’t asked you to.”

“That doesn’t mean she wants me there.”

“No. It means you don’t know. You’ve decided not knowing is safer than hearing her answer.”

Jack looked toward the far shore. A line of cloud had gathered above the hills, but the water below it was still. He had read that sort of sky for years. He knew when to change course before the wind reached the channel. With Sarah, he kept waiting for a sign that would let him act without risking anything. There would never be one.

“I don’t want to make her feel obliged,” he said.

“Then don’t ask for an answer. Carry the parcel. Say good morning. Let her decide what comes after that.”

He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the paper. The loaf inside shifted slightly. It was an ordinary weight, no heavier than the one Sarah had carried to the ferry, but he felt the strain of holding it as if he were already standing at her door.

“What if she asks why I stayed away?”

“Tell her the truth.”

“That I was afraid?”

Nell looked at him. “You can choose your own words.”

He almost smiled, but the feeling did not last. He had spent years making a virtue of silence. It had kept him from asking people to remain when they might have wanted to leave. It had also kept him from finding out what they would choose if he let them speak.

The ferry shifted against its ropes. Jack felt the movement through the soles of his boots. He could stay at the landing and call the parcel a foolish errand. He could take it across town and knock on the door. Neither choice would change the fact that he wanted to see Sarah.

He had spent three days proving he could keep away. He was tired of the proof.

He took the parcel and stepped ashore. He walked along the harbor wall, past Gideon’s lantern post and the stacked fish baskets, then crossed Lantern Street. At the bakery door he stopped with the parcel under his arm. His hand rose toward the latch and stayed there.

He listened for movement inside. A tray scraped over the counter, followed by the soft thud of a loaf being set down. He could turn away without disturbing her. He could also knock and let her choose whether to open the door. The parcel rested against his ribs, warm through the paper. He lifted his free hand and knocked once. The knock barely carried beyond the door. Jack kept his hand raised for a moment, then lowered it and waited for her footsteps.
