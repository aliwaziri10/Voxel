Jack kept the Heron on the ordinary route for three mornings. He stayed away from Lantern Street, although the bakery was easy to find from the harbor wall. He watched the channel, checked the markers, and made each turn at the same point. There was no practical reason to pass the shop, and he used that fact as if it settled the matter.

Nell came up from below with a parcel wrapped in brown paper and tied with twine. She held it out without a smile. “She’s been asking for you,” she said, then corrected herself. “No. You’ve been waiting for her to ask.” Jack took the parcel. Their hands did not meet. Nell watched his face, saw the answer he would not give, and went back to the lines.

He kept the parcel against his ribs and did not open it. Its shape suggested a loaf. He could smell the faint trace of baked crust through the paper, and the familiar scent brought the bakery into his mind more clearly than a view of its window could have done. He told himself the parcel was an errand, nothing more. His grip tightened until the paper creased.

Late spring warmed the deck by midday. Jack kept his attention on the wheel, though his thoughts returned to Sarah’s face at the bakery door. He remembered the flour on her cheek and his thumb brushing it away. The touch had lasted less than a second. He had spent the days since treating it as if it were a line he could not cross again, though he had already crossed it once.

He had tried to replace the thought with work. He checked the ferry’s ropes, cleaned the rail, inspected the channel markers, and took the longer watch when Nell offered to cover him. None of it made the memory smaller. He could not know whether Sarah wanted him to return or whether his absence had given her relief. He would not choose an answer on her behalf.

In his cabin, the loaf Sarah had sent earlier in the week remained on the shelf, wrapped in a cloth with a blue stripe along the hem. He had taken it down twice and put it back without eating. The crust had dried at the edges. He could still smell the warmth it had carried when she gave it to him, and he knew the feeling inside it would reach him before the taste did.

Sarah’s bread carried what she felt while shaping it. He had seen the effect on passengers, the way a quiet room grew still around a slice, the way people looked toward the shore after tasting it. He did not need another reminder of what the loaf on his shelf might tell him. He was afraid of how clearly it would name the feeling he had kept out of his own mouth.

The Heron slowed again at the island point. The water was calm and the current familiar, yet the bow seemed to resist the turn. Jack checked the rudder and eased the throttle. Nothing was wrong with the boat. He brought her through the channel, but the crossing took longer than it should have, and Nell watched him without comment.

Jack did not know what Sarah had found in Fenna’s journal, and he had no reason to connect her bread with the past. He knew only that he had begun to avoid the bakery after touching her cheek, and that the distance had not made him less aware of her. He had told himself he would never ask her to stay. The words sounded less like a principle now and more like a shelter he had built around himself.

At dusk, the harbor lanterns came on one by one. Jack watched the lights from the wheelhouse and kept his hands on the tiller. The old custom said that two people who stood beneath the same lantern when it caught were thinking of each other. He did not go ashore to test it. He stayed at the wheel and let the Heron rock against her lines.

He knew that if he spoke, Sarah’s bread might carry the feeling to the town. He did not want to expose her, and he did not want to make her answer for what he wanted. Yet silence was not neutral. It had made the ferry sluggish, had turned an ordinary crossing into work, and had left the loaf on his shelf untouched. He could not keep pretending the choice cost nothing.

The Heron carried him into the next morning, the parcel still unopened beneath his arm.



By the second afternoon, the Heron’s reluctance had become hard to ignore. She came off the line too slowly, then hung at the island turn as if the channel had narrowed. Jack checked the depth, watched the current, and tried again. The hull moved, but only after he eased the throttle and gave her more room than she needed.

Nell leaned against the rail. “You’re steering like you don’t want to arrive.”

“I’m steering the boat.”

“You’re doing both badly.”

He looked at her, but she had already turned away. She was not cruel. That made her observations harder to dismiss. She had seen him steer through sudden squalls and crowded crossings without losing his calm. She knew the difference between caution and avoidance.

Jack finished the run without answering. When the passengers were gone, he stayed at the wheel and watched the dock empty. He had not gone to the bakery. He had not asked Sarah what she had meant by the loaf or the silence beside the oven. He had managed to avoid her and had gained nothing from it.

That evening he went below and took the old loaf from the shelf. He unwrapped it and held it in both hands. The cloth carried the faint smell of rosemary. The crust had hardened, but the centre was still intact. He broke off a small piece and ate it standing beside the bunk.

The feeling arrived before he had swallowed. It was warm at first, then lonely, then full of a tenderness that made him lower his eyes. He could see Sarah holding the loaf at the oven door, the heat colouring her cheek, the space between their sleeves held open by effort. He set the piece down and wrapped the rest again.

He would not let the bread decide for him. He would not let the ferry decide either. He could go to the bakery because he chose to, and leave if she asked him to. The thought was plain. Acting on it was harder.

On the third morning, Nell brought him the parcel. “There’s no order,” she said. “I wrapped it myself.”

“Then why carry it?”

“Because you won’t.”

She stood at the rail, waiting. Jack looked at the parcel and then at the landing. He had spent three days making his absence look like respect. He could not tell whether Sarah had noticed the effort, but he knew she had not asked for it.

He took the parcel and stepped ashore.
