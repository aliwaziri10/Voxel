Sarah lit the lantern again the night after the stair, and in the morning the bread knew it.

It was not good bread. She was careful about that, because Odalys would be, and a baker who lied to herself about a loaf paid for it at the counter. The rolls had come out of the oven lopsided and gold, a little too brown on one shoulder where she had forgotten them, and when she pulled one apart the crumb steamed in her face like a held breath being let go. Odalys ate one standing at the back door. She chewed it for a long time. She said, “Better,” and then, after a pause so long that Sarah began to count it, “Not good. Better,” and went off down the lane looking pleased with herself and a little as though she had been crying.

The eight gray loaves stayed on the cellar shelf. Sarah went down to look at them before the shop opened. They had not changed color, but the chill had gone out of the cellar for good, and the whole room smelled of yeast and warm wood as it had on the stair, and when she stood among them with her palm lifted she could still feel the exact width of the air she had stood in. An inch. Her hand knew it as a tongue knows the place where a tooth has been. She put the hand in her apron pocket and kept it there for most of the morning.

By noon the heat had thickened until it sat on the shoulders like a hand. Flour clung to her forearms and would not brush off. Out over the far shore, above the line of the hills, the sky had grown a bruise, a yellow-gray bank of cloud standing very still, and every dog in Lantern Street had gone to lie in the shade of its own doorstep and was breathing at the cobbles. Sarah worked with the back door wedged open and the front door too, and no air moved through either.

She knew what she was waiting for, and she was not waiting for it. That was the rule she kept now. She did not wait. She shaped the evening loaves for the harbor master’s widow at the far end of the harbor lane, a standing order, twelve plain rounds in a covered basket, and she did not think about the question she had asked him on the stair. She had given him leave to find one. She had not said by when.

At the back door hung Fenna’s coat. It was a long waxed canvas thing, the color of a wet slate, with a hood and a row of horn buttons and sleeves that came down over her knuckles. Fenna had been a tall woman. Sarah had taken the coat from its peg in the first week because the shop was cold and she had nothing of her own, and she had worn it every wet morning since, and she knew its smell by now the way she knew her own hair: beeswax, and cold iron, and a faint ghost of cardamom in the lining. She took it down now, though there was not a cloud overhead that she could see from the door, and hooked the basket over one arm.

“You’ll want that,” said Odalys, passing in the lane with a crate of eggs. “There’ll be rain before the lamps.”

“It’s a clear sky.”

“It is a clear sky,” Odalys agreed, “over the wrong half of the lake.”

The harbor lane was empty at that hour, and so was the pier. The widow lived in the last house before the water, and she took her twelve loaves from Sarah without a word except to say that the weather was heavy, and that the lake smelled of iron, and that Sarah had better not dawdle. Sarah did not dawdle. She came out of the little yard with the empty basket and found him standing at the head of the pier with his hat in his hands.

Jack had not been there when she went in. She would have noticed. He stood now very straight, in his good coat, with no cloth in his hands and no loaf and nothing at all to carry, and he was looking at the planks with the same careful stillness she had seen at the lantern. A small wind had come up behind him and was stirring his hair, and she saw that his knuckles had gone pale where they held the brim.

“I have a question,” he said.

Her heart lurched, clumsy and large in her chest, like a loaf dropped on a stone floor.

“Go on.”

“Will you let me walk you home?”

She had braced for a different sentence. She had lain awake working out how she would answer it. Now it was this, plain and small and carried up the pier like a cup too full, and she felt the laugh rise in her throat and the other thing rise under the laugh, and she held both of them behind her teeth.

“That is a question,” she said.

“It is the only one I could find.” He turned the hat a quarter turn in his hands. “I looked for a better. I am told it takes practice.”

“It will do,” Sarah said. “It will do very well.”

They walked up the harbor lane side by side, with an arm’s length of cobbles between them. It was not far to Lantern Street. It was a quarter of an hour at her ordinary pace. She walked at half of it, and felt him adjust his stride to hers without a word, and neither of them said anything about that either.

The first drop fell on the back of her hand. It was warm and as heavy as a coin.

The second hit his hat brim. The third hit the cobbles with a small flat sound, and then all the rest of the sky came down at once.

It was not rain as she knew it. It was a wall. It struck the lane so hard that the stones jumped, and the gutters filled between one breath and the next, and the whole street vanished behind a gray roar. Sarah gasped, and the sound was lost in the din. She had the basket in one arm and her other hand was already at the horn buttons of the coat, and she did not think. She tore the coat open and flung one side of it wide.

“Here!”

He did not argue. He ducked under the edge of the canvas and she brought the other panel round, and then the two of them were standing in a single canvas tent that smelled of beeswax and wet iron, with the rain drumming a hundred small fists on the hood above her head.

There was no room. The coat had been made for a tall woman with her arms at her sides, not for two grown people and a basket, and the only way to keep both of them under it was for him to bend his head and for her to turn in against his side. His shoulder came against hers. His hip came against her hip. His arm went round behind her, not touching her back, held an inch off the cloth with a visible effort, and its heat came through the air between his sleeve and her spine anyway. She held the coat closed in front of them with both fists. The basket pressed against her ribs.

“Walk,” she said, because one of them had to say it.

They walked.

It was the slowest she had ever gone anywhere. Her boots were soaked in three steps, and her hem dragged, and the water ran down the inside of the canvas wherever it touched, and none of it mattered. He had to match her step exactly or the coat would drag, and so he did. They went down the lane in a single shuffling, careful beast of canvas and wet wool, hip against hip, with the rain on the hood and the lamps just coming up along the walls, each one blurring to a smear of gold behind the gray.

Heat came through the wet fabric. She would not have believed it. She was soaked to the skin on the outside, her cotton sleeve clinging to her arm, and his shirt was soaked too, she could feel it under the thin cloth of his coat where the rain had found its way in, and under both of them there was the other thing. His body. The long steady warmth of him, coming through two layers of wet cloth as plainly as the heat comes through the side of an oven. She felt it along her whole left side, from the shoulder to the knee. She felt each step he took, the shift and settle of his hip against hers, the muscle that moved in his arm behind her back and then held still again. She kept her eyes on the lane. She kept her fists locked on the canvas so hard that her nails cut her palms.

He did not say a word. She could hear him breathing. It came shallow and careful, and when the lane narrowed at the corner by the grocer’s and the wall pressed them closer, she heard it stop altogether for the space of three steps and then begin again.

Neither of them hurried. A boy ran past under a sack, shrieking with joy, and splashed them both to the knee, and they did not hurry. A cart stood abandoned in the road with its horse hanging its head in the rain, and they went round it at the pace of a funeral. Water ran off the end of the hood in a bright string and fell between their boots. She thought that if the street were twice as long she would have walked all of it, and that the thought was dangerous, and she went on having it.

The storm had broken the heat. She could smell it. The air that came in under the coat’s edge was cool for the first time since spring, and it smelled of wet stone and cut leaves, and under it ran a faintly sharp and sad edge that she knew from other years in other places. It was the smell of the first evening of the turn. The season had been going all summer, and she had not looked at it. Now the rain had laid a hand on her shoulder and shown her where it stood. She did not say so. She pressed closer to the warm side of the coat and let it be a secret between herself and the weather.

The basket was between them, hooked on her elbow and crushed against both their ribs. She had forgotten it. Then, in the middle of the long slow walk, she felt the warmth coming through the wicker and she looked down.

She had given the widow her twelve loaves and kept six rolls in the bottom of the basket under the cloth, for her own supper, the ones she had shaped with her mind on the stair. They should have been sodden by now. The canvas had let in a trickle at the collar, and the wicker was dark with wet. But when she lifted the cloth, the rolls sat there dry and brown and breathing a low, steady heat, as though they had come that minute from the oven. A single curl of steam rose from them and went out into the cool.

She laid the cloth back. She did not look at him. He had seen it, though. She knew he had by the way his arm tightened behind her, one small clench, and then relaxed again, as if it had been caught out.

Lantern Street. The bakery. The window glowed ahead of them through the downpour like a lamp left burning for her, and she felt the end of the walk coming as a loss, a plain physical thing, a warm weight lifted off her. They slowed still further. They came to the shop door and stopped on the step beneath the narrow overhang, where the rain fell in a curtain at the edge of the coat and drummed on the lintel above them.

She did not open the coat. She could not. If she opened it he would step out of it, and the cold would come in, and that would be that.

She raised her eyes to say goodnight. They would not go as high as his. They stopped at his chin, at a small place under the corner of his jaw where a line of water ran down toward his collar, and she found that she could not look at anything above it. His mouth was an inch from the edge of her sight. She did not look at his mouth.

“Goodnight,” she said, to his chin.

“Goodnight, Sarah.”

His voice was very low. He did not move. She did not move. For the length of three long breaths they stood in the dark of the canvas, with the water coming down in sheets on every side and her fists locked on the horn buttons, and nothing in the whole world was said.

Then she let the coat fall open, and the cool came in like a blade, and he stepped out into the rain.

She went into the shop. She stood inside the door in the dim with the water running off her hem onto the boards. When she turned, he was still there on the cobbles in the full downpour, hatless, with the rain streaming down his face, looking at the window. He lifted one hand, a very small movement, and then he walked away down the hill.

The six rolls in the basket were warm on the counter. The coat hung from its peg by the back door, dripping, and she touched its sleeve before she went up the stairs, and the canvas was still warm on the inside, with the exact heat of two.
