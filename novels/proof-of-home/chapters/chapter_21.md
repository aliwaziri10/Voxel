The sky had turned the color of a bruised plum when the clouds split open. Rain fell hard and fast, cold and insistent, and Sarah Quill pulled the heavy wool coat from the hook above the sink, sliding it over her head. Jack Mercer stood beside her, his own coat already damp at the elbows, the fabric clinging and reminding him of every inch of space between them. The coat hung between them like a barrier, and they walked the narrow path toward the harbor, arms wrapped tight against their ribs, shoulders pressed together, hips aligned. Neither wanted the walk to end. The rain paid no attention to their reluctance; it fell in sheets, turning the cobblestones into slick mirrors that reflected the gray sky and the dark silhouettes of the trees lining the lane.

At the corner where the path curved around the old mill, Sarah reached for his shoulder and found his hand gripping hers. The grip was firm enough to anchor her, soft enough to suggest more than necessity. She felt the warmth of his skin through the thin wool and the slight tremor in his fingers that matched the rhythm of the rain. They moved slower than usual, each step deliberate, as if the ground might shift if they hurried. The coat pulled tighter around their shoulders, and the air between them seemed to thicken. She could smell the salt of the sea on the wind coming off the water, mixed with the faint scent of woodsmoke from the houses along the shore. The bread in her basket rested against her hip, warm and solid despite the storm outside. She had baked the loaf earlier that morning, its quiet steadiness recalling a summer afternoon, and it waited unchanged.

They reached the harbor steps just as the last drops fell. The stone was slick under their feet, and Sarah stopped, letting the weight of the coat settle fully across her shoulders. Jack looked up at her, his expression unreadable, and she saw the question in his eyes without him speaking. The rain kept falling, but neither moved. The coat was too large for one person, and the space between them felt charged, an invisible thread linking their breathing. She leaned forward, bringing her face closer to his, and when she spoke, her voice was barely above the sound of the rain.

Goodnight, he said, though she knew he did not mean it as a farewell. His lips brushed the edge of her jaw, cool and brief, and she felt the contact like a small spark landing on hot dough. She did not pull away. Instead, she let the moment stretch, as one might hold a flame at arm's length, careful not to let it go out. The bread in her basket pulsed with quiet warmth, a steady heartbeat beneath the wool. She thought about the loaf she had shaped that morning, the way her hands had moved through the flour, the intention she carried with each fold. That loaf was calm. This moment was not.

They stood for a long time, the rain washing over them, the coat pulling them closer. The pressure of their bodies along the length of the path was a kind of intimacy that required no words. She felt his breath on her neck, the rise and fall of his chest, and understood that this was the part of the day that would linger. The next hour would pass in a blur of wet pavement and distant gulls, and when they finally reached the lamplight inn, the coat would be discarded and the distance would return. But right now, in this moment, the ache was building. It was the ache of wanting to stay, of wanting to be the kind of person who chose one place over another. The bread in her basket stayed dry and warm, a small proof that some things could hold their shape even when everything else dissolved into rain.

She turned to face him properly, and the rain had begun to ease, leaving a glistening film on the stones. The harbor was visible now, the water black and still under the fading light. The Heron waited at the far end of the dock, its hull dark against the darker water. Jack had been here before, a constant presence at the edge of the town. He nodded toward the boat, and she nodded back, the gesture automatic, familiar. The coat lay folded on the bench near the entrance, and she picked it up, wrapping it around her again. Its weight was comforting, a reminder of the night they had spent together in the shelter of the inn, the way he had listened to her talk about the shop, the bread, and the feeling that lived inside her hands.

When she reached the door, she paused. The rain had slowed to a gentle patter, and the air smelled of wet earth and the faint mineral tang of the lake. She looked at him, really looked, and saw the tension in his shoulders, the way his gaze held hers without blinking. He watched her the way he watched everything—carefully, without judgment, with an attention that felt like a promise. She wanted to cross the threshold, take his hand, let the coat slip free, and stand alone in the evening air. But the rain was still falling, the path home was long, and the ache in her chest grew heavier with each minute. She stepped inside the inn, closed the door behind her, and let the warmth of the lamps wash over her.

The bread in her basket stayed dry and warm beneath the coat, a small certainty in the chaos. The last chapter had ended with one inch of air between them, a gap that felt like a held breath. Now, standing in the doorway of the Lamplight Inn, Sarah felt the weight of that silence pressing against her ribs. She would have to choose soon, or not choose at all. The seasons were turning, summer stretching toward its end, and the ache that had been building since the kiss in chapter twenty‑seven would not ease until the very last page. For now, she allowed herself to stand in the doorway, the rain still falling outside, the coat still heavy on her shoulders, and let the feeling of him remain. One inch of air. She could almost see it, the space between them, the possibility of closing it. But the rain kept falling, the bread stayed warm, and the night stretched ahead, full of choices she had not yet made.
