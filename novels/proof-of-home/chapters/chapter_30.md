The Heron began to sulk again in the last of the autumn, and Jack knew whose fault it was.

She took two turns of the key where she had once taken one. She dragged to port in a flat calm and wanted the wheel held against her like a horse shown the barn door. For a little while after the evening on the lake she had run as true as a plumb line, and a boat that has been allowed to run true once will not forgive being asked for less. He did not blame her. He was doing the same thing to himself.

The weeks since the cottage had run under him like water under a keel, steady and cold, each one colder than the last. He had kept his word. He had waited and not asked, and he had learned what a promise like that took out of a man, a little every hour, paid out like line. They were not apart. She came down the pier each morning with the loaves, and when the frost lay thick he carried her basket up Lantern Street and said goodnight to her face with his hands in his pockets, where they could be trusted. Twice she had come out to the cottage for supper, and twice they had sat on opposite sides of the scrubbed table without once looking at the bench. Each time she left a round loaf on the board. Each time he lay awake with his palm open on his chest, as if it still held the shape of her back.

Her bread had changed. It no longer tasted of joy, or of the plain deep want that had sung down Lantern Street the morning after the lake. It tasted of waiting. He ate a heel of it every dawn before the first run, and it tasted of a lamp left burning on a sill with the door on the latch, of a woman at a window who had made up her mind to stand there until she knew. It was patient. That was what undid him. It asked nothing. It only stayed lit.

The whole lake could taste it. The passengers ate in silence and watched the far shore as if they expected to be met there. The woman in the green scarf wept once, quietly, over a roll, and apologized to it. Out on the water the fishermen in their skiffs, who had never had a crumb of it, lifted their heads toward the harbor as the loaves passed and held still and could not have said why. The waiting drifted across Larkmere Water like woodsmoke, and the Heron carried it to and fro on her sulking keel.

On the clerk’s board the square chalk had been rubbed down to a short line. It was a matter of mornings now. Nobody spoke of it. Everybody read it.

Gideon Teague was waiting at the head of the pier in the gray before the first run, with his taper in his belt and, for once, no ladder.

“A word, Jack, if I may.”

“Is it about a lamp?”

“It is not about a lamp.”

The old man did not look at him. He looked at the water, where a skin of ice had begun to form among the reeds, and took from inside his coat a paper folded small and gone soft along its creases as cloth.

“Fenna wrote this the winter after he went,” said Gideon. “She gave it to me and told me to keep it for whoever loved the ferryman. Not before they knew it themselves, she said. And not after it was too late.” He turned the paper over once in his fingers. “I have spent a good many years trying to judge which of those I was in for.”

“And now?”

“Now you have kissed her in front of the whole harbor and an old man on a ladder. I would say you know it.” He glanced at the board. “And the chalk is nearly gone. I told you once I wondered every year whether keeping a thing was a kindness. I find I am out of years.”

Jack took it. It weighed nothing. “Have you read it?”

“The night she gave it to me, and not since. Read it where nobody can see your face.” Gideon laid two fingers to his cap and climbed Lantern Street to his lanterns.

Jack put the paper inside his coat. It lay against his ribs as light as a leaf and as heavy as a sounding lead, and it stayed there through the whole of the morning run.

He read it in the wheelhouse at the noon lull, with the door shut and the Heron rocking at her lines. The hand was Fenna’s, tight and black and upright. She had written it in plain words, not in marks. He understood that for the courtesy it was, to a man who could not read her margins.

To whoever loves the ferryman, and to the ferryman, if he has any sense.

He should have asked. I want it set down, since I cannot say it to him. He decided that wanting me to stay would be selfish and that going first would be kind, and he took the last run of the season, and I let him. Two people stood on either side of one question for years, each waiting for the other to be brave first, and the lake took the years.

I kept a lantern lit for him. A lantern is not a question. Do not hang one in a window and call it patience.

Whichever of you is reading this, ask. Do not wait to be asked. Say it badly, say it once, say it in the rain. Then bear the answer.

F.

He read it twice.

Ask.

For the space of one reading he thought it was a door. Then he thought of a woman on a bench in his cottage, her back against his chest, telling the wall that a man could be large the way weather is large, and that she had made herself the size of whatever room was left.

He had felt it in himself on the cellar stair and again by the stove: a freshet where there had been a trickle, brown and bank-high and wanting the whole valley. A man who asked her to stay with that behind him was not putting a question. He was putting a flood to a doorsill. I want you to stay, in his mouth, would be no request. It would be rising water with her standing in the bed of it, and she had spent years making herself small enough to live through that. He would not be the one who left her a smaller room.

And the other road was written on the paper in his hand. The ferryman before his grandfather had called his silence kind. Jack had called his the same word, quietly, for most of a season. A man cannot read his chart when both channels are marked in the same ink.

Nell took the stern line at the end of the noon run and looked at his face, and then at the place on his coat where his palm lay flat over the paper, and said nothing, which cost her visibly.

“Go up to Odalys,” said Jack. “I am taking her out. Alone.”

“Uncle?”

“Please, Nell.”

She went. She did not argue, and that was how he knew what he looked like.

He took the Heron past the harbor wall with nobody aboard and nothing to carry but himself. She would not settle. The engine kept losing its note and finding it again, like a man humming a tune he had forgotten the end of, and she wallowed in the flat gray calm with her head wandering to port. He ran her out to the middle, where the shores were only low lines on either side, cut the engine, and let her lie.

He did not lash the wheel. He had no right to ask her for a kindness he had not earned.

He sat on the bench beside it and tried the question in every order, as a man tries every chart for a passage that is not on any of them. Asked first, asked last, asked in daylight with the whole harbor watching, asked in the cottage with the stove ticking. Each way he put it, he heard her breath catch, and each way he could not tell whether he was hearing a yes or a woman feeling the room grow smaller.

After a long while the Heron swung on the faint air until her bow pointed at the town, and he took it for a verdict and could not have said whose.

The engine caught on the first turn. She took him home willingly, the one thing all day she had consented to, and the sun dropped behind the roofs as he came in, a smear of copper under a lid of cloud.

Three passengers stood at the head of the pier with their baskets, and Nell stood in front of them with her arms folded. “She has a cough,” she was telling them, with tremendous authority. “She is not fit to carry a cat.” They drifted off unconvinced. Nell took the stern line from him without a word, gave him a look with no meddling in it at all, said only, “It is going to freeze, Uncle,” and climbed the pier toward the street.

He did not go ashore. He stood at the end of the pier by the mooring post with the paper in his fist and watched the light go. Frost came up white on the boards. The lamps along the quay stood black and cold, and at the foot of Lantern Street a small figure with a taper had not yet begun.

He heard her before he saw her: boots on the frozen planks, quick, and then slower. She came down the length of the pier in Fenna’s waxed coat with the horn buttons done to her chin and her breath smoking, and stopped a pace off. He did not know when she had learned that. A lantern post stood at her shoulder, black and unlit.

“Nell told three people the Heron has a cough,” said Sarah.

“She has.”

“Nell also told me it was not the Heron.”

He said nothing. The letter was a hard small knot in his fist, and she looked at it, and her face changed.

“That is Fenna’s,” said Sarah quietly. “I would know the fold.”

“Gideon kept it. She left it for whoever loved the ferryman.” He heard how that sounded and let it stand.

Her gloved hand went to her mouth and came down again. “What does it say?”

He had thought of the weather. He found, with the lake behind him, that he had run out of weather. “That he should have asked.”

The water slapped once against a piling and was quiet.

She did not move. He watched her understand it all: what he had not asked, and what she had not answered, and the short line of chalk on the clerk’s board. She had promised to tell him to his face when she knew, and he saw that she did not know yet, and that standing there not knowing cost them both the same.

“Sarah,” he said. It was all he had.

She crossed the pace in one step.

Both her fists shut in the front of his coat, gloved and shaking, and she hauled him down to her hand over hand, like line gone slack, without a scrap of grace, and her mouth found his. It was not the lake. The lake had been a question put gently. This was what the question had been about. All the weeks of waiting broke out of her at once, cold lips and hot breath and a small raw sound against his teeth, and he caught her by the back of the coat and by the hair at the back of her head and lifted her onto her toes and walked her two steps, three, until the iron post stopped her shoulders and she did not seem to notice it. There was no thought left in him. Not the depth, not the draft, not the set of the current. She was warm through every layer of wool, ribs and hips and the whole length of her pressed to the whole length of him, her fists wringing his coat as if she meant to climb inside it. He heard himself say her name into her mouth, and could not have said it twice.

He had not asked.

The thought came up like a sounding line, cold and exact, from a great depth. He had asked her leave at every door they had come to, as a man tests ice before he steps. Here, at dusk, with a dead woman’s letter crushed in his fist, he had forgotten how, and she had not waited, and neither of them had minded. That was what frightened him. The size of it. A want that could put out the habit of a whole season in one breath.

He thought of a woman saying large, the way weather is large. He felt it in himself, not a trickle now but a freshet, brown and bank-high, and she was standing in the bed of it with her fists in his coat.

He stepped back.

It took the whole of him. He took his hands from her one finger at a time, and she felt each one go, and her fists tightened once in the wool and let go. He took a pace. He took another, backward along the frozen boards, until the mooring post met his shoulders and his fist closed round it and the old rope scar went white across the knuckles. He was shaking. So was she. Her hair had come loose. Her mouth was bright and her eyes were black and she was breathing as if she had run the length of the town.

“I did not ask,” said Jack.

“I did not wait.”

“I always ask.”

“I know.” Her breath smoked between them. “I noticed.”

“You told me about a man who was large the way weather is large. I felt it in me just now. I want you so much that it would fill the room and leave you none to stand in, and I am afraid that is the very thing that will send you away.” The post was cold under his fist. “Not the lake. Not the winter. This. Me, wanting you like this.”

“Jack.”

She took half a pace toward him and stopped. He watched her stop. He watched her think about the half pace, and the post at her back, and the width of planks between them, and he saw the moment she chose. Her hands went behind her. Her gloves closed on the iron of the lantern post and held, the knuckles standing out through the leather, and she did not follow.

She had been very good once at standing with her hands folded, and this was not that. This was a woman keeping the width he had made, and holding an iron post so that she would keep it. It was the most generous thing anyone had ever done for him, and it very nearly took his knees.

Along the quay the taper had begun. The lamps caught one by one with a small soft sound, each flame arriving a breath after the one before, working down the boards toward the end of the pier. The lamp above her took. It laid her shadow across the frozen planks toward him, and the shadow stopped a yard short of his boots.

He held his post. She held hers. Under the boards the black water went on breathing.
