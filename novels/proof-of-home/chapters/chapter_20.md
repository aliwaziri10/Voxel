The Heron rode flat against the dock. High summer had stilled the wind on Larkmere Water, and the surface lay like a sheet of glass that had not yet cracked. Jack coiled the mooring line and stepped ashore. He noticed what people carried, as he always did, but today there were only a few market baskets and a child with a dripping ice cone, and none of it was his concern.

The ferry would not carry a lie. He knew this as he knew the weight of the tiller in his hands. If someone aboard hid what they felt, the Heron drifted slow and sulked, and the crossing took longer than it should. Today the water had been calm and the passengers had been honest, or at least quiet enough not to disturb the surface. He had brought them across without incident, and now the boat sat quiet beside the pilings, waiting for evening.

He walked up from the harbor into the quiet street. Lantern Street took its name from the harbor lights, but the lamps were not lit yet. Gideon Teague would come at dusk and make his round, and until then the town held its breath in the golden hush before evening. There was a saying about the lanterns, that whoever stood at the same lamp when it caught was thinking of the same person. Jack did not put much stock in sayings, but he noticed the unlit lamps all the same.

He passed the Lamplight Inn without going in, where Odalys was probably inside wiping down tables and reading faces as she always did. He kept walking until he reached the bakery.

Sarah was inside, visible through the glass as she moved behind the counter. She had flour on her forearm and the sleeves of her dress rolled to her elbows. The ovens had been banked for the day, but the heat still rose from the brick, a slow exhalation that met him on the sidewalk. He stopped and watched her for a moment as she arranged loaves on a rack, her hands careful, her posture familiar. He noticed the way she carried herself—the set of her shoulders, the small movements of her hands as she moved through the space she had inherited from her aunt.

She turned and saw him, unlocking the door and opening it before he could knock.

"You're late," she said.

"I had a full boat."

"Then you should have stayed on it."

He looked at her hands: one held the keys, the other rested on the doorframe, fingers curled around the wood as if she were holding herself in place.

"I came to tell you something," he said.

She waited, not stepping back, not inviting him in.

"I can't make you stay," he said. "And I'm afraid that I want to."

The words hung between them; the key in her hand did not move, and she did not look away.

"You don't get to decide that for me," she said.

"I'm not deciding. I'm saying I won't ask."

"That's the same thing."

"It isn't."

"It is exactly the same thing. You think if you don't ask, you aren't trapping me. But you are. You're making the choice for both of us."

She pushed the door wider, and the heat from the ovens rushed out to touch his face. "Come inside."

He followed her into the shop. Loaves wrapped in cloths rested on the cooling racks, and the air smelled of flour and yeast and something older that he could not name. Sarah walked past the counter toward the back room, Jack trailing behind her, his boots quiet on the floorboards. He noticed the flour still dusting her hairline, and that she did not brush it away.

"The flour cellar," she said. "I need to check the bin."

"The lamp?"

"It's down there."

They went through the narrow door at the rear of the shop, where the stairs descended into dimness. A single lamp hung from a hook on the wall, its flame small and uncertain. Jack went down first, Sarah following. The stairs were too narrow for two to pass, so they had to stop. She stood above him, or he below, the space between their shoulders no wider than a board.

The lamp guttered; a draft must have found its way through the crack beneath the cellar door, and the flame bent low and nearly died before steadying again. Jack looked at her, and she looked back. Neither of them moved.

"Sarah," he said.

"I'm listening."

"I never ask anyone to stay."

"I know."

"My mother left on this ferry when I was a boy. She stepped onto the Heron one morning and she never came back. I watched the water take her, and after that I decided I would never be the one to ask someone to remain."

She said nothing, her fingers locked behind her back. His fists hung at his sides, knuckles pressed against his thighs.

Nobody was stopping them: the door to the bakery was shut, Odalys was across the street, and Nell was on the dock or somewhere else, never coming this close anyway. The lamp, the narrow stair, and the heat pressing down from the roof above were all that filled the space around them.

Jack did not reach for her, and Sarah did not step down. They stood close enough to feel each other breathe; the air between them was one inch thick, and it held.

The cellar smelled of yeast and warm wood, a scent that rose from the sacks of flour, the bins, and the old beams holding up the shop for sixty years. It was the smell of Fenna and Sarah, and of the bread baked here for as long as Jack could remember. He stood in it and did not speak.

He kept his eyes fixed on the flame; she kept hers fixed on his face. The distance between them did not change. His breath came slow and shallow, and hers matched it. A word went unsaid, then another, then all of them.

The first real hurt had been hers. He remembered it from the last time they had stood this close, or nearly this close, when she had looked at him and seen that he would not choose her. He had carried that hurt ever since, a stone in his pocket, wet with lake water.

"I can't make you stay," he said again, quieter.

"And I can't make you ask," she said.

"Then where does that leave us?"

"On these stairs," she said. "With the lamp."

The flame guttered once more and caught. The light fell on her cheek, the line of her collar, and the flour still dusting his jacket. Neither of them touched. Neither of them moved. The air stayed one inch thick, and the heat of summer pressed against the walls, and the water outside lapped at the dock like something waiting to be crossed.
