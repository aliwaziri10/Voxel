Jack held out against the loaf until midnight.

He had carried it into the wheelhouse and set it on the shelf beside the grey one from the night before, and for hours he looked at the pair of them the way a man looks at two stones in a quay wall, trying to judge which was holding up the other. But the plain loaf kept a little warmth from her arms, and he had never learned to leave warmth lying about. At midnight he took it out onto the deck, with the Heron’s lamp turned low, and tore off the heel where the lake could see him.

It tasted of flour and water and salt. She had told him so on the pier, and she had not lied, and he knew what a lie tasted like in her bread. This was not one. It was a loaf with nothing put into it on purpose, and by the third mouthful he understood that the nothing weighed more than all the grey ones together. She had carried it down the hill in both hands, and everything she had kept out of it was still in the crumb, in the shape of the places where it had been held back. He could not name one piece of it. He wanted every piece. He sat on the bench by the wheelhouse with crumbs on his knee and the water slapping the hull, and he did not sleep.

I miss you. I did not expect to. And you are standing right in front of me.

He had answered with a speech about the autumn. He had listened to himself make it, in the harbor clerk’s voice, as a man hears a line part out in the dark: one long clear interval in which he could see exactly where the weight would fall and could do nothing about it. Two fishermen had been picking at a net six feet away, and he had delivered his kindness in front of them, as though a crowded place were an excuse for being cruel.

His hand went to the pocket before he told it to. The small round weight lay where it had lain for a fortnight, against his ribs. At dawn it would be there still.

The standing loaf did not come down to the gangplank in the morning. The grocer’s boy brought it, out of breath, and said Miss Quill had a great deal of work on. Jack said that was understandable. He took the cloth from the boy by its corner, and the loaf inside was warm and honest and had been made by a woman who had decided to be civil, which was the worst news yet.

The Heron took an hour and a quarter over a crossing that wanted forty minutes. She dragged at every buoy. She sulked past the island with her nose low, and the passengers fanned themselves with their hats and said the lake was heavy today. Nell kept to the stern with her arms folded and watched the far shore back away as if it were an argument she had already won.

On the second run the old man with the canvas satchel, the one who always waited for every other passenger to move, stopped beside the wheelhouse on his way off. He did not look at Jack. He looked at the water.

“She was quicker,” he said, “when you were happy.”

Then he shifted his satchel and went ashore, and Jack kept both hands on the tiller and found there was nothing to say to a man who had only told the truth.

Toward evening, with the last load ashore and the lamps coming up one by one along the harbor wall, Nell stopped on the gangplank with the stern line over her shoulder.

“Look up the hill,” she said.

He did not need to. Every dusk since midsummer he had looked for the small gold square that lay on the cobbles outside the bakery. Last night it had not been there. Tonight the rest of the street was already lit, and the place where it should have been was a dark gap, like a plank gone from a quay.

“Two nights,” Nell said.

“I know.”

“Then I cannot think why you are still standing on this deck.” She took the stern line up the pier with her and did not look round.

He walked the length of the pier and half of Lantern Street before he knew he had left the boat. Gideon was on his ladder at the top of the hill, trimming the last lantern, and did not turn his head.

“Latch is up,” he said, to the wick.

Jack waited on the step with his hand on the latch for the length of one held breath. A woman went by with a bucket and a look of frank pity. He lifted it to be rid of her.

The shop was dim. The blinds were half down, and the long counter had been scrubbed to the grain, and Sarah was behind it with a cloth, wiping a place that was already clean. She did not look up when the bell rang. Her hair had come loose on one side. There was flour on her wrist and she had not noticed. He stood inside the door with his hat in his hands, struck by how small the room was and how entirely she filled it.

“We are closed,” she said.

“I know.”

He crossed to the counter. He unbuttoned the left pocket of his coat and, for the first time in a fortnight, took out what lay in it and set it on the scrubbed wood between them. The bun had gone as hard as quay stone. It had split along its old seam, and the cardamom had faded to a rumor, and the two small dents in the top were there still, side by side, with a narrow gap between them.

She looked at it a long while. Then she picked it up and turned it over in her fingers like a coin from another country, and set her thumb into one of the dents. It fitted exactly.

“Those are mine,” she said. “I never meant to put them there.”

“I know. I kept them anyway.”

She put the bun back down, nearer to him than to her, and took her hand away.

“I could not throw it in the lake,” he said. “I could not eat it either.”

“It will have gone to stone.”

“Yes.”

“Then it is no use to anyone.”

“It was of use to me.” The sentence came out so plainly that he heard it land on the wood beside the bun. He had planned a great many sentences on the hill, and every one of them had been about the weather. This was the only one left. He laid his hands flat on the counter to stop them moving. “I cannot make you stay, Sarah, and I am afraid of how much I want to.”

The cloth went still in her hand. Her eyes came up, red at the rims. She had not slept either, and she looked at him as though he had spoken a language she had studied for months and was only now hearing aloud.

“On the pier you told me I would hardly think of the ferry.”

“I was wrong.”

“You were not wrong. You were deciding.” She laid the cloth down. “You wrote me a whole winter, Jack. The autumn, and the people I would have about me, and how soon I would forget. You did it kindly, and you did it without asking me one question.”

“What question?”

“Any of them.” Her voice shook and she steadied it. “Stop deciding for me. If I leave, I will have left. If I stay, I will have stayed. But I will be the one to say it, and you will not take that from me by being polite.”

It went into him cleanly, like a sounding lead into deep water, and found no bottom.

“Why?” she asked, more quietly. “Who left, that you are so sure how it ends?”

He had told no one. Gideon knew, because Gideon knew everything, and his grandfather had known and never spoken of it, and neither of them had ever said it to him in words. He looked at the bun.

“My mother,” he said. “She went up the plank of the Heron in the rain, in a grey wool coat with a brass button hanging by one thread. I was small enough that the pier rail came to my eyes. My grandfather stood behind me with his hand on my shoulder. He did not ask her to stay, and he did not hold me back, and I did not say a word. I was too young to know I could have.” He drew breath. “I have never asked anyone to stay. It is the one thing I am careful about. If you ask and they go anyway, you have watched them choose. If you do not ask, they were only going.”

The shop was quiet. Outside, a cart rattled down the hill and was gone.

“Come with me,” Sarah said.

She took the lamp from its nail and went through the back of the shop without looking to see whether he followed. A low door stood open there, and behind it a stair so narrow that his shoulders brushed both walls. He went down after her, watching the flame bend and straighten in her hand and her apron hem swing against each tread. The air did not cool as it should have. It warmed. The wall under his shoulder gave back heat through the wool, as a hull does at the end of a long day in the sun.

Halfway down she hung the lamp on a nail and turned around.

He stood one step above her, which brought her face to the level of his throat. When she lifted it, her breath crossed the small distance and settled on his collar, and he felt each exhale as a single warm thread laid against his skin.

“Look,” she said, and nodded at the dark below.

Past her shoulder a shelf ran the length of the far wall. Eight loaves sat on it, dense and grey, and he saw at once that they were no longer cold. Whatever chill they had given off had gone out of the room. They sat there quietly, like skiffs hauled up above the waterline for the winter.

“That is what it costs me to be quiet,” she said. “I will not pay it again. I came to this town to stop.” She drew a breath he felt more than heard. “I promised myself one season. The light leaves the shop a little earlier every week, and I have not decided. I will not tell you that I have when I have not. When I know, I will say it to your face, and you will hear it from me and not from a loaf.”

The lamp guttered.

It did not go out. It sank to a low blue bead on the wick and held there, and the stair filled with a dim, moving dark. Her face was very near. He could see the pulse in her throat and a strand of hair against her cheek and her mouth, not smiling, with the breath going in and out of it. His fists were shut at his sides. His coat pulled tight across his shoulders because he had locked every muscle in his arms to keep them where they were. Her hands were behind her back. He could see the strain in her elbows, and he knew without seeing that she had laced her fingers there, the knuckles white from holding on to one another.

An inch of air lay between his chest and hers. It was no more than the thickness of a finger. It was the whole width of the lake.

The cellar smelled of yeast and warm wood and flour, like the inside of a hull left all day in the sun. Neither of them spoke of it. He breathed it in and felt it go through him slowly, and felt her do the same.

He could not ask her to stay. He could not ask her anything. He stood on a stair and spent every ounce of himself on doing nothing, and the doing of nothing was the hardest work he had ever done on land or on water.

“Ask me a question,” Sarah whispered. “Any one.”

“Not yet.”

“Then go home and find one.”

He climbed the first three steps backward, because he could not make himself turn his back, and then he turned. She did not follow. He crossed the dark shop, past the stone bun on the counter, and out into the lane, and did not stop until he reached the end of the pier.

The boards still held the day’s heat. Behind him the wheelhouse lamp, which he had forgotten to put out, made a small yellow room on the water, and in it the two loaves sat side by side on the shelf, the grey and the plain, waiting for him to decide which of them he was.

He took his place at the end of the pier with the black water at his boots and his hands still closed. Behind him the hill rose in its rows of lamps. He did not turn. He had no right to.

Then the dark gap in Lantern Street flared, the way a match flares in a closed room, and spilled gold across the cobbles. The reflection came down the water to his feet and lay there without breaking. Behind him, for the first time all day, the Heron shifted against her lines, easy and a little ashamed, like a creature let out of the cold.
