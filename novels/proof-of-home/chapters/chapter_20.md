Jack ate the loaf at midnight, standing on the deck with the Heron’s lamp turned low.

He had meant not to. He had carried it into the wheelhouse and set it beside the grey one from the day before and looked at the pair of them the way a man looks at two stones in a wall, trying to judge which was holding the other up. But the plain one still kept a little warmth from her arm, and he had never been able to leave warmth lying about, so in the end he tore off the heel and ate it where the lake could see him.

It tasted of flour and water and salt. That was all. She had told him so on the pier and she had not lied, and he knew what a lie tasted like in her bread. This was not one. It was a loaf with nothing put into it on purpose, and by the third mouthful he understood that the nothing had weighed more than the grey ones. She had carried it down the hill and across the planks in both hands, and everything she had kept out of it was still in the crumb, in the shape of the places where it had been held back. He could name none of it. He wanted all of it. He sat down on the bench by the wheelhouse with crumbs on his knee and the water slapping the hull, and he did not sleep.

I miss you, and you are standing right in front of me.

He had answered her with a speech about the autumn. He had listened to himself do it, in the harbor clerk’s voice, the way a man hears himself fall down a flight of stairs: one long clear interval in which he could see exactly where he would land and could do nothing about it.

The standing loaf did not come down to the gangplank in the morning. The grocer’s boy brought it, out of breath, and said Miss Quill had a great deal of work. Jack said that was understandable. He took the cloth from the boy by its corner, and the loaf inside was warm and honest and had been made by a person who had decided to be civil, and that was the worst thing yet.

The Heron took an hour and a quarter over a crossing that should have taken forty minutes. She dragged at every buoy. She sulked past the island with her nose in the water, and the passengers fanned themselves with their hats and said the lake was heavy today, and Nell stood at the stern with her arms folded and watched the far shore recede as though it were an argument she had already won.

A small girl on the bench asked her mother why the boat was sulking. Her mother told her that boats did not sulk. The girl looked at the wheelhouse, and at the man in it, and said with the flat authority of the very young that this one did. Jack kept his eyes on the buoy. Nobody aboard had ever been rude enough to say it out loud before, and he found that he could not contradict her.

Near the end of the day, with the last load ashore, Nell said only, “The window’s dark.”

“I know.”

“Two nights now.”

“I know, Nell.”

“Then I do not see why you are still standing on this deck,” said Nell, and took the stern line up the pier with her, and left him there.

He had looked for the gold square on the cobbles every night since midsummer. He had told himself it was a custom. Now the street ran up the hill from the harbor with a dark gap in it where the bakery was, like a tooth gone from a smile, and he found that he had walked the whole length of the pier and half of Lantern Street before he knew he had left the boat.

Gideon was on his ladder at the top of the hill, trimming the last lantern. He did not turn his head. “She has not locked the door,” he said, to the wick.

He stood on the step with his hand on the latch for the length of one held breath. A girl went past with a bucket and gave him a look of frank pity. He lifted the latch before she could say anything about it.

The shop was dim. The blinds were half down and the long counter had been scrubbed bare, and Sarah stood behind it with a cloth in her hand, wiping a place that was already clean. She did not look up when the bell sounded. Her hair had come down on one side. There was flour on her wrist, and she had not noticed, and he stood inside the door with his hat in his hands and was struck by how small the room was, and how entirely she filled it.

“We are closed,” she said.

“I know.”

He crossed to the counter. He unbuttoned the left pocket of his coat, and for the first time in a fortnight he took out what was in it and laid it between them on the scrubbed wood. The bun had gone hard as a river stone. It had split along its old seam and the cardamom had faded to a ghost of itself, and the two small dents in the top were still there, side by side, with a narrow gap between them.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she picked it up, turning it in her fingers as if it were a coin from another country, and set her thumb into one of the two dents. It fitted exactly.

“These are mine,” she said. “I did not mean to put them there.”

“I know. I kept them anyway.”

She put the bun down again, closer to him than to her, and took her hand away.

“I did not eat it,” he said. “I could not throw it in the lake either.”

“It will have gone to stone.”

“Yes.”

“Then it is no use to anyone.”

“It was of use to me,” Jack said, and the sentence came out of him so plainly that he heard it land on the wood beside the bun. He had not planned it. He had planned, climbing the hill, a good many sentences, and every one of them had been about the weather. Now there was only the one that was true, and he put his hands flat on the counter so that they would stop moving.

“I cannot make you stay, Sarah, and I am afraid of how much I want to.”

The cloth stopped. She raised her eyes. They were red at the rims. She had not slept either, and she looked at him as if he had said it in a language she had been studying for months and had only just heard spoken aloud.

“You told me on the pier,” she said, “that I would hardly think of the ferry.”

“I was wrong.”

“You were not wrong. You were deciding.” She set the cloth down. “You wrote me a whole winter last night, Jack. You wrote the autumn and the people I would have about me and the way I would forget. You did it kindly. You did it without asking me a single question.”

“What question?”

“Any of them.” Her voice shook and she steadied it. “Stop deciding for me. I will leave if I leave. I will stay if I stay. But I will be the one who says so, and you will not take that from me by being polite.”

It went into him cleanly, like a sounding line into deep water. There was no bottom he could find.

“Why?” she said, more quietly. “Who left, that you are so sure of the ending?”

He had told no one. Nell knew, because Nell had been there, and Gideon, because Gideon knew everything, and neither of them had ever said it to him. He looked at the bun on the counter.

“My mother,” he said. “She left on the Heron, in the rain. I was small enough that the rail came to my eyes, and a brass button hung off her coat by one thread, and I watched it the whole way up the plank. My grandfather stood behind me with his hand on my shoulder. He did not ask her to stay, and he did not hold me back, and I did not say a word. I was too young to know that I could have.” He breathed in. “I have never asked anyone to stay. It is the one thing I am careful about. If you ask, and the person goes anyway, then you have watched them choose. If you do not ask, they were only going.”

The shop was quiet. Outside, a cart rattled down the hill and was gone.

“Come with me,” Sarah said.

She took the lamp from its nail by the door and went through the back of the shop, and he followed her. There was a low door, and behind it a stair, so narrow that his shoulders brushed both walls as he went down. She went ahead with the lamp held out in front of her, and he watched the flame lean and stand, lean and stand, and the hem of her apron swing against the step. The air changed as they descended. It should have been cool. It was warm. The wall under his shoulder was warm too, through the wool, like the side of a stove that had been banked for the night.

Halfway down, she stopped and hung the lamp on a nail in the wall. She turned round.

He was a step above her. It put her face at the height of his throat, and when she lifted it, her breath crossed the little space and touched his collar. He could feel each exhale as a single warm line against the skin.

“Look,” she said, and tipped her head toward the dark below.

He looked past her, down into the cellar. The shelf ran along the far wall, and on it, in a row, sat eight loaves, dense and grey. They were not cold. He could tell that from here. The chill had gone out of the room, and the loaves sat in it quietly, like boats that had been hauled up for the season.

“I made those,” she said. “They are what it costs me to be quiet. I will not do it again, Jack. I came here to stop doing it.” She drew a breath that he felt more than heard. “I promised myself one season. The light is already going out of the shop a little earlier every week, and I have not decided. I will not tell you I have when I have not. When I know, I will say it to your face, and you will hear it from me and not from a loaf.”

The lamp guttered.

It did not go out. It sank to a low blue bead on the wick and held, and the stair filled with a dim and moving dark. Her face was very close. He could see the pulse in her throat and the strand of hair against her cheek and her mouth, not smiling, with the breath going in and out of it, and he stood with his fists closed at his sides and the cloth of his coat pulling tight across his shoulders because he had locked every muscle in his arms to keep them where they were. Her hands were behind her back. He could see the strain in her elbows. She had laced her fingers there, and he knew it as plainly as if he had seen them, the knuckles gone white with holding on to each other.

An inch of air stood between his chest and hers. It was not much. It was the thickness of a finger. It was the entire width of the lake.

The cellar smelled of yeast and warm wood and the faint sweetness of flour, like the inside of a hull that has stood all day in the sun. Neither of them mentioned it. He breathed it in and it went through him slowly, and she breathed it in, and he felt her do it.

He could not ask her to stay. He could not ask her anything. He stood on a stair and used every ounce of himself to do nothing at all, and the doing of nothing was the hardest work he had ever done on land or on water.

“Ask me a question,” Sarah whispered. “Any one.”

“Not yet.”

“Then go home and find one.”

He went up the stair backward for the first three steps, because he could not make himself turn his back, and then he turned. She did not follow. He crossed the dark shop, past the stone bun on the counter, out into the lane, and did not stop until he reached the end of the pier.

The boards were still warm from the day. Behind him the wheelhouse lamp, which he had forgotten to put out, made a small yellow room on the water, and in it the two loaves sat side by side on their shelf, the grey and the plain, waiting for him to decide which of them he was.

He stood there, with the black water at his boots and his hands still closed. Behind him the hill rose in its rows of lamps. He did not turn. He had no right to.

Then the dark gap in Lantern Street flared, the way a match flares in a closed room, and spilled out gold across the cobbles. The reflection lay down on the water at his feet and did not break. Behind him, for the first time that day, the Heron shifted against her lines like a creature let out of a cold room, easy and a little ashamed.
