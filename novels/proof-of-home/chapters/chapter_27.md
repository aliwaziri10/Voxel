The lifeboat stayed warm.

Sarah had left it on the loft table on the night of the knock, split open where it had fallen, and by morning she had expected it to be the stone it had always intended to be. It was not. She laid her palm on the char and the heat rose into it as steadily as from a banked oven. It was warm the next day and the night after that, and she had stopped telling herself it was an accident of the stove.

The pie at the Lamplight had been eaten, she supposed, though she could not have said what was in it. She and Jack had sat at Odalys’s long table with a full width of lamplight between their shoulders and passed the cream like strangers, while Odalys watched them over the rim of her cup with the face of a woman who has promised to behave. Nell had eaten three slices and said nothing, at tremendous cost. Afterward Jack had walked her to her door and said goodnight to the step. Sarah had climbed the stairs and sat on the edge of her bed in her boots until the street lantern went out.

Since then the weather had turned in earnest. There was frost on the pier planks at dawn, and the grocer had taken in his awnings, and on the clerk’s board the square chalk had not changed, and every morning it was nearer. For three mornings she had given her bread her most honest hands and watched it come out of the oven sitting up, loaf after loaf, like a row of children listening for a step in the street. On the third morning she understood what she was waiting for. She was waiting for a man to find a fourth way of not asking, and he would go on finding them until the lake froze. He had put twenty small questions to her and one that was not small, and a knock at the door had answered it. He would not ask it twice of his own accord. A man who believed that wanting a woman to stay was the same as trapping her did not hold out a trap twice.

She had a card in her apron pocket that said I let him call it kind. She did not intend to let anyone call anything kind.

At dusk she stood at the head of the pier in Fenna’s waxed coat with the horn buttons done up to her chin. The last crossing came in empty, as it always did at that hour, with the lamps swinging along the rail and the lake gone the dull gray of an unwashed baking tin. Nell took the stern line, looked once at Sarah’s face, and discovered that she was needed urgently by a rope in another part of the harbor. She went up the pier at a clip that deceived nobody.

Jack came down the plank last, his cap crushed in one fist. He stopped a pace from her, as he always did now, as though the pace were a rule.

“You are out late,” he said.

“So are you.” She had rehearsed a speech, and it had gone, as speeches did near him. “Take me across.”

“The run is done. The far landing is shut.”

“I do not want the far landing. I want the lake, and nobody else on it.” Her voice shook and she let it. “One passenger. No fare.”

He looked at her for a long moment. She watched him not ask why. She watched him fold the question small and put it away, and her chest turned over like dough folded on itself, so suddenly that she had to look at his collar. Then he put the cap on his head, as a man does when he wants a decision made official, and went aboard and cast off the stern line himself.

He took them out past the end of the harbor wall. Behind them the town dwindled, and Gideon’s taper went along the quay from post to post, and one by one the lanterns caught, small and gold and very far. Ahead lay the open lake and a band of copper along the rim of the sky. The engine settled to its low note. Then the note dropped, not to a stop but to a long slow walk, and Jack lashed the wheel with a turn of line and left the Heron to her own judgment.

Sarah was standing at the bow rail. She did not remember crossing the deck. The iron was cold through her gloves. He came and stood beside her, a hand’s width off, and the width was exactly as large as it had been in every room they had ever shared. She had looked at that width all season.

There was the rule, of course. Every one of Fenna’s cards said it in one margin or another: a kiss given in love sets a loaf’s feeling for good. If she kissed him, the bread she baked from this night on would say so, in every crust, to every door on Lantern Street, and she would never again make a loaf that kept her counsel. She weighed it at the rail with the iron cold under her gloves. A lifetime of loaves that told the truth, against a lifetime of loaves that lied by keeping quiet. It was not a hard choice. It was only a frightening one, and she had been frightened before and lived.

“I am going to say a thing,” she said. “I would like you not to answer it with the weather.”

A muscle moved at the corner of his mouth. “I will try.”

“I want you to kiss me.”

It went out over the water, and the lake took it, and nothing in the world argued.

“I have wanted it since the morning you took my first loaf with your eyes shut.” The words had been standing in line for months, and now they came out of her in a rush, in no order at all. “I used to say I did not mind. I mind. I mind very much when you stand a hand away. I am not asking you to stay. I am not asking you anything. I am telling you what I want, because I was very good, once, at standing with my hands folded, and I have read what that costs. Fenna kissed a loaf in the dark and wished for the rest of her life that she had said no, and I am not going to wish that. And if the bread tells the whole of Larkmere in the morning, then let it tell.”

He did not answer with the weather. He took off the cap he had put on to make a decision official and set it on the rail, very carefully, as if it might wake. Then he turned to face her, and she saw that he was not steady, and that he had decided not to hide it.

“May I,” he said.

“You have asked me that before.”

“I was interrupted.”

A laugh broke out of her, short and wet, and she pressed her lips together to stop it. “Yes,” said Sarah. “Yes, Jack.”

His left hand came up slowly, the way a baker reaches to test a loaf for doneness, wanting to know and afraid of the dent. The backs of his fingers touched her jaw. They were cold from the rail, and they shook. He laid his palm along her cheek, and his thumb found the place where flour had once been, and rested there. For the length of one held breath he looked at her as if she were the lit window at the end of a very long lane.

Then he crossed the last inch.

His mouth was warm and dry and not quite steady, and for the first moment it did nothing but rest against hers, the way a hand rests on a door it has waited all season to open. She tasted lake air and salt, and nothing that was bread. Then he breathed out, a long unsteady breath into her mouth, the held breath of an entire summer let go at last, and the whole of her came open like a loaf broken in half, all steam and softness, and she was kissing him back.

Her gloved hands found the front of his coat and shut in the wool. His arm came around her back and drew her in, and the length of her met the length of him, the cold buttons, the heat beneath them, the hard quick knock of his heart against her ribs. She heard a small sound and understood that it was her own. He kissed her as if he had been taught nothing but patience and was learning everything else in a single minute: slowly, and then not slowly, his mouth moving on hers and opening, and she opened to it. The cold went out of the rail and the deck and the whole evening. She rose onto her toes. His hand slid up into the loosened hair at the back of her head and held her there, and she felt the tremor in his wrist, the strength he was spending to keep it gentle.

Under her boots the deck changed its mind.

All season the Heron had gone like a poorly kneaded dough, heavy and sullen and listing to one side. Now the planks hummed. The bow came round of its own accord and settled on the line of the shore, and the boat ran straight as a strip of pastry under the pin, quick and level, without a shudder. Nobody was at the wheel. The wheel did not seem to mind.

Along the harbor the lanterns caught, one and then another and then a long run of them, and Sarah opened her eyes for the space of a breath and saw them over his shoulder. Each flame as it took showed her a little more of the town, until the whole of Larkmere seemed to have come up out of the dark to look. She shut her eyes again. She had never in her life been looked at so kindly by so many windows.

When he drew back it was by degrees, a breath at a time, as though neither mouth had agreed to it. His forehead came down against hers. His arms did not loosen. She stood inside his open coat with her fists still shut in the wool, and she could feel his heart going under her knuckles as hard as hers.

“Hello,” said Sarah, idiotically.

He laughed. It shook through the whole length of him and into her. “Hello.”

“Your boat is running.”

“She has not run like this in all the years I have had her.” His voice was rough. His thumb had not left her cheek. “I think she approves.”

It should have been enough. She had thought, in her loft with the cold loaf, that a kiss would be the end of a question, a door pulled shut on a draft. It was not a door. It was the first slice cut from a loaf still hot, and the rest of it lay waiting on the board, steaming, and she was hungrier than she had ever been in her life.

They did not speak of the board. They did not speak of the season, or of the card in her pocket, or of any question that began with the word stay. He kept her inside his coat all the way in, and the Heron brought them to the pier on her own good line, and at the end of the boards Gideon Teague was on his ladder with his taper, studying the sky with great attention, as a man will who has seen everything and means to be paid in discretion.

At her door Jack kissed her once more, short and hard, the way a man seizes a crust before the plate is taken away. Then he stepped back and locked his hands at his sides, and she saw the tendons stand in his wrists. “Goodnight, Sarah,” he said, to her face this time.

“Goodnight.”

She watched him down the lane. At the corner he turned and raised an arm, and she raised hers, and neither of them could look away until the dark did it for them.

In the morning her bread knew.

She had not slept. She had lain awake with her mouth still warm, and gone down in the black hour before light, and fed the starter and weighed the flour and put her hands into the dough, and it took them as a lover takes a hand. It rose too fast. It came up over the lip of the bowl and she laid a cloth across it and the cloth lifted. When the first loaves came out the crusts were blistered gold, and they sang, a small crackling hymn as they cooled, and the smell that went down Lantern Street in the cold air was joy, plain and loud as a bell.

She broke a heel and tasted it. The joy was there, enormous, and under the joy lay the want: a deep slow ache for his mouth and his hands and the whole unspent winter, a hunger the kiss had not fed but only woken. She put the heel down. She had told no one. She would not need to.

The grocer’s wife came at seven and ate a roll at the counter, and stopped, and turned toward the water with her hand at her throat, and laughed aloud. The first fisherman through the door grinned at his boots. Even the old man with the canvas satchel, who had never in her hearing said a word he did not need, paid for his loaf and told it, “Good.”

On the loft table the lifeboat sat as warm as skin. In its crock by the stove the starter had climbed out over the lip in the night and run down the stoneware in a slow pale tongue, hungry and shameless, and Sarah looked at it and could not find it in her to scrape it back.
