The summer had settled into Larkmere like a warm loaf cooling on a stone table. Sunlight lay thick and golden over the water, turning the surface into something that shimmered and rippled with the patience of dough waiting to rise. Sarah stood at the edge of the dock where the Heron cut through the morning mist, her hands wrapped around the handle of her rolling pin, flour dusting her knuckles. The air smelled of wet pine and baked rye from the bakery behind her, and somewhere down the street a child was laughing, the sound bright and far away.

She could hear Jack piloting the boat, the soft creak of timber and the rhythm of oars. He had been here before, always here, the way the tide came in and went out. Tonight the water was clear enough to see the bottom, and the sky above was a pale blue that made the whole world feel possible.

When he stepped onto the dock, she felt the familiar shift in her chest, the one that meant something was changing. He was taller than she remembered, though time had done that too. His coat was damp with salt, and his face held the quiet expression of a man who had learned to keep his thoughts close. He did not speak at first. He simply looked at her, and she saw the calculation behind his eyes, the way he measured the space between them.

"You're early," he said. His voice was low and unhurried.

"I'm exactly on time," she replied. The words came out steadier than she expected. "The bread needs to finish rising."

He nodded, a slight movement of his head, and turned toward the boat. But before he reached the edge of the dock, his hand brushed against hers. Just a momentary contact, the kind of touch that happens when two people are both reaching for something they cannot quite name. Her fingers stopped an inch from his sleeve, and she felt the warmth of his skin through the thin fabric of his shirt. He did not pull away. Neither did she.

"Where were you going?" he asked.

"The bakery," she said. "It's almost ready."

His gaze flickered to the shop visible behind her, the window glowing with the amber light of afternoon. For a moment she thought he might ask about the bread, about the loaves she had been shaping all day. Instead he said, "I was just passing through. The Heron takes the shortcut today."

"That's right," she said. "And you?"

"Home," he answered. "Always home."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. She felt the echo of them settle in her belly, a heaviness that had nothing to do with the bread she had been kneading. She wanted to say something—something true—but the words tangled themselves in her throat, the way dough tangles when you try to fold it wrong.

"I miss you," she said. The admission surprised her. It was simple, direct, and entirely unguarded. She had practiced hiding it, had built walls of routine and routine of routine until the truth lived beneath the crust of her daily life. But standing here, with the sun on her face and the smell of yeast in the air, the truth rose unbidden.

Jack's expression did not change. He did not look away, did not offer comfort or deflection. He simply waited, his hands loose at his sides, his posture open in a way that seemed to invite her to continue.

"I know," he said. "I've missed you too."

The honesty of it stung more than any criticism could have. She had hoped he would understand, that he would see the same hunger she felt. But understanding was not the same as acceptance, and she knew that already. He was not the type to rush into affection. He believed in letting things unfold, in giving people room to choose.

"I should go," she said. "The bread won't wait forever."

"Stay a moment," he said. "Just a moment."

She looked at him then, really looked, and saw the exhaustion beneath the calm. There were lines around his eyes that spoke of long hours on the water, of mornings spent alone in a cabin, of nights when the silence was louder than any storm. He was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. And yet he had come to her door, to her dock, to stand beside her without asking permission.

Her heart beat faster. The ache she had been carrying for weeks pressed against her ribs, and now it found purchase in this moment, sharp and undeniable. She wanted to tell him that she understood. That she had felt the same pull, the same desperate hope that he was the answer to a question she had been asking her whole life. But the words would not come, and she knew that saying them would mean admitting something she was not ready to admit.

"One more loaf," she said. "Before I go."

He nodded again, and she turned toward the bakery. But before she reached the door, his hand moved—not pulling her away, but reaching out. His fingers closed around hers, and for a heartbeat the contact was complete. She felt the warmth of his palm, the slight roughness of calluses, the pulse beating just below his thumb. Then he withdrew his hand, and she was alone again, standing on the dock with the sun still warm on her face and the taste of something unsaid hanging in the air.

The dock was crowded with fishermen, their boats bobbing gently in the water, lines coiled and ready. The morning light caught the silver threads of the nets, and somewhere a gull cried overhead. Sarah watched the scene with the detached attention of someone observing a process she was part of but not fully understanding. The bread in her mind was rising, patient and steady, and she wondered if Jack was doing the same thing inside himself, waiting for something to emerge.

She walked back toward the bakery, her steps deliberate, each footfall a small decision. The streets of Larkmere were narrow and lined with shops, and the scent of fresh bread drifted from her shop like a promise. Inside, the oven hummed with heat, and the air was thick with the sweetness of proofing dough. She could feel the weight of the loaves she had shaped earlier, the ones that carried her feelings like invisible ingredients. Some were calm and steady, the bread of confidence and clarity. Others were heavier, darker—loaves that tasted of longing and the ache of wanting someone who was not there.

She pulled the bag from under the counter and began to sort the loaves. The one she had been most proud of sat in the center of the shelf, a round loaf of dark rye that had absorbed the memory of her hands. As she lifted it, she noticed something strange. The bread was ordinary, solid and brown, but when she brought it to her nose and inhaled, she caught a faint trace of something else—a hint of salt and something floral, like the first sip of tea after a long night.

She did not know what it meant. She had not been able to explain it to anyone, not even Fenna's recipes, which were written in a language of small marks that she was only beginning to learn. But she knew, with a certainty that bypassed thought, that the loaf was carrying something. Something she had been trying to bake into it without knowing how.

That evening, when she returned to the dock, Jack was there. The Heron was moored at the end of the pier, its hull gleaming in the fading light. He stood beside it, his hands resting on the gunwale, and he looked at her with an expression she could not quite read.

"You stayed," he said.

"It's not much of a reason."

"No," he agreed. "But it's something."

She sat on the edge of the dock, her back against the wood, and watched the water. The sun was sinking lower, painting the surface in shades of copper and gold. In the last chapter, she had felt cold after warmth—the relief of leaving, the sudden chill of the evening settling over her like a blanket. Now the cold was different. It was deeper, more persistent. It was the cold that comes after a wound has opened and will not close itself.

She thought of the loaf in her bag, the one that tasted of nothing she could name. It sat heavy in her arms, and she realized with a sudden clarity that it was not empty. It was full of something she had been afraid to acknowledge. The longing. The ache that had been growing since the kiss in chapter twenty-seven, since the moment she had finally let herself believe that maybe, possibly, she could want someone without losing herself to it.

The bread magic was subtle, the kind of magic that lived in the spaces between sensations. She had always known that her work was not merely making food. Every loaf she shaped carried the emotion of her hands, the intention of her heart. When she was angry, the bread turned bitter. When she was joyful, it rose golden and fragrant. When she was sad, it became dense and heavy, as if the sorrow had taken root in the dough and refused to be removed.

And now, sitting on the dock with Jack's hand on the gunwale beside her, she understood that the bread was not just reflecting her feelings—it was feeding them. The loaf in her bag was carrying the shape of what she had been trying to become: someone who could love without disappearing into the love. Someone who could stay without surrendering.

She reached into her bag and took out the dark rye loaf. It was warm from the oven, and when she held it, she could feel the faint pulse of something inside it, a rhythm that matched the beat of her own heart. She had not eaten it yet. She had not needed to. The bread was not for consumption in the ordinary sense. It was a vessel, a container for the feeling she had been carrying too long.

Jack turned to look at her. His eyes were serious, his expression unreadable. He did not move closer, nor did he step back. He simply stood there, present in a way that made her aware of his presence, of the space between them that was neither too large nor too small.

"Do you want to talk about it?" he asked.

The question hung in the air. She could answer yes, could accept the invitation to share the weight she had been carrying. Or she could refuse, could retreat into the safety of the bakery, of the routine of rising dough and cooling loaves, of the small certainties of her craft.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "Not yet."

He nodded slowly. "Then we'll find another time."

She stood, tucking the loaf back into her bag. The walk to the lamplight inn was long, and the evening grew cooler with each step. The streets of Larkmere were quieter now, the sounds of the day fading into the background. She passed the Lamplight Inn, its windows dark, and felt a flicker of something—recognition, perhaps, or the memory of a connection that had existed before.

Inside the inn, Odalys was arranging candles on the bar. She heard the bell above the door, saw Sarah enter, and recognized the look on her face. It was the look of someone who had been given a gift and was uncertain how to receive it.

"Come in," Odalys said. "Tea is ready."

Sarah followed her inside, and the warmth of the inn enveloped her. The fire crackled softly, casting shadows that danced across the wooden beams. At a corner table, Jack was already seated, a cup of tea in front of him. He looked up as she approached, and for a moment their eyes met.

Something passed between them. Not a confession, not a declaration. Just the acknowledgment of something that had been building for months, a current running beneath the surface of their interactions. Sarah felt the pull of it, the desire to bridge the gap that had grown between them. But she also felt the weight of what it would cost, the risk of opening herself further.

She sat across from him, and the silence stretched between them. Outside, the last light of day faded into deep blue, and the harbor lanterns began to glow along the shore. The tradition was old: whoever stood at the same lantern when it caught was said to be thinking of the same person. Jack was thinking of her. She was thinking of him.

The ache in her chest had not gone away. It had only shifted, finding new places to lodge. Now it sat alongside the memory of the loaf, the bread that carried her longing in every crumb. She could feel it rising, the way dough rises when it is left alone in a warm place. The feeling was spreading, becoming harder to contain.

She picked up her pen and began to write in the notebook Fenna had left her, the one filled with those strange marginal notes. She wrote a few lines, describing the sensation of the bread in her hands, the way it mirrored her emotions. The ink was dark on the paper, and she watched the letters form, each one a small commitment to the truth she was choosing to tell.

Outside, the lanterns flickered to life, their amber glow spilling onto the water. The Heron was already moving, cutting through the darkening lake, its wake trailing behind it like a ribbon of light. The ferry kept its people, as Jack had said. Those who needed to cross would find their way. Those who did not would remain on the shore, safe in their solitude.

Sarah remained at the table, her heart beating in time with the fire. The bread magic was still active within her, working its slow alchemy. She had not yet spoken the words she needed to speak. But she had begun to understand that the first step toward healing was not to stop feeling, but to let the feeling guide her forward. To trust that the bread she carried was not a burden to be hidden, but a bridge to be crossed.

The night deepened around her, and the ache in her chest grew stronger. It was the ache of wanting, of hoping, of standing at the edge of something that might finally release her. She would not know if it would lead to resolution or to further loss. But she knew, with a certainty that came from the very substance of her life, that the next chapter would begin soon, and that the story was only just beginning to reveal its shape.

She finished her tea, set the cup aside, and looked at Jack. He was watching her with an expression she could not quite name. Perhaps it was recognition. Perhaps it was the beginning of something that had been waiting for this exact moment.

"Thank you for coming," she said quietly.

He raised his cup slightly, a gesture of acknowledgment. "For what?"

"For being here. For seeing me."

The words were simple, but they carried the weight of everything she had been holding back. She did not expect him to respond with understanding. She did not expect him to offer comfort or to promise that the ache would ever truly fade. What she expected was this: a willingness to sit with her in the uncertainty, to bear witness to the pain she was trying to heal.

Jack nodded, and the nod was all she needed. The lanterns burned brighter, and the water reflected their light in patterns that seemed to shift and move on their own. Somewhere in the distance, a bird called, and the sound was lonely and beautiful.

Sarah rose from the table, her movements slow and deliberate. She gathered her bag, checked that the dark rye loaf was secure inside, and walked toward the door. The night air was cool against her face, and she breathed it in deeply, tasting the salt of the lake and the faint sweetness of the evening flowers.

As she stepped outside, she paused at the edge of the dock. The Heron was silent for a moment, its engine idling, before it began to move again. The water lapped against the hull, and the reflection of the lanterns danced across the surface. She thought of the bread in her bag, the one that carried her longing, and she felt a small, quiet resolve settle in her chest.

She would not stay tonight. She would not stay tomorrow. But she would not leave without having faced what she had been avoiding. The first real hurt had arrived, and it was not a wound to be bandaged quickly. It was a wound that would teach her something essential, something she needed to know before she could ever truly belong to someone.

The bread magic continued its slow work, transforming her fear into something that could be shared, transformed her isolation into connection, transformed the ache into a path forward. And as she walked away from the dock, toward the lamplight inn and the warmth of others, she carried the knowledge that the longest journey was not the one from one place to another, but the one inward, through the layers of feeling that had been buried beneath her hands all these seasons.

The night was deep and full of possibility. The ache was still there, but it was no longer a wall. It was a doorway, and she was ready to step through it.

She turned once more, looking back at the dock where Jack stood silhouetted against the fading light. He did not wave, did not call her name. He simply stood there, present, waiting. And she understood, with a clarity that surpassed words, that some things could not be rushed. Some connections required time, and some wounds required patience.

The bread in her bag rose a little higher, as if responding to her resolve. The first chapter of the ending was already forming, and she was ready to meet it.

The water lapped against the hull, and the lanterns burned on, and Sarah Quill walked into the night, carrying her longing like a loaf that was finally ready to be shared.
