Sarah found out how many loaves she had made by going down to the cellar and counting them. Eight. Nine, she corrected herself, because one had gone down the hill the evening before in Odalys’s arms, and she had not yet decided whether to forgive that. The eight sat in a row along the flour shelf, grey and squat as stones turned up by a plough. She stood on the bottom stair with the lamp held out and counted them twice, because the first count did not seem possible.

She remembered making the first three. The rest her hands had made without her. That was the trouble with hands. They kept faith with a feeling long after the rest of her had agreed to drop it.

The cellar was cold. It was high summer, the street above had been baking since dawn, and down here her breath stood faintly in the lamplight. The cold came off the loaves. She felt it at her ankles, thin and steady, the way a draught comes under a door. She lifted the nearest one and it hung from her hands like a wet sack. The crust had not blistered anywhere. She pressed her thumb into the base and the dent stayed, and did not spring back, and she thought: that dough never even tried to rise.

She meant to throw them out. She carried two up as far as the back door and stood in the morning light with the bin lid open and could not do it. Fenna’s journal had rules scattered through its margins like seed, and none of them was about this, but there are things a baker does not do, and tipping out a loaf with her own heart kneaded into it is one of them. She carried them down again and set them back in their row. She did not say sorry to them out loud, but she wanted to.

Then, because she was alone and nobody would know, she broke the end off the nearest loaf and ate it.

It did not taste of anger. She had braced for anger and would have welcomed it. It tasted of a door being closed very quietly in a room at the far end of a house, by a person who did not want to wake the sleepers. The crumb sat cold on her tongue. Under the cold was the plain fact of it, as sharp as a stone in a shoe: an hour on a crowded deck with her shoulder against his sleeve and her mouth shut, waiting for him to turn his head. He had not turned it. He had told her about the wind.

She put the rest of the loaf down and wiped her hands on her apron for a long time.

Odalys came when the first rush had thinned, and she did not come through the front. She came in at the back with her basket empty on her arm and stood in the doorway between the flour sacks, the way she did when she had made up her mind to be unpopular.

“You took one to him,” Sarah said, before she could say good morning.

“I did.”

“Without asking.”

“You would have said no.” Odalys set the basket on the table. “He got one mouthful down and could not manage a second. I watched him try. And I will tell you one thing more. He has your bun in his coat. The orange one, from the lantern evening. Buttoned into the pocket over his ribs, in this heat, for a fortnight.”

Sarah’s hands stopped on the dough.

“A man does not carry a thing like that against his body if he means to ignore it,” Odalys said. “He carries it the way you carry a letter you are afraid to answer.”

“Then why did he talk about the weather?”

“Because he is frightened, and a frightened man makes poor conversation.” Odalys pulled out the stool and sat down, which she never did before noon. “A loaf is a letter, my girl. You cannot bake a different letter from the one you wrote. I watched you try all yesterday, and it is no use. The only thing that will clear that cellar is saying the words out loud, with your mouth, to the person they are addressed to.”

“Fenna never did.” It came out sharper than Sarah meant. “Fifty years of bread and a hook in the window, and she never said it to anyone.”

Odalys looked at her for a long moment. “No,” she said. “She didn’t. Look where it got her.”

She picked up her empty basket and went out the way she had come. Sarah stood with her palms flat on the dough and felt it, cool and slack, the way a dough goes when the yeast is tired. She had not put a thing into it yet and it was already giving up.

She worked through the morning because there was no other way to carry it. Rolls for the grocer, long loaves for the inn, a tray of plain biscuits for the mill. She kept to her old rule and kept her mind on flour and weight, and the rule had gone brittle. Every time she folded the dough she was back on the deck. Every time she scored a crust she heard the three words he had given her. It is hot.

The grocer’s wife came in at mid-morning for the mill’s biscuits, and Sarah, to be useful to herself, put one on a saucer and pushed it across the counter the way she always did. “Go on. Tell me if the salt is right.” The woman bit it, chewed, and stopped. She stood quite still with her hand on the counter and her eyes gone to the window. Then she set the rest of the biscuit down on the saucer as carefully as if it might wake.

“It is a very good biscuit,” she said. “It is only that I have just remembered the winter my husband went to the coast for his mother’s funeral and I had the whole house to myself. I had not thought of that winter in years.” She laughed, a short embarrassed breath, and pressed her fingers to her collar. “Who is it that you are missing, dear?”

“Nobody,” Sarah said. “It’s the heat.”

“Of course it is,” said the grocer’s wife, and bought two dozen, and took them out into the street at arm’s length, like a woman carrying a pan that had boiled over. Through the window Sarah watched her stop at the corner and look back at the shop, and then at the harbor, and then at nothing.

Larkmere could taste it. That was the fact she had been carrying around the cellar all morning without setting it down. It was not only a ferryman who had to swallow what she baked. Every loaf she sold went out of the door with her whole heart in the crumb, and a town was going to eat it.

She was angry, she found, and it was not at him. It was at the part of herself that had stood there and said nothing. She knew that part well. She had spent years in another city learning to make herself smaller so that she would fit beside another person’s silence, to take the corner of the bed and the corner of the table and the corner of every conversation, and she had come to Larkmere with a borrowed season and a promise that she would stop. She had not made the promise to anyone. She had made it to the dough, in the first week, with her arms in a bowl of flour up to the elbow. She would not fold herself into a quarter inch of wool and call it company.

The light in the shop was already moving. In spring it had lain across the counter until nearly supper. Now it slid off the flour bins a hand’s width sooner every week, and she could read in it exactly how much of her season was spent.

At noon she did the only thing she could think of. She made one loaf and asked nothing of it. Flour, water, salt, a ladle of starter from the crock. She shaped it with her eyes on the window instead of on her hands, as though looking away might keep the feeling from going in. It rose. It split along the crown in the oven with a small dry crack, like a knuckle, and when she turned it onto the rack it was pale gold and ordinary and quiet. She held her palm over it. Warm. Only warm. It was not a good loaf. It was a blank one, and she understood why. A baker could not hide a feeling in dough. She could only take it out of the dough and carry it out of the shop in her own two hands, to the person it belonged to.

She wrapped the loaf in a clean cloth and tucked it under her arm.

She went down Lantern Street at the hour the lamps went up. Gideon Teague stood on his ladder at the top of the hill with his taper in his fist, and he looked at her and at the loaf under her arm and said nothing at all. He lit the lantern beside him a little slower than he needed to, so that the light seemed to follow her down the hill.

The Heron lay at her berth with the last passengers gone ashore. The air over the water was thick and gold and smelled of tar and warm timber. Nell stood in the stern with a coil of line, and she took one look at the woman coming down the pier and said to nobody, “I must see to the bilge,” and went below with the speed of a person who had never in her life seen to a bilge.

Jack was at the bow. He had his coat on despite the heat, buttoned to the throat, and on the left side, over his ribs, there was a small round shape in the cloth. She saw it from twenty paces. She kept walking.

He straightened when he heard her boots. He did not come to the rail. He stood with the bow line slack in his hands, and when she stopped at the edge of the dock he let it fall and came the length of the deck, slowly, as a man comes toward a gate he has locked himself.

“Sarah.”

“I brought you bread.” She held out the loaf in its cloth. He leaned across the gap and took it in both hands, as he had taken the very first one, with that same care, and the care of it closed her throat. “It’s only flour. I couldn’t make it say anything. I thought you might be glad of that.”

“Thank you.”

She had made a speech. She had made it walking down the hill, and it had been a good one, with a beginning and a reasonable middle. It was gone. What she had left was one sentence, and she found that she was going to say it.

“I miss you, and I did not expect to, and you are standing right in front of me.”

The water knocked softly at the pilings. A gull walked along the rail and thought better of it. Jack did not move. For a single breath she saw it cross his face, the same flash she had seen at the lantern, hunger and a plain fear going by too quickly to be named, and then she watched him put it away. She watched the drawer shut.

“It is a long season,” he said. “The crossings take everything I have. I am not much to miss, Sarah. You will find that it passes. When the autumn comes and you have your own people about you, you will hardly think of the ferry.”

She heard all of it, down to the last word. He had written her an entire winter in which she was already gone, and he had said it kindly, which was worse than anything he could have said unkindly.

“I see,” she said.

Her hand had come up. She noticed it as if it belonged to a woman standing next to her. It had risen from her side to the height of his sleeve with the fingers open, a hand’s breadth from the buttoned pocket, and he saw it too. His eyes dropped to it. He did not move. She waited the length of one breath for the sleeve to come and meet her, for the smallest shift of his weight, and it did not come.

She lowered the hand. She did it slowly, so that the fingers would not shake, and tucked it into the fold of her apron, and the heat went out of her palm like a loaf carried into a cold room.

“Goodnight, Jack.”

She turned and walked up the pier. She did not look back. The planks rang under her boots one at a time, and she listened behind her for his step, for the scrape of the gangplank, for the small noise the Heron made against her lines when a boot shifted aboard, and she heard none of it. He had not moved. He was standing exactly where she had left him with a plain loaf in his hands, and she knew it as surely as if the back of her head had eyes.

At the shop door the lantern hung on its hook, unlit, where she had left it that afternoon. She stood in front of it in the dim with a match in her fingers. Then she put the match back in its box and closed the lid. She went up the stairs in the dark, and behind her, for the first night since midsummer, the window held only glass, and the cold, and the long street with its lamps going up one by one, each of them lit by other hands.
