The dough had risen through the night without complaint. It lifted the linen cloth into a gentle, even dome, and when Sarah lifted the corner in the shop's low gray light, the loaf kept its shape. It did not slump. It did not ask anything of her. She had shaped it the evening before, her thoughts on nothing but flour and water and the dough's weight in her palms, and calm hands made calm bread. This loaf would taste of ordinary mornings, of nothing urgent, of a kitchen that smelled only of yeast and the oven's slow heat.

She set it on the cooling rack and turned to the counter. The shop stood empty. The lamp above the work table burned low, casting a long shadow from the flour bin toward the door, and the air held the cool stillness of late spring before the day had decided to warm itself. She had come in before the street was awake to check the proof and start the oven for the first batch, and now she stood with her palms flat on the countertop, feeling the wood still cold beneath her hands.

She pressed a fingertip to the side of the loaf. The dough sprang back slowly, holding its shape. Fenna’s margin marks called this an easy loaf, bread that needed a steady hand and time to rise. Sarah was still learning to read those marks. This morning, at least, the lesson seemed plain: the loaf had risen tall while she slept.

She dusted her hands on her apron and walked to the oven. The brick was warm but not yet hot. She would wait. She would let the shop wake around her. She took a knife from the drawer and scraped a small line of flour from the counter edge, watching it fall into the bin below. The motion was familiar. The motion was quiet. The shop smelled of yesterday's rye and of the loaf that had risen through the night, and for the first time since she had come back to Larkmere the morning felt easy, as though the decision about whether to stay or leave could wait until the bread was baked.

She had promised herself one season, and half the season was gone. She had not made the decision. The bread had made it easy to put off deciding.

The bell above the door rang.

She did not have to look to know who it was. Jack Mercer carried the silence of the ferry with him even on land, a quiet that settled in the space behind his shoulders and made the rest of the room seem louder by comparison. He came in and stopped just inside the threshold, his boots drying on the mat, and held out a flour sack.

"The mill sent the wrong thing," he said. "Or nothing at all. I can't tell which."

Sarah took the sack. It was light. She turned it over in her hands and found nothing inside but a scrap of paper with no writing on it.

"The delivery turns out to be nothing," she said.

"I know," Jack said.

They both knew. He had come across the water for an empty sack, or for no reason at all, and they were standing in the empty shop with the lamp low and the counter between them. Sarah set the sack down. Her hand lingered near the edge of the counter. Jack's hand rested on the opposite side, his fingers curled around the wood, and neither of them moved to close the gap.

"You didn't have to bring that across," she said.

"I wanted to cross," he said.

"In this light?"

"Especially in this light."

She almost smiled. The easy feeling of the morning was still in her chest, the calm bread still cooling behind her, and the conversation came without effort, the way good dough comes together without forcing it. They talked about the mill, about the river being high, about whether the inn would need extra bread for the coming market day. The lamp burned lower as they spoke. The shadow of the flour bin stretched across the wood toward the door.

She reached for the bread knife. "You should have a feeling. You came across for nothing."

"I'm not hungry," Jack said.

"You crossed the water. That makes you hungry whether you know it or not."

She cut a slice from yesterday's loaf, the rye that had carried nothing but the ordinary steadiness of a day spent measuring flour. She put it on a small plate and pushed it toward him. He did not take it. He looked at the bread. He looked at her. She took a bite herself. The crumb was soft. The crust held the heat of the oven. It tasted of nothing but bread, which was its own sort of miracle after weeks of loaves that tasted of longing and hesitation and the ache of leaving.

Jack's coat was damp at the shoulders from the lake spray. She noticed it now, the darkening of the wool, the way he had not shaken it off when he came in. He kept it on as though he were ready to leave at any moment, as though the ferry was still waiting for him even though he stood in her shop with an empty sack in his hands.

Odalys had left an order for the inn, and Sarah wrote it on a slip of paper. She pushed it across the counter to Jack. He took it without letting their hands meet, then studied the list as if the quantities required his full attention. They leaned on opposite sides of the counter, her hip against the wood, his shoulder near the wall. The gap stayed open. Neither tried to close it.

Jack turned the slip of paper over in his hands. "She wants sourdough," he said.

"She always wants sourdough," Sarah said.

"Even when the water is high."

"Especially when the water is high."

"Fenna left the margin marks for a reason," Sarah said. "She said every loaf had a story, and the story was written in the edges where nobody thought to look."

Jack turned the slip over again. "What does your story say today?"

"Today it says nothing," Sarah said. "Today it says easy."

"That's a story too," Jack said.

He looked at the paper. He looked at her. The counter between them held the sack, the slip, the space where their hands had almost touched. Sarah leaned her hip against the wood on her side. Jack leaned his shoulder against the wall on his. They did not touch. They only held their hands near each other on the counter edge, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him radiating across the empty space. Nobody came in. Nobody called from the street. Nothing stopped them, and they held back anyway.

The lamp needed oil. Sarah went to the cupboard and brought out the small brass can. She poured a measure into the reservoir and trimmed the wick with her fingers. The flame grew steady again. Jack watched her hands. She watched his. They stayed on opposite sides of the counter, leaning in toward each other without closing the distance. The loaf on the rack cooled to room temperature. The crust cracked once, a small sound in the quiet shop.

She pressed her palm to the loaf one more time. It was still holding its shape. It had not fallen. The easy morning was still there, still intact, but a feeling had shifted in the air between them, a feeling that had nothing to do with the bread and everything to do with the man leaning against the wall on the other side of the counter.

She thought of the season. She thought of her promise. One season, and then she would decide. The season was not over. The bread was still rising.

"I should go," Jack said.

He did not move. He tried to retreat and could not. His hand stayed on the counter. His eyes stayed on her face. He shifted his weight from one foot to the other, a small movement, the way a boat shifts at its mooring when the current changes but the line holds fast. He turned toward the door. His fingers touched the latch. He stopped. He turned back.

"Stay," Sarah said, and she did not mean it as an invitation. She meant it as an observation. The shop was warm. The bread was cooling. The hour had gone soft around the edges.

Jack looked at the door. He looked at the loaf on the rack. He looked at her.

"The ferry keeps its people," he said quietly.

"And this shop keeps its baker."

He was quiet for a moment. The lamp flickered. The shadow of the flour bin stretched longer across the wood.

"I am not your people," he said.

"Not yet," Sarah said.

She said it before she could stop herself. The words sat between them, heavier than the sack, heavier than the silence. The easy morning had turned into a feeling else, a feeling that did not rise as predictably as bread, a feeling that did not wait for her to be ready. A tightness formed in her chest, not painful but insistent, the way dough resists when it has been shaped too tightly and needs to rest before it can rise again.

Jack pushed himself off the wall. He did not take a step toward the door. He stayed where he was, leaning against the counter, his hands still near hers, the gap between them unchanged and yet suddenly full.

"I should go," he said again.

"Go then," Sarah said.

He did not go. He stood there with the light low on his face and the empty sack on the counter between them, and Sarah felt the smile that had been on her lips since he walked in, since the first moment she had seen him in the doorway with his quiet and his wrong delivery, since he had touched her hand and not let go right away. She had been smiling the whole time.

Jack nodded. He turned toward the door. His hand brushed the counter edge as he passed, just a near thing, almost contact, and then he opened the door and stepped out into the street. The bell rang. The door closed.

For the length of a breath, Sarah thought he might turn back. She kept her hands on the counter and did not call after him. The bell above the door was still trembling faintly when she looked at the place where he had rested his shoulder. The loaf had risen tall in the morning light, its crown smooth and full. She was still smiling when she lifted the cloth from the rack.



The parcel was no more than a loaf wrapped in brown paper, with no order slip and no note tucked beneath the string. Sarah turned it over in her hands, looking for a mark from the mill or the inn. There was nothing. She could have asked Jack to explain it, but he had already admitted he had no reason to bring it. He had come because Nell had sent him, and because he had chosen to obey.

She set the parcel beside the bread knife. The paper made a dry sound against the counter. Jack watched her, waiting for a question. She could see how ready he was to answer a practical one, and how little prepared he was for a question that mattered. She decided not to make him defend the errand.

“Did Nell tell you the flour order was wrong?” she asked.

“She told me there was a delivery.”

“That’s not quite an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

Sarah laughed. The sound surprised her, and it seemed to surprise him too. His shoulders eased. He looked down at the slip of paper in his hand and said the inn had asked for more rye than usual. She told him Odalys had been feeding extra guests, and that the cook had started putting rosemary in the evening stew. He asked whether rosemary belonged in stew. She told him it did if the cook knew what he was doing.

The talk wandered. Sarah described a batch that had risen too quickly in the warm kitchen and a customer who insisted that bread should be square. Jack told her about a child who had asked whether the Heron could turn around in its own length. He had shown the child how the current helped the boat pivot, and the child had spent the rest of the crossing watching the wake.

Sarah listened, smiling at the image of Jack explaining the turn with patient hands. She could picture him giving the child his full attention, as if the question mattered. The thought made her look at him longer than she intended. He met her gaze, then glanced down at the paper. Neither spoke for a moment.

The lamp needed oil. Sarah stood to refill it, and Jack moved aside so she could reach the cupboard. They did not brush shoulders. When she returned, he had not shifted from his place at the counter. The quiet felt easy again, but now she knew how quickly ease could change into wanting.

The lamp flame steadied, and its light fell across the counter between them. Jack’s hand lay open beside the empty sack. Sarah set the oil can away, then returned to her side of the wood. The distance remained exactly as it had been.

Sarah turned to the rack. The loaf had risen tall overnight, its crown full and untroubled. She laid the cloth over it, then lifted the corner again to check the crust. Her smile had stayed with her through the talk, through his silence, through the moment he finally left. The shop was quiet now, but the smile remained.
