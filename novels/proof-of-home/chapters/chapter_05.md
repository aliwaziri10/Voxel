The flour arrived with the morning tide. Sarah stood on the dock and looked down at the crate. It was bound in canvas and rope, and it weighed more than she had expected. She had hauled sacks of flour before, she had lifted batches of dough that outweighed a child, yet this crate refused to budge. She lifted one corner, then set it back down. The gangplank of the Heron rose steeply, its wood slick with spring dew. Below her, Larkmere Water moved slow and dark against the pilings, the colour of pewter in the early light.

Jack Mercer stood on the deck of the ferry. He gripped the tiller rope in one hand and watched her with the quiet attention he always gave to things that needed moving. His shirt was the grey of lake stone, rolled at the sleeves. His hands were steady. She had noticed that about him long before she noticed anything else.

“It will not go up alone,” Sarah said.

“I did not think it would,” Jack said.

She considered waiting. She considered sending Nell down with an extra pair of hands, or waiting until Gideon Teague came past on his lantern route. But the bakery needed the flour. The ovens were already warm. She walked down the gangplank and took the far corner of the crate. Her palms met the rough weave. The canvas bit into her skin.

Jack took the near side. They lifted together.

The weight shifted toward the centre. Her fingers found the wooden edge beneath the canvas, and his fingers were already there. Skin touched skin. Callus brushed callus. The gangplank groaned beneath their boots. Sarah felt the wood tremble under them, the ferry pulling slightly at its mooring, the water lapping against the hull.

She held on.

She let her fingers linger a moment longer than necessary, feeling the warmth of his hand, the slight tension in his knuckles, the way his breath stayed even while his shoulder shifted. She thought of flour slipping through her fingers, of dough that resisted before yielding, of a loaf that needs time to rise before it can be touched, and of the quiet that follows a lift. Then she let go.

They carried the crate up the plank. Jack stepped onto the dock first and turned to take the rope. Sarah followed. The flour smelled of grain and the mill, dry and clean, the smell of bread before it became bread.

“Thank you,” she said.

Jack nodded. “It’s lighter with two.”

He did not look at her. He looked past her, toward Lantern Street, toward the bakery with its flour‑dusted windows. Nell stood at the rail of the Heron, her arms crossed, her sharp eyes missing nothing. Gideon Teague stood near the harbour lantern post, his back to the water, his face turned toward them, saying nothing at all.

Sarah turned toward the bakery and tried to leave the touch on the dock. It followed her inside. As she carried the flour to the bin, she remembered Jack’s hand beneath the crate edge and the way he had let her find it without pulling away. One second of contact should have been easy to dismiss. Her body kept returning to it.

She measured flour into a bowl, added water and starter, and worked the mixture until the dry patches disappeared. The dough caught at her knuckles. She scraped it free and folded the mass over itself, concentrating on its texture. She had noticed Jack stretching the last crossing before he turned for home; she had wondered whether he wanted her to stay aboard. But he would never ask another person to remain if he thought the request might become a kind of trap.

The touch beneath the crate had been practical. They needed both hands to carry the weight, and neither had said anything about the contact. Sarah could explain it that way. What she could not explain was why she had left her fingers there after finding the wooden edge. She covered the bowl and washed her hands; the water clouded with flour, then ran clear. When she reached for the towel, she caught herself turning it to its clean side again. She stopped, gave a short laugh, and hung it back on its hook.

The first rolls came out while the dough rested. She brushed their tops with melted butter and arranged them on the rack. A young mother came in with a child asleep against her shoulder and asked for a small loaf to take home for supper. Her husband had caught the early ferry to the far landing and would be back after his work was done.

“He always says he isn’t hungry on the crossing,” the woman said, smiling down at the child. “Then he comes home and eats half the loaf standing at the counter.”

Sarah wrapped a round loaf in paper. “Then I hope he gets home hungry.”

“He will.” The woman tucked the parcel under her free arm. “He always does.”

When the door closed, Sarah looked at the empty place on the rack. The loaf had been meant for a family expecting supper, nothing more. She was glad it had come out plain and comforting. She turned to the next order and checked the name against her list.

She returned to her own dough. It had risen enough to shape. She dusted the table, tipped it out, and gathered the outer skin beneath her palms. Her hands moved with more care than usual. She tucked the seam underneath, turned the loaf, and pressed lightly at the crown. A ferry horn sounded across the harbor. She did not turn toward the window, though the muscles at the back of her neck tightened.

She had promised herself she would not think about Jack. She had not promised she would succeed.

The loaf settled into its basket with a smooth, even curve. Sarah scored it once and slid it into the oven. While it baked, she weighed the remaining flour into smaller sacks, tied each one shut, and marked them with plain paper labels. The work made the minutes pass. It did not empty her mind. Twice she caught herself rubbing her thumb against the place where his finger had brushed hers. Each time she stopped and put her hand to another task.

By the time the afternoon light had shifted across the floor, the mark on her palm had faded. The warmth had not. She tested the loaf with a light tap, heard the hollow note beneath the crust, and set it on the rack to cool. Its shape was good. The feeling inside it was harder to name, but she could not pretend it was absent.

She wiped the board clean. At the window, the harbor narrowed between the buildings. The Heron was already beyond the first buoy. Sarah saw only the white line of its wake before the water closed over it, and turned away before she could search the deck for Jack.

The oven had reached the right temperature. Sarah shaped the loaf, pinched the seam closed, and set it on the peel. She checked the salt in the next batch before covering the bowl. This loaf was for the inn’s breakfast order; she wanted it reliable, with an even crumb and a crust that would hold until morning.

She consulted Fenna’s recipe journal. The margins were filled with small marks, a language of dots and slashes that Sarah was slowly learning to read. Next to the entry for a plain country loaf, Fenna had written three dots in a triangle. Sarah did not yet know what they meant. She followed the recipe anyway, using flour that had travelled on the Heron, water from the tap, salt from the bowl by the sink.

She kneaded. The dough resisted, then yielded. It was alive under her hands, warm and breathing. She thought of Jack’s hands on the tiller. She thought of his fingers under the crate. She did not think of them. She thought only of the dough.

When the loaf came out of the oven, the crust was golden and the bottom sounded hollow when she tapped it. She set it on the cooling rack and wrapped her arms around herself. The bakery smelled of yeast and fire and the honest labour of her hands.

Odalys Pike came in just before noon. The bell above the door chimed. Odalys wore her innkeeper’s apron over a dress the colour of dried lavender. She ran the Lamplight Inn, and she read faces for a living. She read Sarah’s immediately.

“You look as if you’re hiding a thought,” Odalys said.

“I’m not,” Sarah said.

“You baked a loaf,” Odalys said. She walked to the counter and pointed at the loaf. “What is that?”

“Bread.”

“You baked it this morning.”

Sarah nodded.

Odalys cut a slice and ate it standing at the counter. Halfway through, she stopped and looked down at the crumb. She turned the piece over in her fingers, then took another bite, smaller than the first. Her gaze moved to Sarah, but she did not tease her as she usually would. The tea cooled untouched beside her elbow. She brushed crumbs from her apron before reaching for the cup, then changed her mind and left it there. Outside, gulls quarrelled over the harbour wall, and the clock kept ticking above the oven.

When Odalys finally spoke, her voice was quiet. “This is an honest loaf,” she said.

Sarah’s hands stopped mid‑motion. “I didn’t put anything in it.”

“You didn’t have to,” Odalys said. She looked at Sarah, her eyes sharp and soft all at once. “It speaks for itself.”

Sarah set the knife down. “What does it say?”

Odalys considered the slice in her hand, turning it so the light fell across the uneven crumb. “That you’re trying very hard to call an ordinary feeling.”

“It is ordinary. Flour, water, salt.”

“And the hands that worked it.” Odalys set the bread on the plate instead of taking another bite. “You don’t have to tell me what’s wrong. I’m not asking.”

Sarah glanced toward the window. The ferry had already left the landing; only a thin white seam remained on the water. “I don’t know what’s wrong,” she said, and the admission surprised her with its honesty.

“Then don’t give it a name yet.” Odalys folded the paper around the loaf. “Let it be a loaf today. Tomorrow it may tell you another story.”

Sarah wanted to ask whether Odalys had tasted the same feeling she had felt under the crate, the brief warmth of Jack’s fingers. She could not make herself say his name. Odalys waited a moment, then lifted the parcel and tucked it under her arm. She left the tea untouched and did not offer another joke.

Sarah could have blamed the uneven rise on the weather or the flour. Odalys watched her across the counter, and the loaf sat between them with its lopsided crown. Sarah reached for the knife instead of answering.

“I should get back to the inn,” Odalys said. She wrapped the rest of the loaf in paper. “Thank you, Sarah.”

“Of course.”

Odalys left. The door closed behind her with a soft chime.

Sarah was alone with the empty space on the rack. She touched the counter where the loaf had been. Flour dusted her fingertips.

Outside, the light turned toward evening. Spring afternoons were short, the sun slipping below the hills before Sarah was ready. She swept the floor. She stacked the clean bowls. She checked the proofing dough for tomorrow.

Gideon Teague lit the harbour lanterns at dusk. Sarah saw the first glow from the bakery window, a yellow eye opening over the water. Whoever stood at the same lantern when it caught was said to be thinking of the same person. She did not stand at the lantern. She stayed behind the counter, her hands on the flour sack.

She thought of Jack on the Heron. She thought of him wanting a longer crossing, of the ferry drifting slow beneath him, and of the way he would not ask her to stay. She thought of the crate, of the fingers under the edge, and of the second she had held on.

She turned the sign to Closed and checked the lock. Dusk had settled over Lantern Street, but she stayed inside to finish the work before going home.

Her own hands waited for her at the sink. She filled the basin with warm water. She washed the flour from her fingers, watching it cloud and drain. The water ran clear. She flexed her fingers under the towel, then lowered them.

She opened the drawer beneath the counter and took out Fenna’s old linen cloth, the one kept for covering loaves that needed a slower rise. Its hem had come loose at one corner. Sarah threaded a needle and made three small stitches, pulling the thread tight enough to hold but not so tight that the fabric puckered. The work required her eyes and both hands. For a little while, she could think of nothing beyond the needle passing through the weave and returning to the light. The needle flashed once in the window light. When she tied the thread, her fingertips met in a small, firm knot. She held them there, then laid the cloth beside the cooling rack. The repair was not invisible; three small stitches showed against the worn linen. Fenna had never bothered to hide a mended thing if the work would hold. Sarah smoothed the cloth once, checking the seam with her thumb, and left the needle threaded for the next tear.

She dried her hands on a towel and left them resting on the counter, palms down, as if waiting for the warmth to return.
