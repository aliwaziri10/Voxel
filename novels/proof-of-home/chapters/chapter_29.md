Sarah walked out to Jack’s cottage with a covered bowl in her basket and a burnt loaf under her arm, and told herself she was bringing supper.

The water road ran out of the harbor along the shore, a rutted strip of cinders between the lake on one side and a hedge of bare brambles on the other. It was the first week the frost had held through the afternoon. The ruts had gone hard as crusts and rang under her boots, and the air had the clean metal smell that meant the first fires of the year were lit in every house she could not see. Behind her the pier lamps were coming on in a curve. Ahead lay the dark line of the shore, and at the very end of the road one small square of yellow.

He had asked her at the gangway that morning, addressing a mooring cleat. “Would you come to supper,” he had said, in the voice of a man reading out a weather report, and she had said yes before he finished. Nell had announced to a coil of rope that she was sleeping at the Lamplight tonight on Odalys’s orders, and had then vanished with the ease of practice.

Sarah had baked all day and could not have named one loaf of it. Her hands had known what to do, and she had let them, and by dusk her whole body hummed like a bowl rung with a spoon.

The door opened before she reached it. He stood in it in his shirtsleeves with the lamp behind him, and the light laid a line along his shoulders and left his face dark, and she could not tell if he was smiling. She thought he was frightened. She thought, with a rush of tenderness that nearly stopped her on the step, that they both were.

“You came,” said Jack.

“You asked.”

“I did not think that was how it worked.”

“It is how it works,” said Sarah, and ducked in past his arm.

It was one room and a lean-to, low-beamed and very clean. A black stove ticked in the corner with its door ajar. There was a kettle, a table scrubbed white with two chairs drawn up to it, a shelf of charts rolled and tied with cord, and along the wall beside the stove a narrow bench with a gray blanket folded at one end. Nothing hung on the walls. Nothing on the floor wanted tidying. It was the room of a man who expected, at any hour, to be called away.

She set the loaf on the table, and the bowl beside it. “I brought the ballast.”

He looked at the lifeboat. It sat on the boards black and lopsided with its split crust turned up like a lid, and he put out two fingers and touched it as she had in her loft. His face changed. “It is still warm.”

“It has been warm for a week. I have stopped asking why.”

“I would like it entered,” said Jack, “that I have improved.”

He had made a stew of perch and potatoes. It had caught a little on the bottom of the pot, and he apologized for that three times, and she ate two bowls. There were two chairs and neither of them used them. They sat on the bench with the bowls on their knees and their shoulders a hand apart, because the table would have put a width of wood between them and the bench had none to spare. It was built for one. Every time she lifted the spoon her elbow brushed his, and every time he went still, and every time she did not move away.

He took her bowl when it was empty. He crossed to the stove to feed it, with his back to her, and she watched the line of his neck above the collar and understood she could not sit another minute at the width of a hand.

She rose. She crossed and stood behind him. He knew she was there, and he laid the poker down very slowly on the hearth, as a man puts down a thing he no longer trusts himself to hold.

“May I,” said Jack, to the fire.

“You are going to ask me that every time.”

“I intend to. It appears to be going well.”

He turned. In the red light his face was open in a way she had seen only once, on the lake, and she put both palms on his chest and felt his heart hammer through the shirt, and rose onto her toes.

It began as the other had, slowly, and then it did not. His mouth was hot and unsteady. His hands, which on the Heron had stayed so careful, came up and spread across her back and drew her in until nothing lay between them but wool and breath. The stove burned on her calves. She had her fingers in his hair, and then at his collar, and the top button gave under them, and she found the bare skin at the base of his throat with her thumb and felt him shudder from his shoulders to his boots. He made a sound she had never heard from any man. His mouth moved down her jaw to the place under her ear where her pulse beat, and her knees gave a little, and the room whitened at the edges, and she thought quite clearly: I will not stop. Nobody could ask it of me.

She stopped.

She did it with both palms flat on his chest and her eyes shut. “Jack.” It came out in two pieces. “Stop.”

He stopped. He stopped so instantly that she felt the whole length of him obey it, as a finger leaves a hot pan. His hands left her and opened, fingers spread, and hung at his sides. His breath sawed in the quiet. His forehead dropped to her hair, and for a long moment neither of them did anything but breathe and shake.

“I am sorry,” he said, thickly.

“Do not be sorry.” She still had her palms on him. She could not seem to take them off. “Look at me. It is not that I do not want you. I want you so much I cannot think my own name. Please do not let me hear you take it any other way.”

He lifted his head. His eyes were black.

“I will not give you the half of me,” said Sarah. “I have done that. I have the method by heart. I know how to give a place the pieces of me that fit, and leave the rest on the stairs. If I come to you I want to come whole, with nothing held in reserve, and I do not yet know that I can stay with my whole heart. The winter I cannot see. Until I can, I would be giving you a thing I might take away again.” Her voice broke. “I will not do that to you. I would sooner shake in this room all night.”

He did not speak. She watched him take it in. She watched the muscle jump at the corner of his jaw, and the hands at his sides close slowly into fists and then, with an effort she could see in his forearms, open again.

“Then I will wait,” he said. His voice was rough as a crust broken on the board.

“Jack.”

“As long as it takes.” He said it first to the floor, and then he made himself say it to her face. “I have waited on that lake for most of my life for nothing at all. I can wait for you.”

It cost them both. She felt it go through the cottage, a hunger neither would feed, and she wanted to weep at how gentle he was.

“Come here,” he said, “and sit down, before I forget myself,” and she laughed, and it caught halfway, and she went.

He took the gray blanket from the end of the bench and shook it out, and for a moment she saw him smell it, tar and old rain, and his face went far off and came back. He sat in the corner where the bench met the wall and drew her down in front of him, her back against his chest, and wrapped the wool around them both. Between her spine and the buttons of his shirt there was no space at all. Between her hips and his there was exactly a hand’s width of air, and she felt the labor of it in every muscle of her body, in the iron of his forearm lying across her ribs. He did not move it an inch. His breath stirred the hair at her neck. His heart knocked against her shoulder blade, hard and quick, and slowly, over an hour, slower.

They talked.

She had not planned to, but the dark and the ticking stove and the heat of him behind her took the lid off her. She told him about the city. She told it without turning, to the wall, in the plainest words she could find.

“There was a man. A long time. He was not cruel.” She felt him go still behind her. “I want you to understand that. He was not unkind. He was only large, the way weather is large, and I made myself the size of whatever room was left. Every year a little smaller. I could sit on the edge of a sofa for a whole evening. I could say I do not mind in a voice so level that I believed myself. I got so good at it that I could not have told you what I wanted for breakfast.” She laid her fingers over his forearm. “One morning I took a coat and the key to this shop, and I left while he was asleep, because I did not know how to leave out loud. I thought I came here for the bakery. I think I came to see whether a woman might be allowed to take up room.”

He said nothing for a while. His arm tightened, once, and eased.

“You take up room,” said Jack, very low. “I have noticed it from the first day. I think the whole harbor has.”

She laughed, and it broke in the middle, and she let it. He did not tell her not to cry. He only held on, and when she was quiet he said into her hair, “Tell me about the winter.”

“I do not know,” said Sarah. “That is the truth, and I am sorry it is. I have a shop and a season I promised myself, and I have you, and I am so afraid that wanting you is the reason I would stay and not the answer. I do not want to stay because I am held. Nobody is keeping me.” She turned her head against his chest. “I am lying here.”

“I know.”

“And you?”

He was quiet for a very long while. The stove ticked. “I am afraid of the answer,” he said at last.

“Which one?”

“Either one. I used to think it was the no.” His chest rose against her shoulders and fell. “It is the yes. A no would only be a loss I know how to hold. A yes means I will have a thing I can lose.”

She turned in his arms then, carefully, until she could see his face, and laid her forehead against his collarbone, and they lay so, wrapped, his palm open and still on her spine and hers wound in his shirt. She did not kiss him. She did not need to be told what that would cost. They talked of small things after that, of Fenna’s margins and his grandfather’s barometer and how the starter smelled in a cold week, and in the small hours the talk gave out into silences, and the silences were not empty. She felt him fall asleep with his palm still shaped to her back. She lay awake in the heat of him and wanted him more than she had ever wanted anything, and did not move.

The window went gray before dawn.

She slid out from under his arm without waking him. He turned toward the warm place she had left and his fingers curled round the shape of it. She stood a moment with her heart too large for her chest. Then she took the cloth from her bowl.

The dough had risen in the night and lifted the cloth like a held breath. She laid it on his scrubbed table, and shaped it with her sleeves pushed up, by the light of the stove and the pale window, and put into it what she had.

She did not hide it. She had done that for a season. Tenderness went in first: the weight of his forearm across her ribs, the way he had said as long as it takes. Then the other thing, the want that would not be hurried, the hunger of a night spent a hand’s width off, and she folded that in too and let it show. The stove had a small oven he had plainly never used for anything but warming boots. She set the round in it and sat on the hearth with her arms around her knees until the crust took color and the whole cottage smelled of him and of herself.

She left it on the table in a clean cloth. She laid her palm over it once. It was warm and humming, and under her fingers it said, quite distinctly, yours, and then, more quietly, not yet.

She pulled on Fenna’s coat and let herself out. The frost lay white on the cinders and the lake was the color of pewter, and she walked home along the water road with her empty bowl in the basket and her mouth still warm.

At the bend she stopped, and turned.

The cottage window had lit. A man stood in it, not waving and not moving, with the lamp behind him and one palm flat on the glass. She did not wave either. She lifted her chin and walked on toward the harbor and the first gold edge of the day, and behind her the window stayed lit, steady as a loaf set to cool on a sill.
