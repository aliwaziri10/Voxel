The oven breathed warmth against the kitchen wall and Sarah could not sleep. It was late spring and the sky had gone soft gray over Larkmere, the evening when the air smelled of wet stone and distant pine. She stood behind the counter of her bakery on Lantern Street and watched the flame inside the big iron belly pulse slow and steady. The dough sat on the wooden board before her, a rough mass of flour and water and salt, waiting for her hands to give it shape.

She had promised herself one season. One season of bread and quiet mornings, with the promise that she might stay. But the promise felt smaller now, folded into itself like a layer of proofing time. The ache had settled somewhere beneath her ribs, a low hum that rose whenever she thought of leaving. Tonight it pressed against her chest and made her want to rise from the table and walk the streets until her feet found the riverbank and the water ran cold against her skin.

Her fingers found the dough again. She pushed and turned the dough, feeling the resistance change under her palms. This was the part of making bread that no one taught you in school: the way the dough remembered your touch and grew calmer as she worked it, carrying what she felt while she shaped it. Right now she felt the hollow space behind her sternum, the wanting that had been building since Fenna died. It was not a sharp desire. It was more like a slow tide pulling at the shore, patient and inevitable.

The oven glowed orange through the window above the sink. From here Sarah could see the faint blue-white light spilling onto the floorboards, a line of heat that seemed to reach out and touch her. She knew exactly where Jack was standing. He had come down the lane at half past midnight, his coat pulled tight against the chill that crept up from the ground. His boots were heavy on the stones outside, and he moved with the deep stillness of someone who had not slept well either.

He stopped at the threshold of the kitchen and looked in. The dough was still rough, still unformed, but she could see the way the surface had begun to ripple under her fingertips. She did not turn around. Instead she reached out and laid a palm flat against the board, feeling the temperature rise through her wrist. The heat was real and immediate, a wave of warmth that spread up her arm and settled somewhere in her throat.

Jack stepped closer. He was taller than her by a few inches and his face was weathered in the way of men who spend their days looking out at water. There was a scar along his jawline, old and thin, and his eyes were the color of slate. He had not spoken since she had opened the door. Now he simply stood there, watching the dough, his breathing slow and even.

She could have asked him to wait, told him to go home and find his own bed, or urged him to stop lingering in her shop at three in the morning. But the ache in her chest had grown heavier, and she found herself wanting to fill the silence with something other than questions. So she nodded once, a small motion, and turned back to the dough.

The minutes stretched. At first it was just a minute. She counted the seconds in her mind, the way she always did when the numbers became hard to hold. Then the minute became an hour. The heat from the oven seemed to deepen, and she could feel the dough responding to her presence, pressing back against her hands as if it recognized her. She added another handful of water, a splash that made the surface slick and shiny. The dough resisted at first, then yielded, becoming pliable and alive under her palms.

She leaned forward slightly, bringing her forehead closer to the board. The scent of yeast filled the room, sweet and sour at once, and underneath it was a hint of brine, a warmer note that reminded her of the sea even though she lived inland. The bread was doing what breads do. It was taking her feeling and giving it form. And the feeling was not yet clear to her. It was a pull, a direction toward the water, toward the dark places where things happened.

From the doorway Jack shifted his weight. He was still close enough that she could see the faint stubble along his neck, the way his shirt hung loose on his shoulders. He did not move away. Neither did she. The oven's glow painted both of them in amber light, and the heat wrapped around them like a blanket that neither of them wanted to remove.

She felt the contact before she saw it. A brush of her knuckles against his forearm as she reached for a rolling pin. His skin was cool, slightly damp from the humidity that clung to the bakery walls. She did not pull away. Instead she pressed the pin into the dough, turning it over and smoothing the surface with practiced hands. The motion was automatic, the result of years of working the same tools, but tonight it felt different. Tonight the dough seemed to lean into her touch, accepting the pressure, and she understood that acceptance meant something.

"Good evening," he said. His voice was low and unhurried, the same voice she had heard on the ferry many times before. "How is the shop?"

She did not answer immediately. She was thinking about the bread she had already started, the way the heat was changing its texture, the way the surface began to bubble at the edges. The dough was warming faster than it should have, and she could feel the risk of it overheating, of burning the delicate balance she had spent hours creating. She needed to work quickly, but also to breathe, to let the feeling settle into something she could control.

"I'm fine," she said finally. "The bread is... it's waking."

That was true enough. The dough was indeed waking, expanding and rising as the heat from the oven met the moisture in her hands. She could almost taste the transformation, the way the simple ingredients came together into something that was more than the sum of its parts. But she did not say more. There was no need. The ache in her chest had become a companion, a constant presence that would not fade until she decided what to do with it.

Jack studied her face. She caught the glance and the slight furrow between his brows, wondering what he was seeing. Perhaps he noticed the tension in her shoulders, the way her hands had slowed despite her best efforts to keep them moving. Or perhaps he was simply observing the scene as he always did, noting how the water moved and the light shifted, and how people kept secrets in the folds of their clothing.

She returned to the dough. Her movements were slower now, more deliberate. Each fold and press was a conversation between her hands and the mixture. The bread was telling her something, though she could not quite hear it. She tried to listen, to let the feeling guide her rather than forcing it. This was the art of baking, she had learned. You could not command the dough to be what you wanted. You could only offer it your attention and your patience, which includes your willingness to wait.

The clock on the wall ticked. Outside, a bird called somewhere in the trees, a short and sharp sound that faded into the evening. The bread continued to rise, the surface gleaming with a film of oil that had formed as the heat intensified. She could see the bubbles forming at the top, tiny spheres of steam that burst and vanished. It was beautiful, in its way. The bread was alive, and she was its caretaker.

At some point she realized she had not spoken to Jack in more than ten minutes. The silence between them was comfortable, familiar. It was the silence of two people who had known each other long enough to understand that words were not required for connection. She could feel the warmth of him beside her, the slight shift of his weight as he adjusted his stance. He was present, fully present, and she was grateful for that. In a world where everyone was always running somewhere, this was a rare gift.

She lifted the rolling pin and began to roll the dough into a log. The motion was rhythmic, almost meditative. The wood of the pin sang against the flour-covered surface, and she felt the grain of the dough beneath her fingers, the way it yielded and compressed. This was the part of baking that most people missed: the patience required, the trust placed in the process. You could not rush or force bread into shape. You had to let it happen, and in letting it happen, you became part of it.

Jack watched the log emerge from her hands. He did not comment on the progress. He simply stood there, his arms at his sides, his gaze fixed on the dough. She could have asked him to join her at the counter, to share the warmth of the kitchen. But the ache was stronger now, and she needed to focus on the bread, on the task at hand. The feeling she carried was not something to discuss. It was something to bake.

The oven's heat intensified. The dough on the log began to puff and expand, the surface stretching upward in gentle ridges. She could see the first signs of the crust forming, a pale golden edge that promised the finished product. The smell was filling the bakery now, richer than before, carrying notes of caramelization and warmth. It was a smell that belonged to late spring and the beginning of summer, a season when the world felt possible again.

She paused, resting her hand on the log, feeling the heat radiating through her palm. The sensation was intense, almost painful in its intensity, but she welcomed it. This was the price of creation. To bring something into being required surrender to the process, and surrender required endurance. She had endured before. She had baked through difficult weeks and losses, and through the long nights when the ache was loudest. This was simply another trial, another step toward whatever came next.

Jack shifted again. This time he moved closer, his shoulder nearly brushing hers as he leaned in to examine the dough. She did not flinch. She had learned long ago that people often approached her with something they could not name, and the easiest response was to remain still and let them find their own path. He was doing that now. He was observing and not demanding, and he appreciated that more than he could say.

She turned back to the log and began to score it, making shallow cuts with a knife. The pattern was simple, a series of parallel lines that would help the bread expand evenly during the next stage. As she cut, she felt the knife slide through the dough, and for a moment the bread seemed to respond, the fibers loosening, the structure relaxing. It was as if the act of cutting was a release, a permission for the bread to become something new.

The ache in her chest had not disappeared. It had deepened, settling into her bones. It was accompanied now by something else, a strange clarity that came with the bread rising. She could see the shape of what was coming—not the finished loaf, but the intention behind it. The feeling she was carrying was not merely longing. It was hope, tangled with it. Hope that she might stay and that the season might be enough, while the ache might transform into something bearable.

She rolled the scored log into a cylinder and set it on the cooling rack. The heat was fading, the oven's glow dimming to a soft red. Soon the bread would need to go into the oven proper, to finish its journey from the counter to the proofing box. But for now she stood at the oven door, shoulder to shoulder with Jack, neither of them willing to break the spell of the moment.

The contact between them had deepened. She could feel the warmth of his proximity, the slight vibration of his heartbeat through the air. It was not a romantic touch, not yet. It was something quieter, something that existed in the spaces between words. She reached out and touched his forearm again, this time with her thumb, brushing it lightly across the raised skin near his elbow. The gesture was small, almost accidental, but it sent a shiver through her that had nothing to do with temperature.

Jack did not react. He simply looked at her, his expression unreadable. For a moment they stood there, the oven's glow casting shadows between them, and the silence stretched. Then he exhaled slowly, a breath that seemed to carry the weight of the entire evening, and turned back to the dough.

They remained like that for what felt like an hour. The bread continued to rise, the crust darkening to a deep gold. The ache in Sarah's chest pulsed in time with the rhythm of her breathing. She could feel the bread transforming, the simple ingredients becoming something that carried her feeling and her hope, while also reflecting her uncertainty. It was not yet ready. It would need more time and heat, and also more patience. But she was patient now. She had learned that lesson from Fenna and the long years of baking, and from the understanding that the best things in life require time to mature.

When she finally stepped back to check the progress, the dough was almost done. The log had expanded into a round loaf, its surface puffy and golden, the cracks forming patterns like frozen lightning. She lifted it gently from the rack and set it on the lower shelf of the proofing box. The bread was sleeping now, waiting for the next stage of its journey. She would return tomorrow and the day after, and eventually it would emerge from the oven as something that could feed a family, something that could carry her secret feeling to the world.

Jack had not moved from his position by the oven door. He was watching her with those slate-colored eyes, and he could see the question forming behind his gaze. *What are you doing?* he seemed to ask. *Why won't you let me in?*

But she could not answer. The bread was already rising, already becoming something more than dough. And she knew, with a certainty that went deeper than logic, that this moment, marked by shared warmth and forced closeness, was not something to be rushed. It was a seed planted, and seeds took time to grow.

She turned back to the counter and began to prepare the next batch. The morning would come soon and the shop would open; the world would continue its slow rotation. But for now, in this small space where the oven breathed and the bread rose, she was content to exist in the quiet space between heartbeats. The ache was still there. It would always be there. But so was the bread, rising steadily, carrying her feeling forward into whatever came next.

Outside, the first stars were appearing, faint and distant, reflected in the dark water of Larkmere. The night was deepening and the bakery was warm, and somewhere in the distance the Heron waited for the dawn. Sarah Quill stood at the center of it all, her hands still dusted with flour, her heart still heavy with the promise she had made to herself. The season was still young. The bread was still rising. And the ache, patient as dough, would wait for her to decide what to do with it.
