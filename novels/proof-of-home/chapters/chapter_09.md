Sarah could not sleep. The bakery had cooled around her, but the oven still held a red glow. She stood at the worktable in her nightdress and apron, the dough waiting beneath a damp cloth. Lantern Street had gone quiet outside.

She had promised herself a season in Larkmere, enough time to learn Fenna’s recipes and decide whether the shop could become hers. She had not promised herself she would be untouched by the place. Lately, leaving had grown harder to imagine. Jack had become part of the town’s daily shape: a figure at the tiller, a voice at the landing, a pair of hands she kept remembering.

Her fingers found the dough again. She pushed and turned the dough, feeling the resistance change under her palms. This was the part of making bread that no one taught you in school: the way the dough remembered your touch and grew calmer as she worked it, carrying what she felt while she shaped it. Right now she felt the hollow space behind her sternum, the wanting that had been building since Fenna died. It was not a sharp desire. It was more like a slow tide pulling at the shore, patient and inevitable.

The oven’s glow crossed the floorboards. She heard a footstep in the alley, then a pause. Jack had come down the lane after the last crossing, coat pulled close against the spring chill. He stood beyond the threshold, as wakeful as she was.

He looked in at the worktable. Sarah kept her palm on the dough and waited. When he did not speak, she nodded toward the open space beside the oven. It was permission to stay, though she did not know why she had given it.

Jack stepped closer. He was taller than her by a few inches and his face was weathered in the way of men who spend their days looking out at water. There was a scar along his jawline, old and thin, and his eyes were the color of slate. He had not spoken since she had opened the door. Now he simply stood there, watching the dough, his breathing slow and even.

She could have asked him to wait, told him to go home and find his own bed, or urged him to stop lingering in her shop at three in the morning. But the ache in her chest had grown heavier, and she found herself wanting to fill the silence with a feeling other than questions. So she nodded once, a small motion, and turned back to the dough.

At first she meant to let him stay for a minute. She would finish the fold and send him back to the ferry. But the dough needed care, the oven needed checking, and the loaf would not be rushed. She moved between the table and the oven. Jack remained near the door. Neither mentioned the passing time.

She leaned forward slightly, bringing her forehead closer to the board. The scent of yeast filled the room, sweet and sour at once, and underneath it was a hint of brine, a warmer note that reminded her of the sea even though she lived inland. The smell of yeast and barley filled the kitchen. The feeling in the dough was not clear, but she knew it had changed. It held sweetness and a shadow beneath it.

From the doorway Jack shifted his weight. He was still close enough that she could see the faint stubble along his neck, the way his shirt hung loose on his shoulders. He did not move away. Neither did she. The oven's glow painted both of them in amber light, and the heat wrapped around them like a blanket that neither of them wanted to remove.

She reached for the rolling pin. Jack shifted at the same moment, and their shoulders came close enough for her to feel the warmth of him beside her. They did not touch. Sarah kept her eyes on the dough while he turned his attention to the oven door.

"Good evening," he said. His voice was low and unhurried, the same voice she had heard on the ferry many times before. "How is the shop?"

She looked at the dough before answering. The heat had brought it along faster than she expected. She needed to shape it and set it aside, but she did not want to hurry simply because Jack was standing there.

"I'm fine," she said finally. "The bread is... it's waking."

That was true enough. The dough was indeed waking, expanding and rising as the heat from the oven met the moisture in her hands. She could almost taste the transformation, the way the simple ingredients came together into a feeling that was more than the sum of its parts. But she did not say more. There was no need. The ache in her chest had become a companion, a constant presence that would not fade until she decided what to do with it.

Jack studied her face. She caught the glance and the slight furrow between his brows, wondering what he was seeing. Perhaps he noticed the tension in her shoulders, the way her hands had slowed despite her best efforts to keep them moving. Or perhaps he was simply observing the scene as he always did, noting how the water moved and the light shifted, and how people kept secrets in the folds of their clothing.

She returned to the dough. Her movements were slower now, more deliberate. Each fold and press was a conversation between her hands and the mixture. The bread was telling her a feeling, though she could not quite hear it. She tried to listen, to let the feeling guide her rather than forcing it. This was the art of baking, she had learned. You could not command the dough to be what you wanted. You could only offer it your attention and your patience, which includes your willingness to wait.

The clock on the wall ticked. Outside, a bird called in the distance in the trees, a short and sharp sound that faded into the evening. The bread continued to rise, the surface gleaming with a film of oil that had formed as the heat intensified. She could see the bubbles forming at the top, tiny spheres of steam that burst and vanished. It was beautiful, in its way. The bread was alive, and she was its caretaker.

The silence grew easy without becoming comfortable. She knew the sound of Jack’s breathing now, the slight shift of his boots when he changed his weight. She had no right to feel relieved that he stayed. She felt it anyway.

She lifted the rolling pin and began to roll the dough into a log. The motion was rhythmic, almost meditative. The wood of the pin sang against the flour-covered surface, and she felt the grain of the dough beneath her fingers, the way it yielded and compressed. This was the part of baking that most people missed: the patience required, the trust placed in the process. You could not rush or force bread into shape. You had to let it happen, and in letting it happen, you became part of it.

Jack watched the log emerge from her hands. He did not comment on the progress. He simply stood there, his arms at his sides, his gaze fixed on the dough. She could have asked him to join her at the counter, to share the warmth of the kitchen. But the ache was stronger now, and she needed to focus on the bread, on the task at hand. She could not discuss the feeling. Her hands could only shape it.

The oven's heat intensified. The dough on the log began to puff and expand, the surface stretching upward in gentle ridges. She could see the first signs of the crust forming, a pale golden edge that promised the finished product. The smell was filling the bakery now, richer than before, carrying notes of caramelization and warmth. It was a smell that belonged to late spring and the beginning of summer, a season when the world felt possible again.

She paused, resting her hand on the log, feeling the heat radiating through her palm. The sensation was intense, almost painful in its intensity, but she welcomed it. This was the price of creation. To bring a feeling into being required surrender to the process, and surrender required endurance. She had endured before. She had baked through difficult weeks and losses, and through the long nights when the ache was loudest. This was simply another trial, another step toward whatever came next.

Jack shifted again. This time he moved closer, his shoulder nearly brushing hers as he leaned in to examine the dough. She did not flinch. She had learned long ago that people often approached her with a feeling they could not name, and the easiest response was to remain still and let them find their own path. He was doing that now. He was observing and not demanding, and he appreciated that more than he could say.

She scored the loaf with three shallow cuts, spacing them by eye. The blade dragged at the second slash, so she wiped it clean before making the third. Beside her, Jack shifted the cooling rack away from the oven door to give her room.

The feeling in her chest had not eased. Hope had joined it, and that made the wanting harder to bear. Hope asked her to imagine a future; fear kept naming the ways it could end.

She rolled the scored log into a cylinder and set it on the cooling rack. The heat was fading, the oven's glow dimming to a soft red. Soon the bread would need to go into the oven proper, to finish its journey from the counter to the proofing box. But for now she stood at the oven door, shoulder to shoulder with Jack, neither of them willing to break the spell of the moment.

They stood shoulder to shoulder at the oven door. The heat warmed Sarah’s cheeks. She could feel the space his body occupied beside hers without turning to look. She did not reach for him. He did not reach for her.

Jack did not react. He simply looked at her, his expression unreadable. For a moment they stood there, the oven's glow casting shadows between them, and the silence stretched. Then he exhaled slowly, a breath that seemed to carry the weight of the entire evening, and turned back to the dough.

Neither of them moved until the timer rang. Sarah turned first, grateful for the interruption, and checked the loaf through the oven glass. The crust had browned along one side. She reached for the peel, then stopped when Jack shifted to give her room. “Thanks,” she said. He stepped back without answering, and she took the loaf out herself.

When she finally stepped back to check the progress, the dough was almost done. The log had expanded into a round loaf, its surface puffy and golden, the cracks forming patterns like frozen lightning. She lifted it gently from the rack and set it on the lower shelf of the proofing box. The bread was sleeping now, waiting for the next stage of its journey. She would return tomorrow and the day after, and eventually it would emerge from the oven as a feeling that could feed a family, a feeling that could carry her secret feeling to the world.

Jack had not moved from his position by the oven door. He was watching her with those slate-colored eyes, and he could see the question forming behind his gaze. *What are you doing?* he seemed to ask. *Why won't you let me in?*

But she could not answer. The bread was already rising, already becoming a feeling more than dough. And she knew, with a certainty that went deeper than logic, that this moment, marked by shared warmth and forced closeness, was not a feeling to be rushed. It was a seed planted, and seeds took time to grow.

She cleared the board and weighed flour for the next batch. Jack had gone, but she kept noticing the space where he had stood. When the scale settled, she added the salt and reached for the water. The dough needed her attention; she gave it that, kneading until the rough mass began to smooth beneath her palms.

Outside, the last light had left the harbor. Jack stood beside her at the oven, his hands at his sides. The loaf’s crust darkened behind the glass, and neither of them turned away.



Jack did not leave when the loaf went into the proofing box. Sarah heard his boots shift and knew he was still there. She kept busy with the bowl, the cloth, the knife laid beside the sink. The work gave her a reason not to turn her head, but it did not explain why she had made room for him.

The hour had passed without either of them noticing it arrive. The night pressed against the windows. Sarah thought of the room above the shop, of the bed she had left unmade, of the morning work waiting to be done. Jack had his own bunk and the ferry to mind. She should have sent him away while the first minute was still only a minute.

Instead she held a clean cloth near the oven door. The heat rose against her knuckles. Jack stood beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth at his shoulder, yet careful not to let their sleeves meet. That restraint seemed louder than any question he might have asked.

“Will it be ready by morning?” he said.

“The loaf? Yes, if I leave it alone.”

He nodded, taking the answer as if it were meant for more than bread. Sarah wanted to explain that she had not meant to keep him there. She wanted to tell him she had noticed every shift of his weight and every time he looked at her hands. She kept the words to herself. Behind the glass, the loaf cooled; its crust had split darkly along the crown, and the oven light stopped just short of Jack’s boots.

She cleared the last of the flour from the board, running the scraper along the wood until the surface showed through. Jack waited without asking whether she needed help. She was grateful he left the scraper where it was. The space beside her hand remained clear, narrow enough that either of them could have closed it with a shift of the wrist.
