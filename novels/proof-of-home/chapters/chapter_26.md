The loaf weighed as much as a small anchor, and it was the best of four.

Jack carried it up Lantern Street at the end of the afternoon in a clean cloth, held out a little from his body in both arms, as a man carries a thing that may go off. The first three had not survived the night. This one had come out of Odalys Pike’s second oven in a single piece, and he had stopped at the door of the Lamplight Inn on his way past to show it to her. She had looked at it for the length of one slow breath and said, with great tenderness, that she had never seen a loaf so determined.

Nell had told him about the birthday afterward, which was how Nell told him everything that mattered: in passing, over a coil of line, addressed to the rope. The baker had been a year older on the morning crossing, she said. Odalys had made a cake. Nobody had told Uncle because Uncle would only have gone quiet and looked at the lake. He had gone quiet and looked at the lake. Then he had gone to the inn, and Odalys had put him in her kitchen with her flour and her second oven and her patience, which was not infinite, and had stood in the doorway with her arms folded for the better part of three evenings while he ruined everything she owned.

He had never made her a gift. He did not know how it was done. He knew how to bring a woman across the water, and how to say good morning to her, and how to ask whether she took sugar, and that was the whole of his craft. So he had made her a question out of flour and water and salt, because he could not make one out of words, and he did not let himself look at what the question was.

The air had turned. It smelled of the first fires of the year, and there was a thin white rime on the pier planks at dawn that the sun had not yet learned to burn off. The last run stood on the clerk’s board in square chalk, nearer every morning, and he had stopped pretending not to see it. He walked up the lane under the unlit lanterns with the cold coming off the water behind him and the loaf pulling at his arms, and he found that his heart was going as it went in fog, steady and hard, with nothing in front of the bow.

The shop was closing. The shutter was half down and the lamp turned low, and she was on her knees behind the counter sweeping flour into a pan, with her sleeves pushed up and a smear of it across her brow. She looked up at the bell. He stood in the doorway with the cloth held out before him like an offering in a poor harbor temple, and he could not think of one word.

“You are late,” said Sarah.

“I know.” It came out rougher than he meant. “Nell told me afterward. I am sorry. I should have known.”

“Nobody expected you to know.” She had risen to her feet with the pan still in her hand. Her gaze went to the cloth, and to the shape under it, and back to his face, and he watched her read the whole thing there: the three evenings, the flour in his hair, the sheer folly of it. Her mouth began to move. “Jack. Is that a loaf?”

“It is meant to be.”

“Did you make it?”

“I did.” He held it out. “It is not good.”

She put down the pan. She came around the counter and took the cloth from him with both hands, and the weight of it dragged her wrists down, and she said “Oh,” in a small high voice, and then she began to laugh.

It was not the laugh he knew. He had heard her laugh behind the counter at Odalys, and over the rail with the passengers, a warm quick sound. This one came up out of her helplessly, and went on, and she had to set the loaf down on the counter with a crack like a dropped oar to keep from dropping it on her foot. She laughed with her forehead on her arm. She laughed until she wheezed.

“Is it,” she managed, “is it a loaf or is it a ballast stone?”

“I thought it would do for either.”

That finished her. He stood in the wreck of his own dignity with his ears burning and found that a knot in the middle of his chest had come loose and was rising.

“Come upstairs,” she said at last, wiping her eyes with a floury wrist. “I have a knife that has seen worse. And I have tea. Come up. Bring your loaf.”

He had never been above the shop. The stair went up behind a door at the back, steep and narrow and smelling of woodsmoke, and she went before him with the lamp, and at the top there was a single long room under the slope of the roof. A bed in the far corner with a patched quilt. A stove, ticking as it cooled. A shelf of stained cards weighted down with a flatiron, and a small square table under the window with two chairs drawn up to it, so close together that their legs overlapped beneath. He ducked under the beam. It was the smallest room in which he had ever stood with her, and it held her whole life in it, and he did not know where to look.

She set the lamp on the shelf. She put the loaf on the table between the chairs and went for the bread knife.

The knife did not go in. She set the edge to the crust and leaned, and it skated off with a shriek and rapped the table. She tried again at an angle. She tried a sawing stroke, with her tongue between her teeth. The loaf sat there and let her.

“It is armored,” she said, in a voice of deep reverence.

“It is the fourth attempt. I turned the oven up.”

“Jack Mercer, you have made a lifeboat.”

He laughed. It came out of him before he knew it was coming, a short astonished bark of a sound that he did not recognize as his own, and then it would not stop. He sat down hard in the nearest chair. She sat in the other, because the table was too small for any other arrangement, and he felt her knee come against his under the boards, and neither of them moved it away. He had laughed like that perhaps twice in his life. His ribs ached. He wiped his face. When he looked up she was watching him over the loaf with her whole face lit, laughing and tender at once, and the old fear that lived under his breastbone turned over in its sleep and did not wake.

“There,” said Sarah, softly, when they had both come down. “That is better.”

She took the knife by the back and brought it down on the crust like a hammer. It split with a groan. She worked a wedge free of the heel, charred black along the outside and pale and raw at the heart, and she turned it over in her fingers and said, “I will go first, since you look as though you might be ill.”

“Do not,” said Jack. “It is more salt than bread.”

“I should like to know.” She put it in her mouth.

He watched her chew. It took a while. She chewed with great seriousness, her lashes lowered, her jaw working, and the lamp lay on her cheek, and he thought, I have poisoned her, and was ready to rise. Then she stopped. She did not swallow. Her eyes came up and met his, and what was in them was not what he had braced for.

“Oh,” said Sarah.

“It is terrible.”

“It is dreadful.” Her voice had gone odd. “It is burnt on the outside and raw in the middle and you used a fistful of salt.” She swallowed, and her gaze had gone bright. “Jack. It tastes of nothing but you.”

He did not understand. He reached across and broke off a corner for himself, because a man could not sit and be told a thing like that, and put it in his mouth.

Char. Salt, a great deal of it. The grainy raw heart. All that was true, and it was plain on the tongue, and then it was not what he tasted at all. Under the char there ran a warmth he had not put there on purpose. It was the thing he had thought of for three evenings at Odalys’s table with his sleeves rolled and his jaw set and flour to the elbows, and he had not once let it into his mind by the front door. It had come in anyway, by the hands. It tasted like a lit window at the end of a long lane. It tasted of a woman laughing at a counter with flour on her forehead. He searched it for the fear as a man probes a channel for a snag, because the fear was always there in everything he made, and he could not find it. There was no undertow in it anywhere. It was only love, plain and unmixed and entirely without skill, and he sat with the thing in his mouth and could not swallow.

“It should not do that,” said Sarah. She was whispering. “Only my hands do that. It is the one rule I have lived by.”

“I did not know it could.”

“I know you did not.”

The lamp hissed softly. Outside, a lantern had come on along the street, one of Gideon’s, and its yellow lay across the table and the wreck of the loaf and the two of them sitting with their knees together. He became aware of it all at once, the way a man wakes aboard to the knowledge that the engine has stopped. The room had grown very small. Her knee against his was a warm bright line from the joint to the shin, and he felt it in the back of his neck. She had put down the knife. Her hands lay on either side of the broken loaf, and her fingers were not quite still.

She leaned forward. It was not much. It was an inch, perhaps, the lean of a woman reaching to look more closely at a thing she had been given. But the table was a small one, and the inch closed the width of it between them, and he saw the flour still on her brow and the faint tremor at the corner of her mouth, and the very fine stir of her breath against the space between their faces.

He did not move. He locked his hands on the table’s edge, one on either side, until the knuckles went bone-white and the old rope scar burned across the left. He did not look at her mouth. He looked at a point on the wood beside her wrist where a knot ran through the grain, and fixed his gaze there with all the strength he had, and it was no use at all.

Her breath caught.

He heard it. It was a small soft hitch in the air, as when a swimmer comes up from under. And in the very next moment his own caught, and the two sounds lay against each other in the little room, one and then the other, like a pair of stuttering engines coming into step. She raised her eyes. They were very near. They were grey in the lamplight and perfectly steady and full of the whole question, and he knew that if he lowered his head the breadth of one hand, all of it would be answered.

He had asked her twenty small questions in a summer. He had asked her whether she took sugar. This was the twenty-first, and it was not small, and it came up out of him on its own.

“Sarah.” His voice cracked in the middle of her name. “May I,” he said, and got no further.

Outside the window the street lantern guttered. The flame leaned over, sank to a blue bead, and held there, shaking, as if the whole street had drawn breath.

She did not say yes. She did not have to. She made a small sound in her throat, and her glance dropped to his mouth and came back, and her chin lifted by the width of a thread.

A fist knocked at the door below.

It was three sharp raps, bright and unhurried, and a voice came up the stairwell after them like a gull coming in over the pier. “Uncle! Odalys says the pie is on the table, and the pie is not going to wait, and if the pair of you are not at the Lamplight before it is cold she will come and fetch you herself. And she says she will not knock.”

They came apart as if the chairs had been struck. His knee hit the underside of the table, and the whole table jumped, and the broken loaf, which had not moved for any force in Larkmere, rocked once on its side and settled. Sarah was on her feet with her hand flat at the base of her throat. He was on his, with his head an inch from the beam and his fists still closed, and for the length of several breaths neither of them spoke or looked at the other. She was shaking. He could see it in her fingers. He was shaking too, a long fine tremor that ran from his shoulders to his boots, like a hull that has taken a sea it was not built for and is still ringing from it.

On the stair, brisk boots went down, and a cheerful whistle receded along Lantern Street until the dark took it.

On the table the loaf lay split open under the lamp, the char black and the heart pale, as cold and hard as it had come from the oven. Sarah put out two fingers to it, very slowly, as if she did not trust her own hand. She touched the broken crumb.

It was warm.
