The taste had no business lasting past the landing.

Jack took the Heron out under a sky the color of old pewter, both hands on the tiller, thumbs settled into the shallow grooves that years of crossings had worn there. The water lay low and cold beneath the bow. A thin wind came off the far shore and found the gap at his collar, and he let it. There were worse things than cold, and cold at least had the decency to say what it was.

Honey first. Then salt. Then the quiet that comes after a door has closed.

He swallowed, though his mouth was empty. The taste stayed where it was.

He had eaten the loaf on the morning run, on the bench beside the wheelhouse, with the passengers going about their business and the linen open across his knee. A small piece to begin with. It had been enough to stop him chewing. The second piece confirmed what the first had told him, and by the time the Heron reached the far landing the loaf was half gone and he had folded the cloth into his jacket pocket with a care he would not have admitted to.

That ought to have been the end of it. Bread was bread. A good loaf could hold a man’s attention through a meal, perhaps two. It could not follow him across open water, settle behind his ribs, and ride there while he steered a channel he knew better than his own doorstep.

It was not only honey and salt. Under them lay a long table with one place laid at the far end of it, and tea gone cold in a cup nobody had lifted, and a key turning in a lock with no one on the other side of the door to hear it. He had never seen that room. He knew it anyway, the way a man knows a stretch of shore from the water, by the shape of what is missing from it.

The Heron came up on the far landing with the current easy under her hull. Nell was on the planks before the bow touched, the first line already in her fist. She made it fast with two turns and a hitch, tested it with her thumb, and looked at the sky and then at him.

“You’re early.”

“The current’s kind.”

“It wasn’t kind on the way out.”

“It changed.”

“Water does that.”

She said it flat, with no smile anywhere in it. Jack stepped past her to check the forward line himself. The knot was sound. He tugged it once, and then again, though it had held the first time.

Nell made a small noise through her nose. She was good at that. She could deliver a whole paragraph without opening her mouth.

“Have you checked the stern line?” he asked.

“Before we left.”

“Check it again.”

She stared at him. Her face was still young enough that surprise showed before she could stop it. Then she walked aft, gave the line a hard pull, and came back wearing the expression of a woman who had been asked to count the water in a lake.

“There,” she said. “It remains a line.”

“Good.”

He went into the wheelhouse to enter the crossing in the log. The pencil lay beside the brass compass, sharpened to a narrow point, and he wrote the usual marks, nothing worth keeping in his head, and set the pencil down parallel to the edge of the shelf. The taste of honey rose again when he bent over the page. He straightened and waited for it to pass, as he would have waited out a squall, and it did not pass.

It was possible the bread had simply been very good. The crust had broken clean and the crumb held together without turning heavy, and he had eaten worse in his life and called it supper. A man could remember a good meal. There was no mystery in that.

But a baker’s bread had no business telling him anything about a woman he had never spoken to. He had seen Sarah Quill from across the landing and then at the foot of the gangway, where she had held the bundle out in both hands as though it might break, her thumbs pressed over the knot of the twine and her shoulders square. She had not asked him to say anything. She had stood very still and let him take it, and he had been grateful for that. He was not grateful for how well he remembered it.

The next crossing was not due for the better part of an hour. He checked the lines. He checked the fuel. He walked the deck from bow to stern and found every fitting where he had left it. Work was usually enough to quiet him, if he let it. He did not let it.

When the passengers began to gather he kept his eyes on their bags and their footing. He took the fare from a woman whose fingers were red with cold and waited while an old man handed a crate over the gap between the planks and the deck. He did not look up Lantern Street until the last of them had boarded. Then he did.

The bakery door was shut.

He cast off. Past the point the usual course ran straight and clean along the channel, and he had run it in fog and in hard rain and with ice gathering along the waterline. The route was not a question. He moved the tiller a fraction to the left anyway, and the bow swung toward the western shore.

The change was small enough that no passenger would have noticed it. The Heron slid along the long curve of the lake, and Lantern Street took a longer place in the view, stone fronts climbing in a row above the water. Jack watched the channel and told himself he was reading the current. A dark seam ran below the surface near the old boathouse, and he followed it for a while, a man with a good practical reason, until the shore came close enough that he had to correct.

When he glanced up again the bakery door was open.

A woman in a pale apron stood inside it, one hand on the door and the other holding a cloth. She was too far off for him to see her face. She looked down at the cloth and then back into the shop, and a moment later the doorway was empty.

His grip tightened on the tiller.

He had slowed the Heron by less than a breath. The crossing was safe. The passengers would reach the far shore on time, near enough. He could have named every practical reason for the turn, and not one of them explained why he had looked up at the exact moment the door opened.

The hull gave a low complaint under his boots.

The ferry did not argue with the weather and she did not argue with him, but she had never been patient with the difference between what a man said and what he carried. A passenger with a lie in his pocket made her drag. A man who kept his feelings lashed down as tight as a storm line made the water thicken around her bow. Jack had watched it happen to other people for years, with the polite interest of a man whose own cargo was in order.

He was not lying. He was looking at the shore because the shore was there.

The Heron slowed again.

The engine held its steady note. The water at the bow gathered and parted and gathered, as if the lake had grown a second skin overnight. He checked the marker. The depth was sound and the wind had not changed. He eased the tiller, then brought it back, and waited for the boat to answer, and she answered at last with a slow, reluctant slide.

Nell, who had been stacking spare fenders near the stern, came forward and stopped beside the wheelhouse door.

“Current again?”

“Yes.”

“Funny. It’s been running the same way since dawn.”

Jack kept his eyes on the water. “Then you can steer it next time.”

“I’d have to learn to stare at Lantern Street first.”

He turned his head.

Nell’s face was perfectly blank. It was the face she wore when she had tied a knot she knew he would inspect twice, or left a tool exactly where he would have to step around it. She did not grin. A grin would have been an admission.

“Keep your eyes on the deck,” he said.

“They are on the deck.”

“Then leave them there.”

She gave a small nod, solemn as a harbor clerk, and went back to the fenders. A moment later she began to hum. It was a single note, repeated under her breath with a pause between each repetition, and it was not a tune. It was a sound chosen to be heard and denied.

Jack could have told her to stop. He watched the water instead, and let the note travel the length of the deck.

At the far landing he brought the Heron in too carefully, and the bow came alongside without so much as a knock. Nell made the line fast. Jack checked her knot, though it was clean, and went below to look at the engine casing and found nothing wrong with it. The engine had run evenly all morning. He wiped his hands on a rag and stood a moment in the low space between the cylinders, listening to the lake tap against the planking on the other side.

The taste had thinned. It had not gone.

By the time the afternoon light began to flatten he had crossed twice more. On the second return he took the route past Lantern Street again and told himself the wind had shifted. On the third he held the Heron in the channel until the bakery came into view, and found his eyes moving to the window before he could stop them.

The door stayed shut.

He did not slow the ferry. He did not turn toward the shore. He held the tiller straight and watched the markers go by at their proper intervals, and he was proud of that, though pride had very little to do with it. The boat ran true. The passengers crossed. The work was done as it should be.

At the landing Nell coiled the line in neat, even loops, each one the size of the last. When she finished, she lifted the coil, set it down, and began again, making the loops neater.

“You’ll wear the rope out,” Jack said.

“I’m improving it.”

“It was fine.”

“Then it’ll be finer.”

He took the coil from her and hung it on its peg, and she let him. Her silence was louder than the humming had been.

“Have you got anything to say?” he asked.

“No.”

“Good.”

“Loudly, though.”

He looked at her. She looked back with a straight face that did not fool him for a moment.

He left her on deck and went into the wheelhouse. The late crossing had no passengers yet. The lake lay open before the bow, darkening from grey to green where the shadow of the shore reached it, and he rested both hands on the wheel and waited for the first person to board.

The taste returned as soon as he sat still. Honey. Salt. A long table, and a cup gone cold.

He knew how to leave people to their own crossings. He knew how to keep his hands on the wheel and his mouth shut, and there were good reasons for both, reasons he had learned young and kept in good repair. A ferryman could carry a person to the far shore. He could not decide what they did once they stepped off the gangway.

The thought should have settled him. It did not.

A small party boarded, talking about the wind. He took their fares and watched their feet cross the planks. Nell cast off, the Heron backed from the landing with her wake opening behind her, and he held her steady until the channel widened and let the bow take the long curve of the lake. Lantern Street fell away. The bakery window became a pale square, then a glint, then nothing he could tell from the other windows along the shore.

He kept his eyes on the water.

At the far landing the passengers went ashore. Nell made the ropes fast and went to check the deck. Jack closed the fuel hatch, wiped the wheel with a rag, and noticed that the light had gone blue at the edges. Across the lake the first harbor lanterns waited, dark in their glass.

He reached into his jacket for the pencil and found a small hard thing against the lining.

A crumb.

It must have caught in the fold when he put the linen away that morning. It lay in the corner of the pocket, no bigger than the nail of his little finger, with a fleck of crust still on one edge. He held it on the pad of his thumb. It was dry now and ordinary, a scrap that should have fallen on the deck and been carried off by the wind.

He could brush it away. The lake would take it, and the next crossing would be the next crossing.

He closed his fingers around it instead.

Nell came back along the deck with the empty coil on her arm. “Ready?”

“In a moment.”

Her eyes went to his closed hand, then to his face, and moved off again before he could decide whether she had seen anything.

“Of course,” she said, and went forward.

Jack opened his palm. The crumb lay pale against the rough lines of his skin. He could not taste it now. He could still remember the first bite, and the quiet that came after it, and the odd relief of having been understood by a thing that had no voice.

He put the crumb back in his pocket and pressed the cloth flat over it. Out past the stern the Heron shifted against her lines, and one small wave lifted her and set her down, and along the dark shore the harbor lanterns caught, one by one.
