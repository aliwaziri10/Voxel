The taste had no business lasting past the landing.

Jack Mercer took the Heron out under a sky the color of old pewter, both hands on the tiller, his thumbs set into the shallow grooves worn by years of crossings. The water lay low and cold beneath the bow. A thin wind came off the far shore and found the gap at his collar. He let it. There were worse things than cold, and cold at least told the truth.

The last passenger had stepped ashore. The line was coiled. Nell had gone forward to check the deck fittings, leaving him alone with the wheel and the taste of Sarah Quill’s bread.

Honey first. Then salt. Then the quiet after a door closed.

He swallowed, though there was nothing in his mouth. The taste remained.

He had eaten the loaf on the morning run, sitting on the bench beside the wheelhouse while the other passengers went about their business. He had taken a small piece to begin with. It had been enough to make him stop chewing. The second piece had only confirmed what the first had told him. By the time the Heron reached the far landing, the loaf was half gone and he had folded the linen carefully into his jacket pocket.

That should have been the end of it. Bread was bread. A good loaf could hold a man’s attention for a meal, perhaps two. It could not follow him over water, settle behind his ribs, and remain there while he steered through a familiar channel.

He eased the tiller to starboard. The Heron answered, her bow moving a little toward the pale line of the shore. The current had turned at the point. He could feel it under the hull, a slow pull from the narrows, and he adjusted without looking down at the water. His hands knew the work. They had known it long before he was trusted to make a crossing alone.

The taste stayed.

At the first buoy he glanced toward Lantern Street. From the water, the town seemed arranged in narrow layers: stone landing, low roofs, the inn’s dark timber front, then the slope of the street climbing between buildings. The bakery sat halfway up, tucked beside a shop with green shutters. Its front window caught the weak morning light. A pale square. Nothing more.

He looked back to the channel.

A man at the tiller had no use for staring at windows. The lake had its own demands. A gust could turn a small boat broadside. A hidden rock could scrape a hull open. A line left loose could catch beneath a boot. He had a list of things worth watching, and none of them stood behind glass on Lantern Street.

The Heron moved cleanly through the narrows. Jack held his course until the far landing came into view, then brought the ferry alongside with a measured turn. The ropes went over. Nell caught the first line and secured it with a quick pull, then checked the knot with her thumb.

“You’re early,” she said.

“The current’s kind.”

Nell looked at the water, then at the landing. “It wasn’t kind on the way out.”

“It changed.”

“Water does that.”

She said it without a smile. Jack stepped past her to check the forward line himself. The knot was sound. He tugged once, then again, though it had held the first time.

Nell made a small sound through her nose. She was good at that. She could say a whole paragraph without opening her mouth.

“Have you checked the stern line?” he asked.

“I checked it before we left.”

“Check it again.”

She stared at him. Her face was still young enough that surprise showed before she could hide it. Then she gave the line a hard pull, though both of them knew it was secure.

“There,” she said. “It remains a line.”

“Good.”

He went into the wheelhouse to enter the crossing in the log. The pencil lay beside the brass compass, sharpened to a narrow point. He wrote the usual marks, nothing worth keeping in his head, and set the pencil down parallel to the edge of the shelf. The taste of honey and salt rose again when he bent over the page.

He straightened.

It was possible the bread had been unusual. The crust had been properly baked, with a clean break and a crumb that held together without being heavy. He had eaten worse in his life and called it supper. A person could remember a good meal. There was no mystery in that.

But the taste had not been only honey or salt. There had been a cold room in it, though he had never seen the room. A chair pushed back from a table. A silence that had settled so thoroughly it seemed to have weight. He had known the feeling before. Not in the same shape, perhaps, but close enough that he had recognized it before he could tell himself not to.

He closed the log.

A baker’s bread should not be able to tell him anything about a woman he had never spoken to. He had seen Sarah Quill only from across the landing, and then at the foot of the gangway with the loaf in her hands. She had held it out as if the bundle were fragile. She had kept her thumbs pressed to the knot and her shoulders square. When he took it, she had not tried to make him talk.

He had been grateful for that. He was not grateful for the way he remembered it.

The next crossing was due before the light had changed much. He checked the lines, checked the fuel, and walked the deck from bow to stern. Each task was familiar enough to quiet a man who let it. He did not let it.

When the next passengers came aboard, Jack kept his eyes on their bags and their footing. He helped an older man lift a crate over the gangway, waited while a child climbed down from a cart, and took a fare from a woman whose fingers were red from the cold. He did not look up Lantern Street until the last person had boarded.

Then he did.

The bakery door was closed.

He turned the key, eased the Heron away from the landing, and held the usual course past the point. There was no reason to take the wider sweep around Willow Bend. The current ran clean near the bank, and the channel was clear. He had made this crossing in fog, in hard rain, with ice gathering along the hull. The route was not a question.

He moved the tiller a fraction to the left.

The bow angled toward the western shore.

The change was small enough that a passenger would not have noticed. The Heron slid along the curve of the lake, giving Lantern Street a longer place in the view. Jack watched the water ahead and told himself he was checking the current. A dark seam ran below the surface near the old boathouse. He followed it for a while, then corrected his line before the ferry came too close to shore.

When he glanced up again, the bakery door had opened.

A woman in a pale apron stood inside, one hand on the door, the other holding a cloth. She was too far away for him to see her face clearly. She looked down at the cloth, then back into the shop. A moment later she disappeared from the doorway.

Jack’s grip tightened on the tiller.

He had slowed the Heron by less than a breath. The crossing would still be safe. The passengers would still reach the far shore. No one had been put at risk. He could name every practical reason for the turn, and none of them explained why he had looked up at the exact moment the door opened.

The hull gave a soft complaint beneath him.

He returned to the channel.

The ferry was not a creature to be argued with. It carried people who said one thing while thinking another, and it had never been patient with the difference. When a passenger carried a lie across the water, the Heron dragged. When a man kept his feelings tied down as tightly as a storm line, the current seemed to thicken around the hull. Jack had watched it happen enough times to know the signs.

He was not lying. He was watching the shore because the shore was there.

The Heron slowed again.

The engine kept its steady note. The water at the bow gathered, parted, and gathered once more, as if the ferry had entered a stretch of thicker lake. Jack checked the channel marker. The depth was sound. The wind had not changed. He eased the tiller, then brought it back, waiting for the hull to answer.

It answered at last with a slow, reluctant slide.

Nell, who had been stacking the spare fenders near the stern, came forward and stopped beside the wheelhouse door.

“Current again?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Funny. It’s been moving the same way since dawn.”

Jack kept his gaze on the water. “Then you can steer it next time.”

“I’d have to learn to stare at Lantern Street first.”

He turned his head.

Nell’s expression was perfectly blank. She had the same look when she had tied a knot she knew he would inspect twice, or when she had left a tool exactly where he would have to step around it. She did not grin. That would have been an admission.

“Keep your eyes on the deck,” he said.

“They are on the deck.”

“Then leave them there.”

She gave a little nod, solemn as a harbor official, and went back to the fenders. A moment later she began to hum. Just one note, repeated under her breath, with a pause between each repetition. It was not a tune. It was a sound chosen to be heard and denied.

Jack could have told her to stop. Instead he watched the water and let the note travel over the deck.

At the far landing, he brought the Heron in too carefully. The bow came alongside without a knock. Nell caught the line and made it fast. Jack checked her knot, though it was clean, then went below to inspect the engine casing. He found nothing wrong. The engine had run evenly all morning. He wiped his hands on a rag and stood for a moment in the low engine room, listening to the water tap against the hull.

The taste had thinned. It had not gone.

By the time the afternoon light began to flatten, Jack had made the crossing twice more. On the second return, he took the route past Lantern Street again. He told himself it was because the wind had shifted. On the third, he kept the Heron in the channel until the bakery came into view, then found his gaze moving to the window before he could stop it.

The door stayed shut.

He did not slow the ferry. He did not turn toward the shore. He held the tiller straight and watched the channel markers pass at their proper intervals. He was proud of that much, though pride had little to do with it. The boat ran true. The passengers crossed. The work was done as it should be.

At the landing, Nell coiled the line in neat, even loops. She made each one the same size. When she finished, she lifted the coil and set it down, then began again, making the loops even neater.

“You’ll wear the rope out,” Jack said.

“I’m improving it.”

“It was fine.”

“Then it’ll be finer.”

He took the coil from her and hung it on the peg. She let him. Her silence was louder than the humming had been.

“Have you got anything to say?” he asked.

“No.”

“Good.”

“Loudly, though.”

He looked at her. She looked back with a straight face that did not fool him for a moment.

He left her on deck and went into the wheelhouse. The late crossing had no passengers yet. The lake lay open before the bow, darkening from grey to green where the shore shadow reached it. He rested both hands on the tiller and waited for the first person to board.

The taste returned as soon as he sat still.

Honey. Salt. A quiet room. He had no right to know the room, and no reason to imagine it. Yet the bread had left him with the certainty of a place where a person had learned to keep her voice low, her hands occupied, her wants folded out of sight. It was not a story he could tell. It was only a pressure behind the ribs, the same pressure he felt when a passenger stood at the rail after landing and looked back across the lake instead of going on with the rest of the town.

He knew how to leave people to their own crossings. He knew how to keep his hands on the wheel and his mouth shut. There were good reasons for both. A ferryman could carry people to the far shore. He could not decide what they did once they stepped off the gangway.

The thought should have settled him. It did not.

A small group boarded, talking about the wind. Jack took the fares and watched their feet as they crossed the planks. Nell cast off the lines. The Heron moved away from the landing, her wake opening behind her. Jack held the tiller steady until the channel widened, then allowed the bow to take the long curve of the lake.

Lantern Street receded. The bakery window became a pale square, then a glint, then nothing he could distinguish from the other windows along the shore.

He kept his eyes on the water.

At the far landing, the passengers went ashore. Nell secured the ropes and went to check the deck. Jack completed the usual work, closed the fuel hatch, and wiped the wheel with a cloth. The light had gone blue at the edges. Across the lake, the first harbor lanterns waited dark in their glass globes.

He reached into his jacket for the pencil and felt a small hard thing against the lining.

A crumb.

It must have caught in the fold when he had put the linen away that morning. It sat in the corner of his pocket, no larger than the nail of his little finger, with a fleck of crust along one edge. Jack held it on the pad of his thumb. It was dry now. Nothing special. A scrap that should have fallen onto the deck and been carried away by the wind.

He could brush it off. The water would take it, and the next crossing would be the next crossing.

Instead he closed his fingers around it.

Nell came back to the wheelhouse carrying the empty coil. “Ready?”

“In a moment.”

She looked at his closed hand, then at his face. Her eyes moved away before he could decide whether she had seen anything.

“Of course,” she said, and went forward.

Jack opened his palm. The crumb lay there, pale against the rough lines of his skin. He could not taste it now. He could still remember the first bite, the quiet that had followed, and the strange relief of being understood by a thing that had no voice.

He put the crumb back into his pocket and pressed the cloth flat over it.

Outside, the Heron shifted against her lines. One small wave lifted the stern and set it down again. The crumb stayed in his pocket as the harbor lanterns caught, one by one, along the dark shore.
