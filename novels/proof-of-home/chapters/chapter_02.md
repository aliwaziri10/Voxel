The ferry cut through water that had not yet forgotten winter. Jack Mercer held the tiller with both hands, his thumbs resting along the worn grooves where generations of Mercers had pressed their thumbs before him. The Heron responded to pressure the way a rested horse responds to reins. She knew the crossing. She knew the weight of his hands.

He could still taste the bread.

Three crossings now since the morning a stranger walked into the bakery on Lantern Street and bought a loaf still warm from the oven. Three crossings since he had stood at the rail with crumbs on his fingers and felt a tightness in his chest loosen that had held for years. The taste had been honey and salt and something quieter beneath them. Something that tasted like an empty chair at a long table.

The Heron's engine thrummed steady beneath his boots. The lake stretched gray-green to the far shore where the pines stood close against the sky. Early spring. The air carried snowmelt and the first green scent of budding willow along the banks. Gulls rode the thermals above the stern, crying without urgency.

Nell leaned against the starboard rail, her arms folded across her chest. Sixteen years old and already she read the water better than most men twice her age. Her dark hair blew across her face. She did not brush it away.

"Uncle Jack."

"Nell."

"You're doing it again."

"Doing what."

"Timing the crossing." She tilted her head toward the western shore where Lantern Street curved away from the water. "We passed the bakery dock two minutes ago. You slowed the Heron by three knots. I counted."

The tiller was warm from his grip. He did not loosen his fingers. "The current shifted near the point. You know that."

"I know the current. I also know you've taken the long way around Willow Bend four crossings running." She said it flat, without accusation. "Loudly, I might add. Very loudly. The quietest loudness I've ever managed."

A smile touched the corner of his mouth and died there. "You're imagining things."

"Am I." She pushed off the rail and moved toward the wheelhouse door. Her boots were quiet on the deck. She had his walk. "The bread was good, Uncle Jack. That's all I'm saying. Loudly. With great volume and no words at all."

She disappeared into the cabin before he could answer.

The Heron slipped past the old boathouse where the paint peeled in strips like birch bark. Jack adjusted the tiller a fraction, feeling the hull respond. The current here ran deep and cold, pulling toward the narrows where the lake pinched between cliffs. He knew every eddy. Every submerged rock. Every place where the water remembered a storm.

He had learned them all from the wheelhouse floor, small enough to hide behind the binnacle while his father steered. His mother had brought him lunch in a cloth bundle. She had sat beside him on the bench and pointed at the shoreline, naming each cove and inlet like she was teaching him a prayer.

The last time she made that crossing, she had not looked back.

The Heron shuddered. Not the engine. Something deeper. The hull groaned as the water grew thick around her, resistant. Jack's hands tightened on the tiller. He knew this feeling. The ferry did not carry lies. It had not carried his father's silence about the mortgage. It had not carried his mother's packed bag. It had not carried the letter she left on the kitchen table, written in ink that smudged where tears had fallen.

The Heron drifted slow and sulked when anyone aboard hid what they felt. The more they hid, the later the crossing.

Jack Mercer was not hiding. He was only curious. Curious about a baker who made bread that tasted like loneliness. Curious about a woman who had come to Larkmere with a suitcase and a promise to herself. One season. That was what Odalys Pike had told him over whiskey at the Lamplight, her eyes sharp in the firelight. One season and then she decides.

The Heron fought the current. The engine labored. Jack breathed through his nose and waited. The ferry would cross when it was ready. It always did.

Across the water, the harbor lanterns were unlit. Gideon Teague would climb the tower at dusk, his joints stiff with the damp, and light them one by one. The old man had known Fenna Quill for fifty years. He had watched Sarah grow from a child who stole biscuits from the cooling rack to a woman who carried her great-aunt's recipes in her fingers. Gideon said little. What he said tended to matter.

Jack had once asked him about the lanterns. The old man had squinted at the water and said, "Whoever stands at the same lantern when it catches is thinking of the same person. The lantern doesn't choose. It only shows."

Jack had not asked what happened if only one person stood there.

The Heron surrendered to the current and slid forward again. The wheelhouse walls held the scent of diesel and pine tar and something older. Salt. Time. The ghost of his mother's perfume, faint as foam on a wave.

He thought of the bread. He thought of the woman who had shaped it. Her hands in flour. The warmth of an oven opening. The way she had looked at him across the counter — brief, measuring, as if she knew the weight of what she had made and wondered if he could carry it.

A stranger noticed her. The words from Fenna's recipe book, written in the margin beside a formula for honey-oat loaves. *A stranger noticed her and the dough remembered.*

Jack had read the line three times before he understood it was not instruction. It was warning.

The Heron reached the far dock. Ropes snapped taut. Nell moved without being told, her body knowing the rhythm. She caught the line thrown from shore. She made the fast. She did not look at the bakery. She did not look at him.

"Evening crossing in an hour," she called up to the wheelhouse. "I'll check the fuel."

"Check the lines first."

"Lines are fine. I made them fine."

"Check them again."

She snorted. The sound carried affection she would never name. "Yes, Uncle Jack."

He watched her walk the dock, her shoulders square against the wind. She was growing tall. Her mother would have been proud. His sister would have worried. The Mercer women worried. The Mercer men stayed and steered.

Jack left the wheelhouse and stood at the stern rail. The lake spread before him, flat as a held breath. Lantern Street curved along the shore, its windows catching the last pale light. The bakery window was dark. The oven would be cold now. Sarah Quill would be upstairs in the flat above the shop, reading her great-aunt's margins by lamplight. Learning the language of small marks.

He wondered if she tasted loneliness in her own bread. He wondered if she knew she was broadcasting it across the water to a ferryman who had no business noticing.

The Heron rocked gently at her lines. The water slapped the hull. Somewhere a loon called, the sound traveling far in the spring evening.

Jack Mercer gripped the rail. He told himself it was only bread. Flour and water and honey and salt. Chemistry and heat. The taste in his fingers was memory. The ache in his chest was the season turning.

He did not ask her to stay. He would not. His mother had left on this ferry when he was a boy, and he had learned the lesson in his bones: wanting someone to stay was the same as trapping them. The Heron kept its people. It did not hold them.

The first harbor lantern caught fire on the tower. Then the second. Their light spread across the water, golden pins in the gathering dark.

Jack stood at the stern and watched the bakery window. He did not move. The current pulled at the ferry's lines. The engine cooled behind him, ticking. The taste of honey and salt and loneliness lingered on his fingers, and he let it.
