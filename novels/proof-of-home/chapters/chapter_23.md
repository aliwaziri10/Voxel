Her hands would not forget the stair.

They had carried a tin lantern all the way down it, and they had kept every step of that walk the way a crock keeps the smell of whatever was last baked in it. She knew the shape of a back that was not hers. She knew a wall of coat and breastbone, and a breath that rose and fell behind her shoulders, and the grip of one hard hand on an iron rail that she had felt along the whole length of her spine without once turning round to look at it. She had hung the lantern on its hook. She had said goodnight to a flame. And then she had walked home with her arms held a little way out from her sides, like a woman carrying a bowl filled to the brim.

By afternoon the wax had been scraped off the pier rail, and white curls of it lay in the gutters, and Larkmere had agreed without a word to behave as though it had never been out on the harbor lanes at all. Sarah went about her work like everyone else. She weighed flour. She fed the starter. She slapped the first dough of the day onto the table and laid her palms on it, and the dough took the stair out of her hands and gave it straight back, a little warmer than before.

“No,” she told it. “Not at the table.”

Odalys had come at first light and eaten a roll standing at the back door with her eyes shut, and gone away humming without asking a single question, which was worse than a hundred of them. The morning loaves had gone out into the town with a faint flush on the crust, nothing a customer would have named. Only the people who bought them stood a moment longer at the counter and looked out of the window toward the water, and went off smiling at nothing.

She could not bake it out. Calm hands made calm bread; that was the rule under every one of Fenna’s cards, and for most of the day she tried to make her hands calm by keeping them busy, and they would not be quieted. They wanted to be held. It was as plain as that, and as foolish, and she was a grown woman with a bakery and a standing order to fill.

In the late afternoon she shaped the loaf for the far shore, the one that crossed on the dusk run, and she shaped it slowly, because she knew what her hands would put into it. She turned it in her palms. She pressed the seam shut with her thumbs. She set it to prove and stood watching it with flour to the wrists, and she said to the empty shop what she had not been able to say on the stair.

“I am not going to hide it.”

It was a small, daft thing to say to a lump of dough. But the dough rose the moment she said it, quick and sure, a full finger’s width in the time it took her to draw breath, and she had to laugh out loud. It came out of the oven golden and plain, and it sat on the cooling rack as ordinary as a stone. She wrapped it in the blue cloth when it was cold. It was the plain cool of any loaf, and she was glad of that. She wanted this one honest. She did not want to carry more than she had agreed to.

The dusk crossing was the quiet one. It was the last of the day and seldom full, and that evening there were six passengers all told, three fishermen with their nets and three women with baskets, who settled themselves forward in the bow with their backs to the stern and their hats pulled low against the last of the light. “Evening, baker,” said the oldest of the fishermen, and she said evening, and he did not say another word. Sarah took the long bench at the stern. She laid the wrapped loaf across her knee and her hands on the loaf.

Nell was not on the deck. “Nell has a cough,” said Jack, and cast off the stern line himself, and told it to the rope. Sarah had seen Nell at noon outside the chandler’s, loud and pink and glowing with health, haggling over a coil of cord. She thought about that, and about how grateful she intended to be, and said only, “I am sorry to hear it.”

He took the Heron out from the pier. The lamps along the rail slid away behind them, a hundred small flames in a row, and the black water gave each of them back upside down and let them go. The engine settled to its low note. She had learned that note by now, the way she knew the sound of her own oven coming up to heat, and she listened to it while the harbor shrank to a bright seam along the dark.

Then he did a thing she had never seen him do. He took a turn of line around the wheel, made it fast, and left it.

The Heron did not seem to mind. She went on, a little slower, as a horse goes on when the rider has dropped the reins and the road is one she knows. Jack walked down the length of the deck with his arms loose at his sides, past the backs of the fishermen, who did not turn, and stopped at the end of the stern bench. He did not sit. He stood with his cap crushed in one fist and the last of the sun behind him, and she could not see his face.

“It is a long crossing tonight,” he said.

“It always is, at this hour.”

“No.” His voice was dry and very low. “Tonight it will be longer.”

He sat. He took the far end of the bench, a full hand’s width from her, and put his eyes on the shore ahead. His hands lay on his knees. She watched them, because they were the only thing she could allow herself to watch. They were big, scarred across the knuckles and brown from the weather, with the thin white seam of an old rope burn across the left, and they were not quite still.

“May I?” he said.

It was one more small question in a summer that had been built of them. Did she take sugar? Had the gate been mended? He had asked her a great many little things and never the large one, and this was not the large one either. It was the smallest of them all, and she thought it might be the heaviest he had ever put to her.

“Yes,” said Sarah.

He did not look at her. He turned his right hand over on his knee, palm up, the way a man sets down a thing he has no intention of pressing, and he waited. It took her a long moment to understand that he was waiting. When she did, she lifted her hand off the loaf, and the hand shook, and she put it into his before it could think better of it.

His fingers closed.

It was nothing like the stair. The stair had been a thing that happened to her, a crowd’s doing, and she had been carried along inside it. This was done. She had lifted her hand herself and set it down herself, and nobody had pushed her, and the difference went through her like heat through an iron pan: slowly, and then all at once, from the palm outward. His hand was warm and dry and hard from rope, as warm as the side of a loaf a minute out of the oven. It covered hers entire. She could feel the ridge of each callus and the pulse in the heel of his thumb, quick and heavy against her wrist, and she understood with a lurch that it was beating at the same pace as her own, or so near that she could not tell which was which.

She kept her eyes on the far shore.

The bench was narrow. For one breath she thought he had kept a gap between them. Then the Heron leaned, a long slow lean to one side as she came round on the lake’s own swell, and the gap closed, and his thigh lay against hers from hip to knee, warm through two layers of cloth. Neither of them moved to open it again.

It struck her, absurdly, that she had been pressed against this man by a market-day crowd and a summer downpour and a stair full of lanterns, and had not once until tonight been given his hand to hold. It was the wrong way round. Everything in Larkmere seemed to happen to the two of them in the wrong order, a little too late, and with an audience.

Straight ahead lay the far shore, a low dark line with a single lamp lit on the landing, small and yellow and a great way off. She fixed her eyes on that. It was the only safe thing in the world to look at. If she turned her head she would see his profile and the line of his jaw and the pulse going in his throat, and she did not trust what her face would do. Beside her, she felt him do the same. She felt it as plainly as the last sun on her cheek: the stiffness of a man looking hard at one point so as not to look at another.

On her knee the loaf began to warm.

She felt it through the blue cloth first, and then through her skirt, a slow gathering of heat in the crumb as though it had just been drawn from the oven. It was a ridiculous thing for bread to do. She had not put this into it; the shaping was hours behind her. But the loaf had been shaped by a baker who had promised not to hide, and it seemed to know that she had kept the promise.

The engine note dropped. It dropped again. The Heron slowed under them, not a stall, only a lengthening, as if the boat had decided the lake was wider than it had been that morning. Forward, one of the women in the bow began to hum, low and tuneless, and did not turn her head. The far lamp did not seem to grow at all.

His thumb moved once across her knuckles.

It was the smallest motion she had ever felt, a single slow stroke from the bone of one finger to the next, and she stopped breathing for the length of it. Her free fingers closed on the edge of the bench until the grain bit in.

“Tell me if you want me to stop,” he said to the water.

“I do not.” It came out so fast that it was nearly a laugh, and nearly a sob. “I do not want that at all.”

He said nothing. But the hand around hers tightened, once, and held, and she heard him let out a breath he had plainly been keeping since the pier.

The last run of the season stood on the harbor clerk’s board in square chalk, and everyone in Larkmere knew the day of it. She had promised herself one season. She had promised herself that when it ended she would know whether she was staying, and she had told him she would say so to his face. She did not know. She knew only what her palm knew, and it knew nothing but the weight of his, and she let the thought of the board go by her like a cloud over a loaf in the window, there and then past. She thought of the first loaf, and how she had stood on the dock and watched him eat it on this very deck with his eyes shut, and she thought: I am in the picture now.

Nobody spoke. The lamps on the pier behind them dwindled to a handful of sparks, and the light went out of the sky in long bands of rose and ash, and the lake lay flat and dark and very large on every side. The Heron went on at a walk. She could not have said how long the crossing took. The bread was warm on her knee and the hand was warm in hers, and between the two of them there was no room left for anything cold.

The far lamp grew at last. The landing came up out of the dusk, planks and posts and a rope ring green with weed, and the Heron slid in against it with a long sigh of fenders. The engine died to a mutter. The fishermen got to their feet and stretched, and the women gathered their baskets, and there was the creak of boards forward as the plank went down.

He let go.

He did it slowly, finger by finger, the way she lifted a cloth from a loaf so that the crust would not tear. The cool air came in over her palm. It was absurd. It was the same air that had been there all the evening. But she felt the loss of him in the middle of her palm like a limb gone, the shape of his palm still printed on her own, and she curled her fingers against it and they would not close.

On her knee the loaf let go of its warmth by degrees, as an oven loses heat with the door left open, until it was the plain cool bread she had wrapped that afternoon.

Across the bench Jack sat with his eyes on the planks and his empty hand lying on his knee. As she watched, the fingers closed slowly on nothing, and held the nothing very tightly, and did not open again.
