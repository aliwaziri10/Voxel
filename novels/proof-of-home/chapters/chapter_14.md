The wind came up hard enough to lift the sails and throw the Heron sideways, and Sarah felt the deck tilt beneath her feet before she understood why.

The morning was clear and bright, a bright early summer light that made the water gleam like hammered copper, but a change gathered from the west, tugging at the rigging with a patience that knotted her stomach. Too far forward, she found her balance compromised by the abrupt blast, and her foot slipped into thin air.

Her forearm pressed against the rail, and the metal sighed under her weight. The Heron shuddered, the bow dipping, and the deck abruptly pitched forward, one rail slipping from her grip. She landed on the planking with a soft thump, her hands reaching the edge of the loaf she carried. The loaf still warm from the oven rested in her arms, preserving the shape of the dough she had just worked, and she sensed a faint pulse of her intent still coursing through it, though the motion had slowed almost to stillness.

Sarah lifted her gaze. The deck teemed with travelers—fishermen returning from the lake and merchants bound for the market—but none seemed aware of the sudden tilt. The wind had seized the sail, and the boat began to turn, water striking the hull with a sound like distant murmurs. A shout mentioned a storm drifting in from the north, yet the voice vanished against the wind. Then Jack appeared beside her.

He stepped next to her, his boots finding steadiness on the tilted deck with a quiet certainty that caught her breath. His hand closed around her forearm, firm and warm, just enough to steady her, just enough to pull her upright. He said nothing; he simply held her there, his chest pressed gently against the curve of her shoulder, his other arm reaching to support her elbow. The boat rocked, the deck pitching them both into a rhythm that matched the wood's creak.

She met his eyes. His face remained unreadable in the shifting light, but she saw tension in his jaw, the way his eyes lingered on her mouth. He knew what she was doing; he always did. The loaf in her grip grew heavy with a rising sense of longing she could not quite name, a feeling that had been gathering since the day she inherited the shop from her great‑aunt Fenna, since the day she realized that the breads she baked carried whatever she felt while shaping them. The oven's heat had become part of her, the warmth seeping into her bones and leaving a mark no washing could erase.

His hand stayed on her forearm, his chest against her shoulder, and she felt the steady pressure holding her upright. Neither of them spoke the word to release. The wind grew stronger, and the Heron lurched again, tilting the deck farther, the rail slipping from her grip. Jack shifted his weight, his free hand coming to rest lightly on the railing's edge, and she noticed the narrow space between them, the wood forming just inches of separation between their skins. That proximity measured four points out of ten, a closeness that demanded constant vigilance, a closeness that made her hold her breath and keep her eyes wide.

The boat drifted on the water. The wind kept blowing. Somewhere between them, the loaf began to change, the crust cracking a little under the pressure of her hands, the dough inside shifting as if listening to the unspoken conversation above them. She sensed the alteration, the subtle shift in texture, the way the bread was starting to lose its containment. It was small, barely noticeable, but it sent a ripple through her that had nothing to do with weather and everything to do with the promise she had made months ago. One season. That was all she had agreed to, one season of trying, of seeing what came next, of deciding whether to stay or go.

She glanced at his mouth. The line of his lips was thin, his expression neutral, but she could see the faint hint of his tongue, the way his eyes held something that might have been recognition or something else entirely. The bread in her hands grew pale, its color fading as the emotion inside it began to surface, and she wondered if he could see it too, if he could sense the longing that lived in the folds of her palms.

The Heron turned again, the water smoothing beneath them as the wind eased slightly, but the boat remained tilted, the deck still threatening to slip away. Jack's grip on her forearm did not loosen; his chest stayed against her shoulder, his presence a steadying force that made her want to lean into it, to let him hold her a little longer. She wanted to say something, to break the spell with words, but the bread in her hands was already changing, and she knew that once the feeling became public, once it entered the town's memory, she would never be able to recall it.

Fine, cold rain began to fall, running down the boat's sides and pooling around their feet. The wind died completely, leaving silence thick and wet. Sarah stood very still, her forearm still wrapped in Jack's grip, and she watched the rain streak across the water and thought of the last time she had touched him on purpose. She had reached for his hand in the shop that morning, before the inheritance was officially settled, and he had pulled back without a word, his fingers brushing hers like a question. She had let go immediately, embarrassed by the intimacy of the gesture, and he had nodded once and returned to his work on the sails. That had been weeks ago. Now the rain fell and the boat rocked, Jack holding her upright while the wind tried to tear them apart, and she understood that this was no coincidence. The sea was doing what the sea did, the weather was doing what the weather did, and somehow those forces were aligning with the bread in her hands and the promise she had made to herself.

She could feel the bread crumbling slightly, the layers beginning to separate, and she knew that soon it would be impossible to hide what it contained. The longing was growing; she felt it in the way her heart hammered against her ribs, in the tightness of her throat, in the tremor of her hands that made the dough slump. The first kiss had been planned for chapter twenty-seven, and until then the ache had been building in small increments, each one smaller than the last, each one waiting for the right moment to break loose. This moment was different; this was the instant when the bread would spill its contents onto the table, when the feeling would become visible to everyone who ate it, when the secret she had guarded for months would finally be shared. And yet she did not move. She did not let go. The rain continued to fall, and the wind died, and the Heron drifted on its slow course across the darkening water, Jack holding her forearm and his chest at her shoulder, neither of them speaking. The loaf in her hands was becoming something else, something that no longer belonged solely to her, something that would soon be shared with strangers and friends and neighbors, something that would carry the weight of every unspoken word and hidden feeling.

She could sense the bread giving way, the crust breaking, the dough inside shifting and settling into a new configuration. It was beautiful in the way that things are when they are about to reveal themselves, fragile and precious and utterly vulnerable. And she understood, with a clarity that cut through the rain and the tilt of the boat, that this was exactly where she needed to be—not at the top of the hill, not in the safety of the bakery, not in the comfort of a life she had chosen before she was ready for it. Here, on this tilted deck, with Jack's hand on her arm and the rain falling and the bread changing beneath her fingers, she was exactly where she was meant to be.

The wind picked up again, just enough to send another shudder through the vessel, and she felt the deck tilt once more, the rail sliding from her grip. Jack adjusted his stance, shifting his weight to keep her balanced, and his chest pressed closer to hers. She could smell the salt on his skin, the familiar scent of the lake and the boats and the work he did each day. It grounded her, anchored her, made the tilt less frightening.

She looked at his mouth again. There was something there, a question or a promise, and she realized that she had been waiting for this moment for so long that she had forgotten how to breathe without hoping for it. The bread in her hands was nearly done, the final layer of crust collapsing inward, and she felt the truth of it spreading through her like a warmth that had nothing to do with the oven. The longing was no longer a feeling she carried alone; it had become part of the bread itself, part of the dough and the rise and the fold, and now it was going to be shared.

Jack's hand remained on her forearm, his chest at her shoulder, and she stood very still, letting the moment hold her, letting the bread in her hands transform into something that would soon be eaten and spoken of and remembered.

She did not let go.
