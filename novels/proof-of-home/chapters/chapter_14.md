The wind came up hard enough to turn the Heron off her line. Jack felt the pull through the tiller before the passengers reacted. He eased the throttle and corrected the bow, watching the west side of the channel where gusts broke the water into short, uneven ridges. The first correction held, but the next wave struck the hull at an angle and pushed the stern toward the shallows. Jack turned the tiller a fraction, waited for the bow to answer, then brought the engine up just enough to keep steerage. The marker buoy rose and fell beyond the rail. He knew the channel well, yet he checked the distance to the shallows twice before looking toward the passengers. The Heron was still under control. He needed to keep it that way.

The morning had begun clear, with early summer light lying across the lake. Now a bank of cloud pressed over the western shore, darkening the water from west to east. The wind pulled at the rigging and sent spray across the deck. Jack checked the marker buoy, then the gap between the Heron and the shallows. The current was pushing harder than it had on the outward run. He eased the throttle and called for the passengers to stay seated. The boat was not in danger yet, but he could feel how quickly the gusts were building. He had crossed this lake in worse weather; that did not make it sensible to ignore the first warning.

Sarah’s forearm struck the rail as the deck tilted beneath her. She caught herself, but the basket in her arms shifted, and her footing went. Jack moved before he had time to call her name. He caught her forearm and drew her upright, bracing his own stance against the next roll.

The passengers shifted and caught at the rail. A fisherman swore as his bucket slid across the boards; Nell caught it with her boot and pushed it back toward him. “Keep clear of the side,” Jack called. A woman gathered her shawl around her shoulders and moved toward the wheelhouse. The fisherman reached for his bucket, but the deck rolled again and he had to grab the rail. Jack watched each movement without turning his head for long. He needed the channel, the passengers, and the wind in view at once. Nell met his eye and nodded; she had the loose gear under control. He kept both hands on the tiller and waited for the bow to answer the next correction.

His hand closed around her forearm, firm enough to hold her upright. He brought his chest to her shoulder to keep both of them balanced, his free hand reaching for the rail. The wet sleeve slid under his palm before he found a secure grip. He could feel the tension in her arm through the cloth, the effort she made to keep her feet planted. He should have released her as soon as she was steady. The boat shifted again, and he kept hold while he braced his boots against the deck.

The wind struck the sail with a flat crack. Jack shifted his weight and felt Sarah’s arm tighten beneath his hand. He kept his stance wide, one boot braced against the raised edge of the deck, his free hand gripping the rail. He had brought people through worse weather. He knew the moment to let a gust pass and the moment to correct the tiller. He had never had to do it while another person’s pulse beat against his fingers.

“Keep your feet under you,” he said.

“I am.”

Her answer was steady, though he felt her trying to find a position that did not press her closer to him. There was no room for it. The deck tilted again, and she caught the side of his coat with her free hand. The grip was brief, meant only to keep her upright. Jack felt it through the wet fabric and had to remind himself that she was balancing, not reaching for him.

He looked over her shoulder toward the marker buoy. It had slid too far to the right. He needed to bring the bow around before the current carried them out of the channel. His hand remained on Sarah’s arm while he made the correction with his free hand. The tiller resisted, then came back. The Heron began to turn.

Sarah looked up at him again. Her gaze moved from his eyes to his mouth, and his thoughts stopped as cleanly as the engine might stop if he cut the throttle. He could feel the rain on his face, the rail under his palm, the pulse beneath his fingers. He knew he should look away. He did, but not before he had seen the question in her expression.

He had no answer that would not change the crossing. He could not kiss her. He could not pretend the look had passed unnoticed. He held her steady and waited for the boat to find its line.

A wave slapped the hull and sent spray over Sarah’s shoulder. Jack shifted his stance to keep her from striking the rail again, then looked past her to the marker. The basket had slipped lower against her coat. He could not reach for it without loosening his hold on her arm, so he braced his knee against the bench and waited for the deck to level.

The Heron slipped off course. Jack saw the marker buoy slide sideways and corrected the bow, keeping one hand on Sarah’s forearm until the deck steadied. Her eyes dropped to his mouth, then lifted to his face. He could not tell whether she was frightened or waiting for him to act, and he would not guess while he still had hold of her.

The loaf was trapped between them. Its crust cracked beneath the cloth, releasing the smell of warm honey into the rain. Sarah’s fingers tightened around the basket. Jack felt the movement through her sleeve, checked the marker, and brought the ferry back into the channel with both hands on the tiller.

When the wind eased, he released her. Sarah stepped clear and adjusted the damp cloth over the crushed loaf. He wanted to apologize for the bread and explain why he had held her, but neither seemed adequate. He checked the passengers, the rail, and the route before looking at her again. She was watching him, her expression unreadable.

He had spent years learning not to mistake silence for permission. He would not turn a frightened look into an answer just because he wanted one. Jack faced the channel and kept his hands on the tiller. Sarah stayed beside him, neither moving closer nor leaving.



The passengers had begun to speak again. A woman shook spray from her shawl and gathered it around her shoulders. The fisherman retrieved his bucket and gave Nell a nod of thanks. Two passengers compared the size of the waves they had seen beyond the marker. Jack heard them without taking part. He had released Sarah, but his fingers still remembered the pressure of her sleeve. He closed his hand around the tiller, checked the bow against the marker, and brought his attention back to the route.

“Are you steady?” he asked.

Sarah nodded. Her mouth opened as if she might answer, then she looked down at the basket. The cloth had slipped, and the loaf beneath it was pressed flat along one side. She pulled the fabric back into place with careful fingers.

“I’m all right,” she said.

He believed her. That was the trouble. He had no reason to hold her any longer, and no reason to reach for her again. He turned to the wheelhouse and checked the marker buoy, making a small correction with the tiller. The Heron answered at once, her bow finding the channel with the familiar ease he had expected from the start.

The crossing continued. Jack kept both hands on the tiller as the water settled and the wind dropped to a steady breeze. He checked the next marker, eased the throttle, and brought the bow back to the centre of the channel. The passengers began talking again, their voices rising as the worst of the gust passed. Behind his shoulder, Sarah shifted the basket from one arm to the other. He knew where she stood without looking. Each time the boat rolled, his attention went to her before he could stop it, and each time he brought it back to the channel.

Sarah worked her arm once at the elbow, testing it without drawing attention to the movement. Jack saw the small wince she tried to hide and nearly asked whether he had hurt her. She looked up before he could speak, and the question died. The wind had forced them together, but it had not decided how long he kept his hand there. He knew the difference. He also knew that an apology would not make the choice disappear.

The pressure of her sleeve lingered in his palm. Jack closed that hand around the wet rail until the cold reached his fingers, then returned it to the tiller. He ran through the next steps in order: check the marker, correct the bow, watch the current near the landing. The tasks steadied his hands, but they did not settle the question of why he had waited so long to release her.

Sarah stood near the wheelhouse with the basket held against her coat. The wind had drained the colour from her face, and damp strands of hair clung to her temple. She glanced once at his hand, now wrapped around the tiller, then looked down at the crushed loaf. She adjusted the cloth over it, careful not to press the broken crust. Jack wanted to explain why he had held her. He had no explanation that would not sound like an excuse, and no right to ask her to make it easier for him.

A passenger called for help with a stuck strap. Jack crossed the deck, bracing one hand on the rail as the Heron rose over a short wave. The leather buckle had twisted under the wet canvas. He turned it sideways, eased the strap through, and told the passenger to keep the case low until they reached the landing. The man thanked him and moved back to his seat. Jack checked the others before returning to the tiller: the woman had her shawl around her shoulders, the fisherman held his bucket between his boots, and Nell had gathered the loose line into a neat coil. Sarah stood near the rail with both arms around the basket, keeping the crushed loaf level. Jack did not offer to take it from her. The ferry’s wake opened behind them, then broke apart in the wind. The marker buoy slid past the stern and vanished in the spray.

At the far landing, Jack brought the Heron alongside the dock at an angle that kept the stern clear of the pilings. Nell caught the bow line and looped it over the cleat; he checked the tension before easing the engine to idle. Passengers stepped ashore in small groups, talking about the sudden gust and the rain. Jack waited until the fisherman had his bucket and the woman had her case before he let the last two passengers cross. Sarah waited until the others had moved past, then approached the gangplank with the basket tucked close against her coat. The crushed loaf had left a dark patch on the cloth. She kept one hand over it as she stepped onto the landing.

Jack stood at the wheelhouse door while Sarah stepped onto the landing. He wanted to ask whether the bread could be saved, whether her arm hurt, whether she had meant the look she gave him. None of those questions was his to ask. He could at least have offered to take the basket, but that would mean stepping toward her again. He stayed by the door and watched her turn toward Lantern Street, the damp cloth gathered carefully around the crushed loaf.

She glanced back once. He did not know whether she meant to. Their eyes met across the gap between dock and boat. He held still, hands at his sides, and let her be the one to turn away.



Jack could not shake the memory of her eyes on his mouth. He had looked away because it was the only choice he trusted himself to make. He had not wanted the wind to stop, not while it gave him a reason to keep her close. That truth sat badly with him. The gust had been real, the danger real, and he had acted as a ferryman should. What followed had belonged to him alone.

He checked the line again, though Nell had secured it. The rope ran taut through the cleat, wet and rough under his palm. He pulled once, then let it lie. There was no fault in the knot. The fault was in the way he had waited after Sarah was steady, in the extra moment he had taken without asking.

Jack checked the line again, though Nell had secured it. The rope lay taut through the cleat, water shining in its fibres. Beyond the dock, the wind had lifted the lake into short dark ridges. He watched a gull cross low over the water, disappear behind a wave, then reappear farther down the channel. Sarah was already halfway up Lantern Street. The basket moved against her side as she walked; she kept one hand over the crushed loaf. Jack looked away only when she reached the bend and the roofs hid her from view. He set both hands on the tiller. The worn wood pressed into his palms, steady and familiar. His right hand flexed once, remembering the place where her forearm had rested. He closed it around the tiller and held on until the next crossing began.
