The morning crossing went wrong in the middle, the way most things on the lake did, with no word of warning.

Jack had taken the Heron out on a clear early-summer light, the water lying flat and green under the bow and the far shore sharp as a cut. He had not looked at Sarah Quill when she came aboard. He had not looked at her at the landing, either, the morning before, with her knuckles still printed on his throat. He had spent a day and a night learning exactly how little he could look at a person and still be civil, and the answer was not at all, and so he kept his eyes on the channel and his hands on the tiller and let the crossing carry itself.

She had said “Good morning” on the plank, and he had said it back, and that had been the whole of their conversation, a plain exchange of two words each, as careful as two people passing on a narrow stair. She went to stand near the rail with a basket in her arms. A loaf for the miller’s wife at the far landing, she had told Nell, wrapped in a cloth and smelling faintly of honey. The smell reached him over the rumble of the engine. It reached him at every turn of the wind. He breathed through his mouth and watched the markers go by.

The wind came off the western shore without a change in the sky first, which was the thing that nagged at him afterward. A dark cloud had been waiting low behind the hills, and he had seen it and had not weighed it. He weighed it now. The surface of the water darkened in a long moving band, and the flags at the landing behind them snapped out all at once, and the Heron put her shoulder into a gust that struck broadside and lifted spray over the rail.

“Sit,” he called to the deck. “Hold the rail, all of you. Sit down.”

Most of them sat. A fisherman swore as his bucket slid the length of the boards. Nell caught it with her boot and slid it back without looking at it, and she was already moving forward along the lashings with that flat, easy balance of hers, one hand always on a rail or a line. He had never been so glad of her. He leaned on the tiller and felt the bow come round against the push of the water, slowly, like a rope hauled hand over hand, and the gap between the Heron’s stern and the shallows narrowed to a number he did not care for.

Then the deck dropped. A swell came under the keel and the whole boat lurched sideways, and he saw Sarah go.

It was not a fall. She caught the rail with her forearm and the rail struck back, and the basket in her arms swung wide, and her boots lost the boards. He did not decide anything. He had one hand on the tiller and one hand free and the free hand went out and closed round her forearm, above the wrist, through the wet wool of her sleeve, and he pulled.

She came upright against him. Her shoulder struck his chest. His left boot braced against the raised lip of the deck and his right hand stayed where it was on the tiller, holding the bow into the wind with a strength he had not known he still had, and in the crook of his other arm was the whole warm weight of her, breathing hard.

“I have you,” he said, and then, because it was too much, “Keep your feet under you.”

“I am,” said Sarah, a little breathless. She was not. She was standing on nothing, on his arm, on the angle of the boat, and she knew it. He felt her try to find the deck with her boots and find it, and straighten, and he waited for the moment when he could let go.

The moment came. He knew exactly when. The next roll was a short one and she was steady in it, her feet planted, her free palm flat on the side of his coat for balance. He had only to open his fingers.

He did not open them.

The boat lifted and sank. Spray blew across them. He stood with his chest against her shoulder and her forearm in his grip, and the pulse under his fingers beat hard and fast and a little out of step with the engine. He could feel the muscle in her arm. He could feel every effort she made to stay where she was, and every effort she made not to press against him, and the plain impossibility of both. The basket crushed between her ribs and his, and a smell of warm honey came up out of the cloth, rich and sweet and so close that it was nearly a taste.

His hand locked on her arm. He felt it lock. The knuckles ached where the bones stood up under the skin, and he did not loosen them. He held her the way he held the tiller in a blow, with the whole of himself, and the whole of himself was no longer thinking about the channel.

Sarah looked up.

Her gaze met his and went down, to his mouth. It stayed there. He watched her look at it. She looked at it as a person looks at a door she has been told is locked, with her lips a little parted and the color rising in her face, and he felt his own thoughts stop as cleanly as the engine would have stopped if he had cut the throttle. There was no sound. There was no wind. There was only the small space between her face and his, the breadth of a palm, and the spray on her lashes, and her breath.

Neither of them said let go.

The Heron drifted. He felt it through the soles of his boots: the hull sliding off her line, the bow falling away from the marker, the stern sagging toward the shallows. The helm was dead under his palm. She had stopped answering him, because he had stopped asking, and a man who has held a course for twenty years does not forget the feel of one slipping from him. He knew that he should look away. He could not remember how.

“Jack,” Sarah said, very softly.

A gust slapped the hull and flung spray over both of them, and the cold of it struck him like a bucket of the lake. He wrenched his eyes from Sarah’s face to the channel, and the buoy was where it should not have been, wallowing on the wrong quarter, and the bow was ten degrees off and falling. He threw his weight on the tiller. His left hand opened. He felt her sleeve slip out of it, and the loss went through him like a blade.

The Heron groaned, and answered.

It took three breaths to find the line. He found it. He brought the bow back across the wind and into the channel with both fists on the wood and his whole back set to it, and the water came alongside smooth and heavy, and the buoy slid by to starboard where it belonged. Behind his shoulder he could hear her breathing, short and quick, slowing by degrees. He did not turn his head. He gave the lake every ounce of his attention and it repaid him with a bland green indifference that he found almost insulting.

“Are you steady?” he asked the water.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He heard her shift the basket. He heard her stop. Then a small, rueful sound, almost a laugh, and she said, “Jack. The bread.”

He looked down. The cloth had come loose and the loaf lay in the basket pressed flat along one side, the crust split across the crown where the two of them had crushed it between their ribs. The honey smell came up thick and warm into the rain, and he knew, without a doubt, that she had put a piece of herself into it, and that it had spilled.

He wanted to apologize for the bread. He wanted to say a great many things. None of them could be said with Nell at the bow and a boat full of passengers listening, and none of them was his to say.

“It will keep,” said Sarah, and drew the cloth over it with careful fingers, and did not look at him. “Bread is hardier than it looks. Most things are.”

He thought of the first loaf she had ever sent across the water, and how he had eaten it on the bench with his eyes shut, and how it had told him the truth about a loneliness he had never named to anyone. He thought that this crushed one was telling it again.

The rain began. It came in short warm squalls, and then the squalls thinned and the gust passed on, and the lake settled back into its green and level self as if nothing had happened. The passengers began to talk. A woman shook spray from her shawl. The fisherman retrieved his bucket and gave Nell a nod of thanks. Two boys compared the size of the waves they claimed to have seen. Jack heard them as he heard the engine, without taking part.

He had released her. His body did not seem to know it. It remembered the shape of her forearm through the wet sleeve, the thin quick pulse, the width of bone, and it would not close properly round the tiller.

He had spent years learning not to mistake silence for permission. A frightened look was not an answer. A glance at a mouth, in a gale, with spray in her eyes, was not an invitation. He told himself that, in order, as he would have run a checklist, and every item on the list was reasonable, and none of it stopped his heart from going on as it was.

Sarah stood near the wheelhouse with both arms round the basket. The wind had drained the color from her face. Damp strands of hair clung to her temple. She glanced once at his fingers on the wood, and then away. She worked her arm at the elbow, testing it, and he saw the small wince she tried to hide.

“Did I hurt you?” he said, before he could stop himself.

“No.” She looked at him then. “You held on exactly as long as I needed.”

It was the kindest lie he had ever been told, and the most dangerous, because he could not tell whether it was a lie.

He thought of telling her how long it had been, by his own count, and how far past steady. He could not make the sum come out in a sentence a decent man could say aloud. So he said nothing, and the Heron went on across the green water with the engine beating under their feet like a second, steadier pulse, and neither of them mentioned that a crossing which should have taken forty minutes had taken fifty.

At the far landing he brought the Heron in at an angle that kept the stern clear of the pilings. Nell caught the bow line and made it fast. The passengers went ashore in twos and threes, talking about the gust. Sarah came last. She waited until the deck was clear, and then she walked to the gangplank with the basket held close against her coat and her palm cupped over the crushed loaf, as if she were carrying a bird.

He stood at the wheelhouse door. He wanted to ask whether the bread could be saved. He wanted to ask whether her arm hurt, and whether she had meant the look she had given him. He did not ask. He could have offered to take the basket, but that would have meant stepping toward her, and he did not trust himself to stop.

She stepped onto the landing. She walked three paces. Then she turned, once, and looked back.

Their eyes met across the gap between the boat and the dock. She did not smile. She did not look away. He stood quite still with his arms at his sides, and let her be the one to turn.

She turned. She went up the landing toward the miller’s house, and the damp cloth lay gathered around the crushed loaf in the crook of her arm.

Nell came up from the bow with the wet line coiled over her shoulder. She glanced up the landing after Sarah, and then at him.

“You all right?”

“Fine.”

Her eyes went to his hand on the wood. She seemed about to say a great deal. She looked at the sky, and at the gull on the piling, and at the knuckles of his right hand, still white. “Want me to bring the engine down?”

“Please.”

She went below without teasing him. That was worse than if she had.

He stood alone on the deck while the engine settled and the hull knocked softly against the piling. The wind had dropped to a breeze. A gull crossed low over the water, vanished behind a short dark wave, and appeared again farther down the channel. At the top of the landing, small and quick in her grey coat, Sarah went round the corner of the miller’s house with her palm still resting over the loaf.

He set both hands on the tiller. The worn wood pressed into his palms. It was cold, and steady, and it had never in its life asked him for anything. His right hand flexed once, and closed, and would not stop remembering what it had held.
