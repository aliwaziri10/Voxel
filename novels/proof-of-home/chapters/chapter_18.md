The bun was still in his coat. Jack knew it the way he knew the weight of the bow line, without looking: a small round pressure against his ribs on the left side, where the flap of the pocket buttoned down over it. He had worn the coat every morning since midsummer. He had not taken the bun out. He had not eaten it, and he had not thrown it into the lake, and the Heron seemed to hold both of those facts against him.

High summer had settled over Larkmere like a lid. The lake lay flat and hot from the first light, scratched by dragonflies and nothing else, and the air over the pier smelled of cut grass and tar and the dust of the market road. Market day. Every farmer for six miles was coming across with eggs in crates, and every weaver with her bolts, and the long queue on the landing had already begun to sweat in its best coats.

He was being careful. It had taken him a fortnight to learn how much work careful was. He said good morning to Sarah Quill at the gangplank in a voice he had borrowed from the harbor clerk. He took the standing loaf from her by the cloth and never by her hand. He kept a plank of dock between his boots and hers, and he named the weather before she could name anything else. He was not unkind. He was a locked gate, painted a pleasant color, and he had done it so well that Nell had stopped teasing him altogether, which was how he knew how well.

Every night the lantern in the bakery window burned a little later than it needed to. He saw it from the pier. He told himself it was a custom.

This morning the loaf came to him in its cloth as always, warm through the wrapping, and he laid it on the shelf in the wheelhouse and did not look at it. Sarah stood a moment on the landing with her market basket on her arm. Her eyes went to his hands, then to his face, and then she looked at the water and said nothing at all, and went to the end of the queue like anyone else.

The passengers came aboard in a long shuffling river of elbows. A farmer with a crate of eggs held to his chest like an infant. A woman with a goose under one arm and the goose’s opinion of the whole arrangement loud in the other. Two boys guarding a crate of peaches that kept trying to roll toward the rail. Jack stood at the wheel and watched them settle onto the benches and the deck, and he did what he always did, which was to notice what they carried. The farmers carried eggs and the sharp worry of a bad sale. The weavers carried finished cloth and the dry pride that came with it. The goose carried nothing but contempt.

The Heron took on their weight and sank by inches. The planks groaned. Nell went down the line counting heads and calling for people to shift along, and the gap on deck closed and closed again until there was no deck left to speak of.

Then Sarah came aboard last, with the queue pressing at her back.

She had nowhere to stand. The benches were full, the rail was full, the crates stood three high along the port side, and the only space left on the whole boat was the narrow strip beside the wheel, between his arm and a stack of egg crates. A man with a sack of turnips shouldered past her. A woman with a basket turned. The crowd closed behind her like a door, and Sarah was shoved sideways, and her shoulder came to rest against his arm.

Jack did not look down.

The Heron cast off. The engine caught under their feet. The lake opened ahead, flat and white with heat, and the crossing would take the better part of an hour, and for the whole of it her shoulder would stay where it was, pressed to his sleeve from the elbow to the point of his arm. There was no room to move. There was no room to breathe in properly. There was only a quarter inch of wool and a layer of thin summer cotton and the live warmth of her, going straight through both.

“It’s hot,” he said.

She said nothing.

“The lake will be like glass by evening.”

Nothing. He kept his eyes on the far shore, on the line of Lantern Street where the bakery stood with its flour-dusted windows, and he kept his hands on the wheel. They were locked there. He could feel the wood biting the pads of his palms, and he let it. It was the only part of him that was telling the truth about how much effort this took, and it was a part nobody could see.

Her shoulder shifted, a small adjustment against the roll of the boat. He felt every grain of it. The muscle at the hinge of his jaw tightened until his teeth ached. The woman with the goose spoke sharply to a neighbor. A child began to count the gulls aloud. Beyond the rail the water slid by, white and slow, and the Heron moved through it as if every foot of the passage had been charged for in advance.

“The wind is down,” he said. “There will be no rain tonight.”

He heard it as she must have heard it. A man reciting a tide table to a woman whose hair had come loose from its pins against his sleeve, whose breath he could count if he let himself, three shallow breaths to every one of his. She smelled of warm flour and cardamom. The scent came up off the collar of her dress, off the nape of her neck, and he fixed his gaze on a buoy marking the channel and read the number painted on it four times over, because the number was easier than anything else.

She did not move away.

That was what he noticed, and he noticed it slowly, the way a man notices that the hull has gone quiet. Past the island the crowd on deck thinned by a pace as the farmers shifted to the starboard rail to watch a heron, the real one, stalk the shallows. There was room now. A handsbreadth, then more. She could have stepped aside. A woman who wanted to be left alone could have taken that space without anyone noticing, and she did not take it. She stayed exactly where she was, pressed to his arm, and gave him the whole length of the lake to say a sentence that was not about rain.

He knew it. He understood the offer as clearly as he understood the depth under the keel.

“It’s a long way across, in this heat,” he said.

It was the worst thing he had said in his life. It sat in the air between them like a stone dropped into a still pool, and the quiet that followed spread outward and outward, and Sarah’s shoulder did not leave his arm, and she did not say a word.

The Heron sulked.

He had seen it before, and it never got easier to watch. When a passenger held a feeling down, the boat took it on itself. The engine ran sullen. The water leaned against the bow as though the lake had grown a second skin. The shore did not come closer for a very long time, and the passengers began to mutter about the delay, and Nell came up the deck with her eyebrows lowered and saw his face and stopped, and went back down again without a word.

Jack held the wheel. A man did not have to look at a thing to know where it was. He knew where her shoulder met his arm, and where her hip stopped a hand’s width short of his, and how often she breathed. He knew the exact weight of the bun in his pocket against the pulse beating beside it. He knew, with the cold clarity of a depth sounding, that if he let go of the wheel and turned his head, the whole careful structure of the last two weeks would come down in an instant, and he did not know what would be left standing.

Gideon’s tin. The heel. The taste of a person on a shore watching a hull grow small.

He kept his eyes on the buoy. It went by. Another came up, and he read that number too.

At the landing the crowd surged. The gangplank went down with a bang and the farmers poured onto the pier with their crates, and Sarah’s shoulder left his arm in an instant. The separation was clean and total, like a knife through a line. Air moved into the place where she had been and it was cool, almost cold, and the sleeve of his coat held the shape of her a moment longer and then did not.

She gathered her basket. She did not look at him. She nodded once, a small stiff thing, as a stranger nods to a stranger in a doorway, and walked up the pier toward Lantern Street with her back very straight, and he watched her go, and he did not call out.

Nell was at the stern coiling a line the long way around, in no hurry. She did not look up.

“You’re an idiot,” she said.

Jack did not deny it.

“I was not going to make a scene in front of a full boat.”

“Nobody asked you to make a scene.” She dropped the coil into the bottom of the boat and straightened. “You could have looked at her. You could have moved your arm an inch. You could have said one thing that was not the weather.”

“The weather is what people talk about on ferries.”

“It is not what she wanted to hear, and you know it.” Nell’s voice was quite level, which was worse than shouting. “She stood against you for an hour with her face like a shut door and you gave her a tide table.”

“There’s no tide on the lake.”

“That’s my point,” said Nell, and went forward to see to the bow line, and did not come back for a long time.

The day lengthened. He took the Heron across eight more times, and on each crossing the lake was flat and white and the passengers were hot and cross, and he talked to no one. When the last load had gone ashore, the sun was low enough to lay the shadows of the pier posts across the water in long black bars. He sat in the wheelhouse with his coat across his knees and did not unbutton the pocket.

Footsteps on the planks. Odalys Pike came down the pier with a cloth-wrapped shape held out in front of her like a bowl she had been told not to spill, and her mouth was set in a line he had never seen on her. She did not say good evening. She stood in the wheelhouse door and held the cloth out to him.

“Take it,” she said.

He took it. It was heavy, far heavier than a loaf should have been, and cold through the cloth in a way that nothing that had come out of that oven that morning should have been. He folded back the corner.

The loaf was dense and grey. The crust had no shine in it, and it had not split along the crown the way a proper loaf split. It looked like a thing that had given up halfway through becoming itself.

“She’s made nine of them since noon,” Odalys said. “The cellar’s full of them. I told her to stop, and she said she could not seem to make a different one.” She folded her arms. “I took this one off her so she would not have to look at it any longer. I could not finish the piece I tried.”

Jack tore off a corner. The crumb was tight and grey and slightly damp. He put it in his mouth.

It was cold. That was the first thing, the cold of the lake in the dead of winter, the cold of a wall that had been warm all afternoon and was now only wall. Beneath it was a flat sourness, like water gone still in a bucket. There was no anger in it. That was what he could not bear. It carried a warmth that had been offered, and refused, and put carefully away, and the person who had put it away was being brave about it, and the bravery was the hardest thing to swallow.

He got the mouthful down. He could not take another.

“I know,” Odalys said. Her voice had lost its edge. “I know. Nobody’s ever finished one of those.” She looked at him a moment longer, and then at the pocket of the coat across his knees, where the bun made a small round shape under the cloth, and her eyebrows rose, and she did not say a word about it, which for Odalys was a mercy. “Eat,” she said, and went back up the pier.

He did not eat anything. He folded the cloth back over the loaf and set it on the shelf in the wheelhouse beside the standing loaf from that morning, the warm one, which he had not touched either.

Out past the pier the lake had gone a dull flat pewter, the last light lying thin across it. The Heron shifted once against her lines, and was still. Across the water, at the top of Lantern Street, a single gold point burned late in a bakery window, and the pewter water took the reflection and held it perfectly, and gave nothing back.
