The lantern at the landing had caught just before dusk. Jack stood at the tiller of the Heron and watched the flame settle behind its glass. He had seen Sarah Quill there earlier. She stood at the same post, her hands full of market bags, her face turned toward the water. They had not spoken. They had only been standing at the same lantern when it caught, and by the old saying that meant they were thinking of the same person.

He did not know if she believed in such sayings. He did not know if the person she was thinking of was him. He only knew that the ferry felt heavier today, as if the water itself had grown reluctant to move.

It was high summer. The lake lay flat and hot, the surface scratched by a few dragonflies and nothing else. The air smelled of cut grass and dust from the market road and the wet wood of the dock. The ferry was packed. Market day brought farmers with eggs in crates, weavers with bolts of cloth, a woman with a goose under her arm, two boys with a crate of peaches that threatened to roll over the gunwales. They jammed onto the Heron until the timbers groaned and Jack had to raise his voice to be heard over the clatter of baskets and the shuffling of feet.

He noticed what people carried. The farmers carried eggs and the sharp fear of a bad sale. The weavers carried cloth and the quiet pride of finished work. The woman with the goose carried the bird’s struggle in her grip. Jack did not say any of this first. He only stood at the tiller and watched them settle onto the benches, the boat settling lower in the water with each new body.

Then Sarah came aboard.

She moved through the crowd like something quiet trying not to be noticed, but she could not help it. She carried a basket of bread from her own shop, wrapped in a cloth, and the scent of it rose in the warm air. She found a spot near the stern, but the crowd pressed in behind her. A man with a sack of turnips shifted his weight. A woman with a market basket turned. The line of people closed up. Sarah was shoved sideways.

Her shoulder landed hard against Jack’s arm.

He was at the tiller. She was standing just beside him, wedged between him and a stack of crates. There was no room to move. The crossing would take the better part of an hour, and for that hour her shoulder would remain pressed to his sleeve.

Jack did not look down.

He talked about the weather.

“It is hot,” he said. “The lake will be like glass by evening.”

She did not answer. Or she answered with something so low he could not hear it over the water lapping at the hull. He kept his eyes on the far shore, on the line of Lantern Street where the bakery stood with its flour-dusted windows. His hands were locked on the tiller, knuckles white, the wood biting into his palms. He did not shift his weight. He did not ask her to move. He did not say her name.

The effort of staying still was something he knew well. The ferry was the only place he was allowed to be sure of himself, and here he was, unsure of every inch of space between his body and hers. He noticed what people carried, and he noticed that she carried the weight of a decision she had not spoken aloud. He would not say it first.

Breath on a collar. He could almost feel it, though she was not that close. Her shoulder was hard against his arm, the fabric of her dress thin in the summer heat, and every time the ferry rocked she pressed slightly harder, or he imagined she did. He held his breath. He fixed his eyes on a buoy marking the channel. He said nothing about the way she smelled, or the way her hair had come loose from its knot, or the way she had looked at him that morning at the lantern.

Other people were close and watching. A child stared. An old woman clutched her shawl. The ferry was full of eyes. He could not turn his head. He could not reach out. He could only talk about the weather and let the lake carry them across.

“The wind is down,” he said. “There will be no rain tonight.”

Still she said nothing. Her shoulder remained against his arm, steady, warm, and there.

The Heron sulked.

Jack had seen this before. When someone aboard hid what they felt, the ferry grew slow and sullen. The water resisted the hull. The crossing stretched. The sun moved lower toward the trees on the far shore. The passengers murmured about the delay, but Jack only stood at the tiller and kept his gaze fixed on the far shore, where Sarah’s shop waited, where her ovens were cooling, where she would soon be alone with her bread and her silence.

He did not look down at her.

He did not move his arm.

He remembered Fenna Quill, Sarah’s great-aunt, who had loved a ferryman once. That man had left on the last run of a season, and Fenna had baked for sixty years after him. Jack would not ask Sarah to stay. He believed wanting her to stay was the same as trapping her. He believed that the ferry kept its people, and that the water was the only place he was allowed to be sure of himself.

When they reached the landing, the crowd surged forward. Sarah stepped away from him. The separation was instant and cold. Her shoulder left his arm, and the air between them felt empty, as if something had been withdrawn. She gathered her basket. She nodded to him, a small stiff thing, and walked up the path toward Lantern Street without looking back.

Jack stood at the tiller and watched her go. The warmth of her shoulder was gone, replaced by the chill of the lake wind rising from the water. Cold after warmth. He had held back, and now the cold had settled in his chest.

Nell Mercer was coiling a rope at the stern. Sixteen years old, sharp as a knife, loyal to her uncle and observant of everything that did not concern her. She looked up from the lines.

“You’re an idiot,” she said.

Jack did not deny it.

“I was not going to make a scene in front of a full boat,” he said.

“You didn’t have to make a scene. You could have looked at her. You could have moved your arm an inch. You could have said something other than the weather.”

“The weather is what people talk about on ferries.”

“It is not what she wanted to hear.”

Nell tossed the rope into the bottom of the boat. She saw everything between her uncle and the baker, but she never meddled near the intimate moments. This was not an intimate moment. This was the edge of one, and she was allowed to stand there.

Jack untied the mooring line. The Heron drifted from the dock. He would take the empty ferry back across the lake, and then he would wait for the next load of passengers, and he would not ask Sarah Quill to stay in Larkmere because he believed wanting her to stay was the same as trapping her.

He believed that. He kept believing it as he guided the Heron into the current, the water pulling at the hull, the tiller steady in his locked hands.

The empty crossing was worse. The boat moved without the weight of passengers, rocking in a way that made every wave more noticeable. He was alone with the tiller and the silence. He kept his eyes on the far shore, where the lanterns would soon be lit, where Sarah’s bakery stood behind its closed shutters. He thought about her shoulder against his arm. He thought about the way she had not said a word. He thought about the old saying and the lantern, and how they had both been standing there when the flame caught, and how that meant something he was not allowed to act on.

He tied up at the landing and walked up Lantern Street. The bakery was dark except for one lamp in the back window. He saw Sarah moving inside, her shape bent over the counter. On the shelf near the door sat a loaf he had not seen before. It was dense and grey, the crust dull, the shape wrong. It looked like something that had given up on rising.

He went in. The bell over the door jingled.

Sarah looked up. Her hands were dusted with flour. She did not smile.

She pushed the loaf toward him across the counter. Jack took it. It was heavy in his hands, heavier than it should have been. He broke off a piece. The crumb was tight and grey, the taste flat and sour, the kind of bread that made a person look toward the water and feel the ache of distance. He could not eat it. He set it on the counter and wiped his hands on his trousers.

Sarah did not watch him. She stared at her own hands.

“I was shaping it while you were on the ferry,” she said. “My hands were not calm.”

Jack did not know what to say to that. He knew about bread that carried feeling. He had eaten Fenna Quill’s loaves for years, and he had eaten Sarah’s since she came back to Larkmere. He knew that calm hands made calm bread, and that longing made bread that ached. This loaf carried something stiff and careful, something held back too long. It carried his own silence, reflected back at him.

He left the loaf on the counter. He left the shop. The street was quiet. The harbor lanterns were lit, Gideon Teague making his rounds, each flame catching in its turn. Jack stood at the landing and watched the water. The Heron was moored nearby, rocking gently, empty now, sure of itself in a way he was not.

He thought of Sarah. He knew she was thinking of him. They had stood at the same lantern when it caught. The old saying held. The water was dark. The cold had not left his chest. He turned and walked away from the shore, the grey loaf sitting on the counter behind him, uneaten, the night pressing close around him like a crowd he could not escape.
