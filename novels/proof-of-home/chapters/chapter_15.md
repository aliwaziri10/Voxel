Sarah pressed her knuckles into the dough and felt it resist, then give. Early summer kept the bakery warm long after sunrise, and the room smelled of toasted oats and the sweet edge of the starter crock.

Fenna’s recipe journal lay open at her elbow. Its margins were crowded with small marks, a language Sarah had been learning since spring one word at a time: a ring with a tick through it for rest the dough, a crooked cross for salt, a slanted stroke for ask the starter before you feed it. She had promised herself a season, and she had begun to count it in loaves. Honey oat for the inn. Sourdough for the grocer. Cinnamon swirl for the children who came by after school with their pennies held out like small hot coals. She had not decided whether she would stay when the weather turned.

She shaped a round, tucking the edges under and smoothing the top with the heel of her hand. The dough was cool and alive. She slid it into the oven and the heat rushed up to meet her face, and she wiped her brow with her forearm and knew she had left a streak of flour there.

The bell over the door had not rung all morning. In the quiet she could hear gulls, and the ferry grumbling across the lake, and her own thoughts, which would not stay on the work. They kept returning to the Heron. The wind. Jack’s hand closing round her forearm, holding her upright long after the deck had steadied, his shoulder warm against hers, and neither of them finding the first word to end it. She had not seen him since. The loaf she had been carrying that day sat wrapped on the high shelf, crushed flat as a hat. She could not eat it and she could not throw it out.

The back door opened.

Jack came through it with a flour sack on each shoulder and his sleeves pushed to the elbow. He smelled of soap and wet wool. He ducked under the lintel, found the narrow way between the brick oven and the tall flour racks, and went down it sideways, as a man goes along a crowded pew, so that nothing would catch on the brick.

“Last two from the mill,” he said.

“By the rack, if you can get them through.”

“I can manage.”

She said, because the silence had a shape and she needed it to have a different one, “Did the mill give you trouble?”

“They asked the old price and I gave them the new one.” He did not smile. “It went the way these things go.”

“Then it went well.”

“It went.”

He set the first sack down by the rack with great care, as though it were asleep. She watched the cords stand in his forearm as he lowered it. He straightened, looked at her once, and looked at the floor, and went out for the second.

Sarah pulled on her mitts. The first batch was ready, forty minutes of work gone the color of honey, and it would be spoiled if it sat. She opened the oven door and the heat poured out, thick and sweet, and she slid the peel under it and lifted it high above her head, as she always did in that passage, because there was no other way to carry it where one person could pass and two could not. She turned and started down the narrow way.

Jack was already coming through it again with the second sack.

There was no room to go around him. There never had been. For a moment they both simply stopped, as two people stop at the same doorway. Then the passage drew them together as the neck of a bottle draws what it holds. His sack caught on the rack on one side. Her elbow caught the oven wall on the other. She stepped forward to keep the tray level, and he stepped forward to keep from dropping the sack, and they met with their hips pressed together and neither able to move.

She stood with her arms raised and locked at the elbow, the tray in both mitts above her head, the hot metal overhanging the space where his shoulder was. She could not lower it. If she tilted it, the loaves would slide. She could not step away, because the oven was behind her. Her face was at the level of his collar, a hand’s width from the open neck of his shirt, and she could smell the soap on his skin and under the soap a warmer scent that she could not name and did not try to.

Jack stopped with his back against the flour rack. He did not breathe.

Her hands, in their mitts, wanted to open. That was the whole of her restraint: two thick cloth mitts and the weight of forty minutes’ work. If she let the tray down she would have to find a spot for her hands, and the only spot left in the world was the front of his shirt. So she held the iron, and the iron held her.

She felt his stillness. She felt it in the whole length of him where they touched, the hip against hers gone to stone, the chest held so still that it had stopped lifting at all. He had the sack clamped in the crook of one arm and the other hand shut in a fist gone white at the knuckles. He did not look at her. He looked at the beam above her head, at the wall, at anything, and a muscle moved along his jaw once and was still.

Sarah turned her shoulder. It was the only movement the passage allowed, a small turn to the side that brought her cheek nearly to the wool at his throat. A bead of sweat started between her shoulder blades and ran down, and she could not wipe it away. The edge of the tray bit into the heel of each mitt. She waited. She did not know what she waited for. She fixed her eyes on a crack in the plaster beside his collar, where old flour had gathered in a pale line, and she counted it, the length of it, the small dark bend in the middle, because if she looked anywhere else she would look at his mouth.

Neither of them said a word.

Through the wool at his chest she could feel a heart going, hard and fast and entirely at odds with the stone of his stillness. His free hand, hanging at his side, opened once and closed on nothing, like a man reaching for a banister that was not there.

The ovens hummed. Beyond the wall a gull cried and was answered. Heat from above fell on the back of her neck and heat from his body came through two layers of cloth, and she thought, quite clearly: no one has stopped us. There is no customer at the counter and no neighbor at the window. Nothing in this room requires me to stand here. I am standing here because I do not want to be the one who moves.

Her arms began to tremble under the weight of the iron.

And the bread knew.

She felt it before she saw it. The loaves on the tray above her gave a small, shuddering lift, all at once, as if every one of them had drawn the same breath. Their crowns swelled and split along the seams in a long crackling run, and a fresh wave of sweetness came down over them both. She had put nothing into that batch but the weight of an ordinary morning. It had taken the rest from the passage. Every loaf above her was telling the room exactly what the two of them were not saying.

Jack shut his eyes.

He breathed out then, slowly, a long unsteady release that moved the hair at her temple. His hip pressed hers a fraction harder and did not ease. She felt the shudder run through him from the shoulders down, and she knew, as surely as she knew the proof of dough under her palm, that he had heard it too.

“The tray,” said Sarah. Her voice came out thin. “It has cooled.”

It had not. It was still too hot to hold. It was only that she had nothing else to say.

“Yes,” said Jack, very low.

He did not look at her. He opened his eyes, and they were dark and steady, and he pressed himself into the rack and slid along it until the passage opened between them. The cold came into the space where he had been. He took the sack from the floor, hitched it up, and left the way he had come, without turning his head.

The latch dropped.

Sarah stood in the empty passage with the tray still over her head until her arms shook so badly that she had to lower it onto the cooling rack. She stood a moment with her hip at the edge of the table, where the pressure of his hip had lain along hers, and the memory of it ran down her side like the warmth of a stone left in the sun. She did not rub it away. The loaves sat there high and gold and split down the crowns like mouths about to speak. She pulled off her mitts, and her hands were trembling, and she laid them flat on the cold edge of the table and looked at them.

“I want him,” she said aloud.

It was the first time. The words drifted out into the empty bakery and nothing answered, and nothing needed to. It was true the way a loaf is true, plain and warm and done. She had been shaping it for weeks without once letting it have a name.

The bell over the door rang.

She nearly dropped her mitts. It was the grocer’s boy, with his order slip held out in front of him like a summons. “Mam says the sourdough, please, miss.” He stopped. He was looking at the loaves. “Miss. They have all cracked the same way.”

“It is the heat,” said Sarah.

He looked at her face, which was hot, and at the loaves, which were gold and cracked from end to end, and with the terrible tact of the young he said nothing at all. He took the sourdough and went. The bell rang behind him for a long time.

She thought: the whole town will be able to read it. She stood very still. Then she let out her breath.

She wanted his hands. She wanted the weight of his arm at her back and his breath at her neck and the long steady quiet of him in a room. She wanted him at the end of the day with his collar open and his boots by her door. She wanted him to ask her for the thing she had not decided how to give.

She pressed her thumb into the table until it stung.

She had come north to see whether she could make a life without asking permission for every part of it. The shop gave her work that mattered and a door she could close at night. She had begun to like the sound of her own footsteps in the rooms upstairs, and the weight of Fenna’s key in her pocket, and how the town had begun to say her name. Wanting Jack did not belong in that life yet. She would not shrink herself to fit into another person’s house, and she had no reason to think he would ask her to. That was almost worse. She could not blame him for the choice she was afraid to make.

And there was the other thing, the one in the margins. A kiss given in love sets a loaf for good. Fenna had written it a dozen ways. If Sarah kissed him, her bread would tell the whole town before she had found her own words. She needed the choice to be hers. She meant to come to him whole, on her own feet, and not be carried there by a rack of bread.

She lifted a honey oat from among the rest and tapped its base. The hollow sound said it was done. She cut a slice and tasted it standing at the table. The crumb was open and even, with honey caught in the warm oats, and underneath the sweetness lay a warmth that did not belong to oats at all. It was the warmth of a hand that had not touched her. It was the weight of an arm she had not yet leaned against. It was the moment on the Heron when she had wanted him to stay.

She wrapped the rest in a clean cloth and set it aside for herself. No customer would carry this loaf into the street.

Then she turned to the board and the work, because it was a busy morning and the ovens did not care. The inn wanted forty rolls by noon, the grocer’s boy would be back for the cinnamon swirls, and the starter would not wait on anyone’s heart. A bowl of dough waited under its cloth. She lifted the edge and pressed a floured fingertip into the surface, and the dent filled slowly. It needed more time, not more heat. She covered it again and moved it out of the draft.

Across the shop the crusts ticked and settled. At the mouth of the passage a little flour still hung in the light from the doorway, turning slowly above the floor they had both refused to leave, and on the dark wood of the table lay the white print of her thumb.
