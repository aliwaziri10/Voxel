Sarah pressed her knuckles into the dough. It resisted, then yielded beneath her palms. Early summer kept the bakery warm long after sunrise, and the smell of fenugreek from the previous batch still lingered above the mixing table.

Fenna’s recipe journal lay open beside her. Its margins were full of small marks, a language Sarah was still learning. A dash meant more salt; a curl meant wait; a loop beneath the ingredients meant the dough needed a second rest. Fenna had baked in this shop for decades. Sarah had promised herself a season, and she had counted it in loaves: honey oat for the inn, sourdough for the grocer, cinnamon swirl for the children who stopped after school. She still had not decided whether she would stay when the weather turned.

She shaped a boule, tucking the edges underneath, smoothing the top. Her hands knew the work. The dough was cool and alive. She set it on a floured peel and slid it into the oven. The heat rushed up to meet her face. She wiped her brow with her forearm and left a streak of flour there.

The shop bell had not rung all morning. Lantern Street was quiet, a quiet that let her hear the slap of water against the pilings below, the cry of gulls, the distant rumble of the ferry as it made its crossing. She did not think of Jack when she heard the ferry. She thought of the dough. She thought of the weight of a flour sack in her arms, the way it settled against her hip, the way it asked nothing of her but to be carried.

She had replayed the storm crossing while she kneaded. Jack’s hand had stayed around her forearm after the deck was steady. She remembered the pressure of his fingers, the warmth of his shoulder, and the way neither had found a word to end the moment. She had not seen him since. The memory had followed her through the shop, into sleep, and back to the worktable.



She stood in the passage for a moment after Jack left. The flour sacks were stacked beside the rack, their corners square, the cloth tied tightly at the neck. He had carried them in without being asked twice. He had also stopped with his back against the rack, his breathing held, while she stood pressed between him and the oven with a tray above her head.

The memory made her arms feel heavy again. She set the tray down, though it had been empty for several minutes, and rubbed the marks the hot mitts had left on her palms. She had not meant to trap him in the narrow space. She had not meant to stay there after the tray cooled. She had only needed to get past him, and then had found herself unwilling to move first.

She could have made a joke. She could have asked him to shift. She could have told him the tray was cooling and the loaves needed air. Instead she had stood still, listening to the ovens and his breath, waiting for the moment to end without being the one to end it.

The three loaves had risen too high. The honey oat had split across the crown, the cinnamon swirl had pushed its spiral outward, and the rye had swollen until the scored marks nearly vanished. Sarah touched the side of the first loaf and felt its warmth through the crust. The bread had taken her feeling and made it visible. She could not pretend she had shaped it calmly.

She carried the tray to the cooling rack and made room for the loaves. The kitchen seemed unchanged: bowls stacked beside the sink, flour on the table, the recipe journal open at the same page. Yet the narrow passage no longer looked like a place to carry sacks. It had become the place where she had learned the name of what she wanted, and the knowledge sat in her body as firmly as the warmth in the bread.

She shaped a second loaf, a rye this time, pressing the seeds into the crust. The recipe journal showed a mark beside the rye instructions that looked like a bird in flight. Sarah did not know what it meant yet. She would learn. Fenna had left her the shop and the recipes and the margin language, and Sarah was learning to read it one loaf at a time. Beside the honey oat recipe, another mark showed two circles touching, or perhaps it was a keyhole. She did not know that one either.

The back door opened. Jack came through carrying two flour sacks, one in each hand. He moved carefully through the narrow passage between the ovens and the flour racks, set the sacks down with a soft thud, and wiped his palms on his apron. He looked at Sarah, then at the oven, then at the narrow space between the racks. He smelled of soap and wet wool.

Sarah pulled on her mitts. She needed to get the tray of risen loaves out of the oven and onto the cooling rack before they overbaked. The passage was narrow. One person could pass with difficulty. Two could not pass at all. She turned toward the ovens, the tray in her mind already, the weight familiar, the heat predictable. She opened the oven door. The heat poured out, thick and sweet with yeast. She slid the peel under the tray, lifted it high, and turned back toward the passage. Jack was there, shifting a sack from one hand to the other, his body filling the space between the brick oven and the tall flour racks. There was no room to go around. She squeezed by with the tray held high, her arms raised, her elbows locked, her breath shallow.

Their hips pressed. Her shoulders brushed his chest. The tray was between them, her arms raised, the metal hot through her mitts, the loaves on it warm and heavy. Jack stopped with his back to the flour rack. He did not breathe. She turned sideways, pressing her side to the oven wall, waiting. There was no one else in the passage. Nobody stopped them. They held back anyway.

The ovens hummed. A gull cried beyond the wall. Sarah could feel the movement of Jack’s chest close behind her, though she could not tell whether the quick pulse in her own body was making her imagine it. She fixed her eyes on a crack in the plaster where old flour had gathered in a pale line.

She thought about why she did not move. Nobody stood in the passage to keep them apart. No rule of the bakery forbade it. No customer waited at the counter. The shop was empty except for the ovens and the flour and the two of them. She could have turned her face toward him. She could have lowered the tray and reached for him. She did none of those things. She held still because holding still was the only way to keep from breaking a feeling she did not yet have a name for.

Her arms began to tremble, just a little, from the weight of the tray. The metal was losing its bite against her palms. The loaves were still warm, still rising, still alive. She could feel Jack’s breath held behind her, the tiny earthquake of his ribs not moving. She thought of Fenna’s recipes, of the small marks that said wait and rest and do not rush. She thought of the bread she had baked since coming back to Larkmere, each loaf carrying whatever she felt while shaping it. She had baked calm bread on calm days. She had baked aching bread when she missed her great-aunt. She had never baked bread for a man standing pressed against her in a narrow passage, and she did not know what that bread would taste like.

The fabric of his apron against her shoulder was coarse cotton washed soft by years of use. His hands on the sack had knuckles white where they gripped the cloth. She was aware of the oven heat at her back and the cooler air of the passage at her front. Her breathing stayed shallow and even, in and out, not matching his because he was not breathing at all. She kept her eyes on the crack in the plaster. She did not look down or up, and she did not turn her head. She could feel where his body ended and hers began, or perhaps she could not tell anymore, the boundary softening under the heat of the ovens and the press of his hip.

On the tray, the loaves all rose at once. She watched them swell, the tops cracking slightly, the scent of baking bread flooding the narrow space. It was as if the dough knew what her hands could not say. The honey oat lifted higher than the sourdough, the cinnamon swirl puffed at the edges, the rye stretched its dark skin tight. The bread carried her feeling, whatever it was, and gave it shape. Sarah had baked long enough to know that calm hands made calm bread, that longing made bread that made whoever ate it ache and look toward the water. These loaves were rising fast and high, as if the dough itself could not wait.

Jack shifted. A fraction. The pressure of his hip against hers did not lessen. He exhaled, finally, a slow release of air that stirred her hair. She did not move. She could not move. The tray had cooled enough that she could lower it, but she did not. She kept it high between them, a barrier and a bridge.

“You’re blocking the flour,” she said. Her voice sounded thin in the narrow space.

Jack did not answer. He turned his head just enough to look at her, his eyes dark and steady. Then he stepped back. The passage opened. The sudden space where his body had been felt cold. He took up his sacks and left without looking back.

Sarah set the tray down on the cooling rack. The loaves had risen high and golden. She touched one with a fingertip. It was warm and firm. When he stepped back, the space he left felt cold. Sarah wanted Jack Mercer. The thought arrived without permission and stayed.

She lifted a honey oat loaf from the tray. It was light for its size, the crust crisp under her thumb. She tapped the bottom. It sounded hollow, the way Fenna had taught her to listen for. She set it on a plate and cut a slice. The crumb was open and even, streaked with oats, smelling of honey and heat and a feeling she could not name. She ate a piece. The bread had already spoken. She wiped her hands on her apron and left a smear of butter there.

She went back to Fenna’s book. The mark beside the honey oat recipe showed two circles touching. She traced it with her thumb. She thought about Jack’s hip against hers, about the loaves rising at once, about the silence in the narrow passage. She did not know what the mark meant. She only knew that she had felt it, that her hands had shaped it into the dough, that the bread had risen to meet her.

She measured flour into a bowl. She added water, salt, yeast. She stirred with her fingers, feeling the mixture come together under her touch. The dough was shaggy at first, then smooth. She turned it out onto the floured table and began to knead. She pushed the dough against the table. She folded it over. She turned it and began again. The rhythm was old and familiar. The heat of the ovens rose around her. The loaves on the rack ticked as they cooled. Sweat gathered at her neck. Flour dusted her forearms. She thought about the season. She thought about Jack and the bread that would rise tomorrow and the day after, and whether any of it would say what she could not.

She knew what a kiss given in love would do to her loaves. Every loaf she baked afterward would tell the town, whether she was ready or not. She wanted to choose her own words. Until then, she could keep the feeling in her hands a little longer.

She walked to the front window. The glass was streaked with flour dust. Outside, Lantern Street lay empty in the afternoon light. A few steps down, the harbor stretched toward the water, calm and blue. She saw Gideon Teague on the harbor wall, lighting a lantern. The flame caught, steady and yellow. Whoever stood at the same lantern when it caught was said to be thinking of the same person. Sarah did not look toward the harbor long. She wondered if Jack stood at a lantern on the other side of the water, waiting for the light to find him. She turned back to the ovens.

She shaped another boule, tucking the edges underneath, smoothing the top. She set it on a floured peel and slid it into the oven. The heat rushed up to meet her face. She wiped her brow with her forearm and left a streak of flour there. She left the loaf on the rack and watched its crust settle.
