Sarah pressed her knuckles into the dough. It resisted, then yielded beneath her palms. Early summer kept the bakery warm long after sunrise, and the smell of fenugreek from the previous batch still lingered above the mixing table.

Fenna’s recipe journal lay open beside her. Its margins were full of small marks, a language Sarah was still learning. A dash meant more salt; a curl meant wait; a loop beneath the ingredients meant the dough needed a second rest. Fenna had baked in this shop for decades. Sarah had promised herself a season, and she had counted it in loaves: honey oat for the inn, sourdough for the grocer, cinnamon swirl for the children who stopped after school. She still had not decided whether she would stay when the weather turned.

She shaped a boule, tucking the edges underneath, smoothing the top. Her hands knew the work. The dough was cool and alive. She set it on a floured peel and slid it into the oven. The heat rushed up to meet her face. She wiped her brow with her forearm and left a streak of flour there.

The shop bell had not rung all morning. Lantern Street was quiet, a quiet that let her hear the slap of water against the pilings below, the cry of gulls, the distant rumble of the ferry as it made its crossing. She did not think of Jack when she heard the ferry. She thought of the dough. She thought of the weight of a flour sack in her arms, the way it settled against her hip, the way it asked nothing of her but to be carried.

She had replayed the storm crossing while she kneaded. Jack’s hand had stayed around her forearm after the deck was steady. She remembered the pressure of his fingers, the warmth of his shoulder, and the way neither had found a word to end the moment. She had not seen him since. The memory had followed her through the shop, into sleep, and back to the worktable.

The back door opened. Jack came through carrying two flour sacks, one in each hand. He moved carefully through the narrow passage between the ovens and the flour racks, set the sacks down with a soft thud, and wiped his palms on his apron. He looked at Sarah, then at the oven, then at the narrow space between the racks. He smelled of soap and wet wool.

“Those the last sacks?” Sarah asked.

“Last two from the mill.” Jack shifted his grip and glanced at the narrow passage. “Where do you want them?”

“Beside the rack, if you can get them through.”

He carried them in one at a time, turning sideways at the tightest point so the sack would not catch on the brick. Sarah watched him set the second one down with care, keeping it clear of the cooling loaves. He brushed flour from the front of his apron, then stepped back to give her room. The space he left was barely wide enough for her to pass with the tray, but it was the only way to reach the cooling rack.

Sarah pulled on her mitts. She needed to get the tray of risen loaves out of the oven and onto the cooling rack before they overbaked. The passage was narrow. One person could pass with difficulty. Two could not pass at all. She turned toward the ovens, the tray in her mind already, the weight familiar, the heat predictable. She opened the oven door. The heat poured out, thick and sweet with yeast. She slid the peel under the tray, lifted it high, and turned back toward the passage. Jack was there, shifting a sack from one hand to the other, his body filling the space between the brick oven and the tall flour racks. There was no room to go around. She squeezed by with the tray held high, her arms raised, her elbows locked, her breath shallow.

Their hips pressed. Her shoulders brushed his chest. The tray was between them, her arms raised, the metal hot through her mitts, the loaves on it warm and heavy. Jack stopped with his back to the flour rack. He did not breathe. She turned sideways, pressing her side to the oven wall, waiting. There was no one else in the passage. Nobody stopped them. They held back anyway.

The ovens hummed. A gull cried beyond the wall. She felt Jack’s chest move close behind her; the quick pulse in her own body made it hard to judge what came from him. She fixed her eyes on a crack in the plaster where old flour had gathered in a pale line.

No customer had come in, and no call had interrupted them. Nothing had required her to wait except her reluctance to be the first to move. That was what stayed with her after he left.

Her arms trembled under the tray’s weight. Heat seeped through the mitts, and the loaves had begun to split along their seams. She thought of Fenna’s marks in the recipe journal: wait, rest, do not rush. Since returning to Larkmere, she had baked through grief and homesickness, letting each batch take on whatever she could not say. She had never baked while a man stood pressed against her in a narrow passage, and she had no idea what this batch would carry.

The fabric of his apron against her shoulder was coarse cotton washed soft by years of use. His hands on the sack had knuckles white where they gripped the cloth. She was aware of the oven heat at her back and the cooler air of the passage at her front. Her breathing stayed shallow and even, in and out, not matching his because he was not breathing at all. She kept her eyes on the crack in the plaster. She did not look down or up, and she did not turn her head. She knew where his body ended and hers began, but the heat from the ovens and the pressure of his hip made the boundary hard to ignore.

Jack shifted. A fraction. The pressure of his hip against hers did not lessen. He exhaled, finally, a slow release of air that stirred her hair. She did not move. She could not move. The tray had cooled enough that she could lower it, but she did not. She kept it high between them, a barrier and a bridge.

“You’re blocking the flour,” she said. Her voice sounded thin in the narrow space.

Jack did not answer. He turned his head just enough to look at her, his eyes dark and steady. Then he stepped back. The passage opened. The sudden space where his body had been felt cold. He took up his sacks and left without looking back.

Sarah set the tray down on the cooling rack. The loaves had risen high and golden. She touched one with a fingertip. It was warm and firm. When he stepped back, the space he left felt cold. Sarah wanted Jack Mercer. The thought arrived without permission and stayed.

She had come to Larkmere to see whether she could make a life here without asking permission for every part of it. The shop gave her work that mattered and a door she could close at the end of the day. She had begun to like the sound of her own footsteps in the rooms upstairs, the familiar weight of Fenna’s key in her pocket, the way the town had started to greet her by name. Wanting Jack unsettled that new balance. She did not want to make herself smaller to fit into another person’s life, and she had no reason to think he would ask her to. That was almost worse: she could not blame him for the choice she was afraid to make.

She lifted a honey oat loaf and tapped its base. The hollow sound told her it was done. She cut a slice and tasted it standing beside the rack. The crumb was open and even, with honey caught in the warm oats. She wiped her fingers on her apron and noticed the butter mark only when she reached for Fenna’s journal.

She went back to Fenna’s journal. The mark beside the honey oat recipe showed two circles touching. She traced it with her thumb. She thought about Jack’s hip against hers, about the loaves rising at once, about the silence in the narrow passage. She did not know what the mark meant. She only knew that she had felt it, that her hands had shaped it into the dough, that the bread had risen to meet her. She took a scrap of paper and copied the two circles, leaving a narrow gap between them. Then she drew them again, touching at the edges. The first looked like two people keeping their distance; the second looked like a single mark. Sarah stared at both versions, then folded the scrap and slipped it into the journal without choosing between them.

She measured flour into a bowl. She added water, salt, yeast. She stirred with her fingers, feeling the mixture come together under her touch. The dough was shaggy at first, then smooth. She turned it out onto the floured table and began to knead. She pushed the dough against the table, folded it over, and rotated the mass beneath her palms. The rhythm was old and familiar. The heat of the ovens rose around her. The loaves on the rack ticked as they cooled. Sweat gathered at her neck. Flour dusted her forearms. She thought about the season. She thought about Jack and the bread that would rise tomorrow and the day after, and whether any of it would say what she could not. If she left when the weather turned, she could take Fenna’s journal and her own recipes with her. The shop would become another place she had lived for a while, not a home she had chosen. If she stayed, she would have to let the decision belong to her rather than to the town, or to Jack, or to a loaf that told the truth before she was ready.

A kiss given in love would change her bread. Every loaf afterward might tell the town before she had chosen her own words. She wanted the choice to be hers. Until then, she could keep the truth to herself a little longer.

She wanted Jack to touch her again. The thought came without the softening words she usually put around it. She wanted his hand at her waist, his mouth close enough that she would not have to guess whether he meant to kiss her. The wanting was not the problem. She had lived with wanting before and survived it. The fear was what the bread would do afterward: every loaf carrying the truth beyond her control, into the inn, onto the ferry, through the hands of the people who bought breakfast. She did not want the town to know before she had chosen how to tell Jack herself. She pressed her thumb into the edge of the counter until the pressure stung, then let go. The desire stayed. It did not become smaller because she had named it. She wiped her thumb on her apron and saw the pale flour mark it left. She rubbed at it once, then stopped before the cloth wore thin.

She walked to the front window. The glass was streaked with flour dust. Outside, Lantern Street lay empty in the afternoon light. A few steps down, the harbor stretched toward the water, calm and blue. She saw Gideon Teague on the harbor wall, lighting a lantern. The flame caught, steady and yellow. The old saying was that a person who lit a lantern at dusk carried another person’s thoughts home with them. Sarah did not look toward the harbor long. She wondered if Jack stood at a lantern on the other side of the water, waiting for the light to find him. She turned back to the ovens.

The next bowl waited beneath its cloth, the dough still low against the sides. Sarah lifted the edge and pressed a floured fingertip into the surface. The hollow filled slowly. It needed more time, not a warmer oven or another fold. She covered it again and moved the bowl away from the draft near the back door. The old marks in Fenna’s journal had never been instructions to hurry; they told her when to stop interfering. Sarah left the dough alone and cleaned the flour from the table while it worked at its own pace.

She shaped another boule, tucked the edges underneath, and slid it into the oven. Heat brushed her face. She turned the tray, checked the crust through the glass, and set the timer. The tray’s edge had browned faster than the centre, so she rotated it and wiped flour from the peel before it could scorch. The passage came back to her once, then gave way to the next task waiting on the counter.

She left the loaf on the rack and watched its crust settle. When the first crack opened along the crown, the smell of toasted oats and rosemary filled the shop. Sarah broke off a corner and tasted it standing at the counter. The bread was good; beneath the honey and salt lay a warmth that made her think of Jack’s hand at her waist and the moment she had wanted him to stay. She wrapped the rest in clean cloth and set it aside for herself. No customer would carry this loaf out into the street. She pressed the cloth flat, leaving a white thumbprint on the dark fold beside the warm loaf on the counter.
