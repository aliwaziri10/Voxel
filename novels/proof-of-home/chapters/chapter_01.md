The yeast woke before she did.

Sarah heard it through the floor, a small wet sigh from the crock downstairs where the starter pushed against its cloth. She lay in Fenna's narrow bed under Fenna's quilt and listened to a living thing that had been in this house longer than she had owned a coat. Then the cold found her ankles, and she got up.

The kitchen was dark and smelled of rye and old honey. She did not light the lamp. Her hands already knew the way: the long table, the flour bin, the tin box beneath it where Fenna's recipes lived on index cards gone soft as cloth. There were no words on the cards. Fenna had written in marks, a crosshatch for steam, a spiral for a slow rise, three dots and a dash for wait until the surface skins. Sarah was learning to read them the way a child learns a second language, by ear, by mistake, and now and then by a perfect loaf that made her throat close.

She had fed the starter once since the funeral, and it had risen to meet her with the same patience it had shown Fenna for sixty years. She stood over the crock with her palm against its cool belly. "Good morning," she said. The words came out rusty. It had been a long time since she had said them to a living thing first thing in the day, and the starter answered with a single fat bubble that broke and settled.

Early spring on the lake meant mornings that still carried winter in their bones. Cold crept under the back door and lay along the floor like a cat. She pulled her cardigan closed and began.

Flour, water, starter, salt. She worked them together in the big wooden bowl with her fingers, and the dough came up shaggy and cold and slack, a thing with no opinion yet. She turned it out onto the table. The heel of her hand pushed, her fingers folded, her wrist turned a quarter, and again. For the first minutes the dough fought her, sticking to her knuckles and tearing when she pulled. Then the strands caught hold of each other. It tightened, grew smooth, began to push back. She loved this moment more than she would have admitted to anyone. It was the only conversation she had been able to hold all the way through since she came north.

She did not think about the city. She had decided that on the drive. But the dough did not need her to think. It took what her hands carried the way cloth takes dye.

She had been very good, in the city, at taking up less room. She could sit on the edge of a sofa through a whole evening. She could say I don't mind in a voice so level it fooled her too. That was all she let herself remember, and it was enough. She felt the rest leave through the heels of her hands: the far end of a table with no one at it, a key that fit a door nobody waited behind, a quiet so long it had a taste. The dough accepted every bit. Calm hands made calm bread. Hers had not been calm since the funeral, and she kept kneading anyway, because there was no other way through the dark hours before dawn than to give them to the bread.

She shaped the first loaf of the season into a round boule and scored it with the lame Fenna had carved from cherry wood, its handle worn to the shape of a thumb that was not hers. The blade went through the skin of the dough with a faint singing sound. One long cut, off center. Let the bread choose its own opening, Fenna had told her once, years ago, in this same kitchen, and then she had said nothing else, because that was how Fenna taught: one sentence, then your own mistakes.

While the oven heated, Sarah opened the tin box and turned over the one card she had been able to read from the first day. It showed a small circle with a line through it, the line bowed in the middle like the hull of a boat. The ferry. Fenna had sent the first loaf of every spring across the lake on the Heron for sixty years. Odalys had told her so over the shop counter, the morning after the funeral. "First loaf goes to the ferry. Fenna never said why." Odalys had shrugged with her whole face. "You didn't ask Fenna why."

So Sarah would send it. She told herself it was only a custom to keep while she decided what else to keep. Her hands had not been informed that it was small.

When the oven was hot she went through to the front of the shop. Lantern Street slept behind its curtains. Across the way the Lamplight Inn showed one yellow window upstairs, where Odalys would already be up, reading the morning in her teacup. The cobbles held the night's damp. Sarah drew the heavy bolt, opened the door, and propped it with the cast iron weight shaped like a sheaf of wheat. The street smelled of cold stone and woodsmoke and, very faintly, of the lake.

By the time the loaf came out of the oven the light had come up pale over the roofs. She set the boule on the wooden rack and listened to it crackle as the crust cooled and tightened, a sound like a hundred tiny doors closing. The shop filled with the smell of it. She stood inside that smell with her arms wrapped around her ribs and felt, for one breath, almost entirely like a person who belonged to a place.

The first customer came at seven. A woman in a coat the color of lake stone bought two honey-oat rolls and a slice of walnut rye. She paid in coins warmed in her palm, and she left without a word, though at the door she glanced back at the rack, at the boule, and then did not glance back again. Sarah watched her cross the street to the landing where the Heron waited, its paint peeling at the bow, its lines slack against the pilings.

A man stood at the rail with his collar turned against the wind.

Sarah had seen him before, from a distance, in her first days back: the ferryman, Jack Mercer, in the same canvas jacket every morning, his hands rope-rough, the knuckles mapped with old burns, resting on the tiller as if the tiller were the only thing in the world he trusted to hold still. Odalys had given her his name and then, with unusual restraint, nothing else. He did not turn when the woman boarded. He did not turn when the gangway thumped under her boots.

Sarah wrapped the loaf. Linen the color of cream, the twine wound twice and knotted. The cloth held the heat of her palms through the weave. For a moment she thought of keeping it. She could say it had burned. She could put it on the shelf with the others and sell it by the slice to the next tourist who wandered up the street, and no one in the world would ever know she had lost her nerve.

Then she picked it up and went out the door.

Lantern Street ran downhill to the water, and she walked down it with the bundle against her chest, the way you carry a thing that might wake. The light lay pale as milk in tea across the harbor. Gulls argued over the shallows. Gideon Teague stood on a short ladder against the first lantern post, wiping the glass globe with a rag, his pole propped beside him. He nodded to her and said nothing, and the nod followed her down the cobbles like a hand at her back.

The landing was busier than it had looked from the shop. A van rolled aboard, tires humming on wet planks. A cyclist banged his pedal against the rail and swore under his breath. Passengers clustered with their bags and flasks and sleepy children, and every one of them, it seemed to Sarah, had nothing to do but watch a woman in a flour-dusted apron walk up to the ferryman with a loaf. Nell Mercer coiled a rope at the stern, her braid swinging, her eyes on her uncle's back. Sixteen years old, and she watched him the way you watch a pot about to boil.

Sarah stopped at the foot of the gangway. The wood groaned under her weight. Jack's gaze left the water and came to the cloth in her arms, and then, slowly, to her face.

Up close his face did not change, and she had the feeling it rarely did. A jaw lined by salt and squinting. Eyes the color of the lake at noon. A mouth that looked as though it had stopped expecting to be asked to smile. She had a sentence ready. She had practiced it twice on the starter. All of it left her.

"First of the season," she said.

He came down two planks and stopped one step above her. She held the bundle out in both hands with her thumbs pressed over the knot of twine, and she kept her hands very still, the way you hold a pan that is too hot, because if she let them move they would shake.

He put his hands under the bundle. One at each end, palms up, taking the weight from below, with the whole width of the linen between his skin and hers. The loaf passed from her arms to his the way a sleeping child passes between two people who have done it before. Not one of his fingers met one of hers. He did it so exactly that she knew it was exact, and the knowledge went through her like heat through oven brick, slow and total. She could not have said whether what she felt was gratitude or the opposite of gratitude.

He did not say thank you. He did not say her name, which he might or might not have known. He turned and walked the length of the deck to the bench beside the wheelhouse and sat with the bundle in his lap, head bent over it like a man reading.

Nell had stopped coiling the rope. She stood with it hanging from her fist, looking at Sarah, and for a moment her sharp face was not sharp at all.

"He doesn't take things from people," Nell said, to the rope.

Then she went back to coiling it, hard, and Sarah did not know what she was supposed to have heard.

Jack unwrapped the linen. The crust shattered clean. He broke off a piece the size of his thumb, put it on his tongue, and closed his eyes.

Sarah stayed where she was at the foot of the gangway, her empty hands hanging.

She saw his shoulders drop. Not slump. Drop, as if he had set down a weight he had carried so long he had forgotten it was a weight. He chewed slowly. The wind took the steam from the torn crumb. He ate another piece, then another. His hands were steady, they had always been steady, but she watched his thumb press into the heel of his other palm in a gesture she knew from her own kitchen, the way you test a loaf to see whether it will spring back.

She knew what was in the bread. That was the worst of it. She had not meant to put it there, or not exactly, and she would have given a great deal for the loaf to taste only of rye and honey and an honest crust. Instead it tasted of the far end of a table, and cold tea, and a key in a lock with no one on the other side of the door. It tasted of three years of being very good at taking up less room. And he sat on that bench eating it with his eyes shut, and he knew. She could see that he knew. He did not look at her. He kept his eyes on the bread as if looking up would cost him more than he could spare.

A stranger had noticed her. Not the baker, not Fenna's niece, not the woman who had driven north with a suitcase and a broken lease. The other one, the one she had spent so many months training to be quiet. Him, in a canvas jacket, with a mouthful of bread.

No one had tasted anything of her in a very long time.

The Heron's horn went, one long blast that she felt in her breastbone. The gangway was hauled up on its chains. The ferry pulled away from the landing with its wake opening behind it in a clean V, and Jack did not look up. He ate the last of the crust, crumbs and all, and wiped his fingers on the linen, and folded the cloth into his jacket pocket with a care that did not belong to a man who had not been moved.

She stood on the landing until the ferry was a dark stroke on the silver. The wind smelled of mud and thawing ice. Her hands, empty now, still held the shape of the twine knot, thumbs bent, fingers curled, and she could not make them let it go.

"Good crust," said a voice at her shoulder.

Gideon had come down off his ladder. He stood with the rag in one hand and the lantern pole in the other, the glass globe at its tip catching the pale morning. He was past seventy and looked as though he had been carved from the same wood as the landing.

"Fenna would have nodded," he said.

"She'd have said the crumb was tight."

"She'd have said nothing. She'd have nodded." He looked after the ferry. "The Heron doesn't carry lies, girl. Whatever went over the water in that cloth went over true."

"I didn't put anything in it."

"That's what Fenna always said." His eyes came back to her, mild and not at all mild. "You staying the season?"

The question lay on the cold air between them. Sarah looked down at her apron, at the flour ground into the cotton, at her hands. Behind her the shop door stood propped open on its iron wheat sheaf, and the smell of the bakery drifted out into the street to find her.

"One season," she said. "I promised myself one."

"One season turns into two." He turned toward the lantern posts standing in their row along the harbor wall, unlit against the day. "Ask Fenna. She came for a summer."

He went off up the cobbles, the pole tapping its slow time. Sarah watched him go. Then she walked back up Lantern Street with her arms crossed over her ribs, and the whole way she felt the place on her palms where the cloth had been, warm, as if the heat of the loaf had not gone across the lake with it but had stayed behind to keep her company.

Inside, the dough for the second batch had risen in its bowl. She wrapped the next loaf for the shelf, a plain boule in plain brown paper, and tied the twine twice around it. She looked down. The knot had come out the same. She did not undo it.

At the long table she laid both palms flat on the cool slack dough and held them there, very still, the way she had held them around the cloth. The dough could not know anything. It went warm under her hands anyway.
