The yeast woke before dawn. Sarah stood at the long table in the dark kitchen, her fingers pressing into dough that remembered her great-aunt's hands. The starter bubbled in its crock, a slow conversation between flour and water that had continued for sixty years without pause. She had fed it three days ago, the first feeding after the funeral, and it had answered with the same patient rise.

Fenna's recipes lived in a tin box beneath the flour bin. Not written in words. Small marks in the margins of index cards: a crosshatch for steam, a spiral for slow fermentation, a dot-dash-dot that meant wait until the surface skins. Sarah was learning to read them the way a child learns a second language — by ear, by mistake, by the occasional perfect loaf that made her throat close.

The kitchen smelled of rye and honey and the sharp sour of a starter that had outlived two wars and three ferry captains. Cold air seeped under the back door. Early spring on the lake meant mornings that still carried winter's bones.

She shaped the first loaf of the season with hands that knew the weight of absence. Round boule, scored deep with the lame Fenna had carved from cherry wood. The blade sang through the skin of the dough. A single long cut, off-center. The way Fenna taught her: let the bread choose its own opening.

While the oven heated, she walked the shop front. Lantern Street slept behind curtains. The Lamplight Inn showed a single yellow square upstairs where Odalys would be reading faces in her tea leaves. The cobbles held the night's damp. Sarah unlocked the heavy door and propped it with the cast iron weight shaped like a wheat sheaf.

The first customer arrived at seven. A woman in a wool coat the color of lake stone, buying two honey-oat rolls and a slice of walnut rye. She paid with coins warmed in her palm and left without speaking. Sarah watched her cross to the gangway where the Heron waited, its paint peeled at the bow, its lines slack against the pilings.

Jack Mercer stood at the rail. He wore the same canvas jacket he wore every crossing, collar turned against the wind. His hands — rope-rough, knuckles mapped by old burns — rested on the tiller. He did not turn when the woman boarded. He did not turn when the gangway thumped under her boots.

Sarah wiped flour from her apron. The boule cooled on the wooden rack, its crust crackling as it contracted. She wrapped it in linen the color of cream, tied it with twine. The cloth still held the heat of her palms.

She carried it down Lantern Street. The morning light spread across the water, pale as milk in tea. Gulls argued over something in the shallows. The ferry's engine coughed once, caught, settled into its low rhythm.

At the gangway, she paused. The wood groaned under her weight. Jack's gaze lifted from the water. He looked at the cloth bundle in her arms. His face did not change. It rarely did. A jaw lined by salt and squint. Eyes the color of the lake at noon. A mouth that had forgotten how to smile before she met it.

"First of the season," she said.

He took the loaf. His fingers brushed the linen. She kept her hands very still around the knot of twine. The contact lasted the space of a breath. Warmth passed through the cloth, his heat meeting hers in the crumb. He did not say thank you. He did not say her name. He turned and walked the length of the deck to the wheelhouse bench, and sat with the bundle in his lap.

Sarah stayed on the gangway. The Heron's lines creaked. A car drove aboard, tires humming on wet planks. A bicyclist clipped his pedal against the rail. Nell Mercer coiled a rope at the stern, her braid swinging, her eyes sharp on her uncle's back.

Jack unwrapped the linen. The crust shattered clean. He broke a piece the size of his thumb, placed it on his tongue, and closed his eyes.

From the dock, Sarah saw his shoulders drop. Not slump. Drop. As if something heavy had been set down inside him. He chewed slowly. The wind took the steam from the torn crumb. He ate another piece. Then another. His hands did not tremble. They never trembled. But she saw the way his thumb pressed against the heel of his palm, a baker's gesture, testing the spring of a proofed loaf.

The loneliness in the bread had found its match.

She had not meant to put it there. Not exactly. But the dough had caught her in the dark hours — the hollow place where the city used to be, the lease she had broken without telling the landlord, the boxes she had shipped and the furniture she had abandoned. The relationship that had ended not with a door slamming but with her realizing she had folded herself into a smaller and smaller box until she could not find the lid. Three years of learning to breathe shallow. Three years of laughing at jokes that scraped. The night she packed her car in rain so cold it turned to sleet on the windshield, driving north until the road ended at a lake she had not seen since childhood.

Fenna's shop had smelled of cinnamon and old paper. The key had fit like it had been waiting in her pocket.

The Heron's horn sounded. One long blast that vibrated in the chest. The gangway was hauled up. The ferry pulled away, its wake spreading behind it in a clean V.

Jack did not look at her. He ate the last piece of the boule, crust and all, and wiped his fingers on the linen. The cloth folded itself into his pocket with the precision of a man who had folded charts for twenty years.

Sarah stood on the dock until the ferry was a dark brushstroke on silver water. The wind tasted of mud and ice melt. Her hands, empty now, still held the shape of the twine knot.

A stranger had noticed her. Not the baker. Not Fenna's niece. The woman who had driven north with her foot heavy on the gas and her heart beating in her throat. The woman who had bought a one-way ticket on a ferry she did not know existed, to a town where the lanterns were lit by hand each dusk by a man past seventy who knew every name and every sorrow.

Gideon Teague appeared at the top of the gangway. His lantern pole rested on his shoulder. The glass globe caught the morning light.

"Good crust," he said. "Fenna would have nodded."

Sarah's throat moved. "She would have said the crumb was tight."

"She would have said nothing. She would have nodded." He descended the wooden steps, boots sure on the slick planks. "The Heron carries more than cars, girl. It carries what people won't say. Your bread just told the ferryman something he's been waiting to hear."

"He didn't say a word."

"Didn't need to. The Heron doesn't carry lies. You sent truth across. He ate it." Gideon paused at the bottom step. "You staying for the season?"

The question hung in the salt air. Sarah looked at her flour-dusted apron. The oven cooling behind the shop door. The starter bubbling in its crock. The tin box of marked cards.

"One season," she said. "I promised myself one season."

Gideon nodded. He turned toward the harbor where the lantern posts stood in a row, unlit against the day. "One season becomes two. Two becomes the rest. Ask Fenna. She came for a summer and stayed for the tide."

He walked away, the pole tapping a rhythm on the cobbles. Sarah watched him go, then turned back to the shop. The door swung shut behind her. The bell above it rang once, a bright note in the flour-dusted air.

She went to the starter crock. Fed it equal parts flour and water. Stirred with the wooden spoon Fenna had carved, the handle worn smooth by decades of palms. The mixture resisted, then surrendered, glossy and alive.

The second batch would be sourdough. The third, cinnamon buns for Odalys. The fourth — she did not know the fourth yet. The dough would tell her when the time came.

She washed her hands at the deep sink. The water ran cold, then warm. She watched the flour sluice away, white clouds dissolving into the drain. Her fingers, clean now, flexed against the towel.

The loneliness was still there. It lived in the space between her ribs, a cool pocket of air. But the bread had carried a piece of it across the water. The ferryman had eaten it. And for the first time since she drove into Larkmere, she felt the hollow place shrink, just a fraction, like dough relaxing under a practiced hand.
