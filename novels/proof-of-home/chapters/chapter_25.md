By the first cold week of early autumn Sarah could read almost every one of Fenna’s margin marks, and the few she could not had begun to feel like a person standing very quietly behind her.

She had learned them the way a child learns to knead, by doing them wrong and then less wrong. A small ring with a tick through it meant rest the dough. A crooked cross meant salt, and a slanted stroke meant ask the starter before you feed it. There were hundreds more, crowded into the margins of the stained cards in Fenna’s tight black hand, and she had been working through them since spring at the loft table, by lamplight, with a pencil and a cup of tea going cold. The card about the ferryman was the one she had carried longest. It had traveled from the shelf to her bedside to her apron pocket and back so many times that the corners had gone soft as cloth. The first lines she had read in the spring: the master of the Heron before Jack’s grandfather took the wheel, and a man whose name was not Mercer. She remembered the sigh she had let out at that, and how foolish it had made her feel.

The last line held three marks she had never been able to read. She had cracked two of them in summer. He was a small upright stroke, and went was an upright with a foot. The third was a short curve beneath a long line, like a bowl set on a shelf. She knew that curve. It sat in the margins of the sickroom cards, beside the plain white loaves Fenna had baked for neighbors who could not pay, and Sarah had always taken it to mean gentle.

Gentle did not fit. He went, gentle. It made no sense, and so for months she had let the line go by her unread, as one lets a stranger pass in a lane.

That morning she laid the ferryman’s card beside a sickroom card under the lamp and read them together, with the stove ticking and the window still black. The little bowl turned over in her mind like a loaf coming out of its tin. It did not mean gentle. It meant kind. And kind was not the same word. Gentle was how you handled a thing. Kind was what you told yourself you were being.

She read the line again, whole, moving her lips.

He said it would be selfish to want me to stay, and kind to go before I could ask. He took the last run of the season. I let him call it kind.

Under the line the old pen had added two marks more, small and cramped, pressed hard into the paper as though they had been written afterward, in a worse hour. Keep. And the sign for the caraway jars.

Sarah laid her palm flat over the card, as she had in the spring, and this time it did not tremble. It had gone past that.

She took the lamp down the back stair in her stockings. The passage behind the ovens ran between the brick wall and the flour racks, so narrow that two people could not pass in it without agreeing to, and she had not walked it slowly since the morning she and Jack had stuck fast in it with a hot tray between them and said nothing at all until the tray cooled. She did not look at the spot. At the far end, under the shelf where the caraway lived, was a cupboard whose door swelled in damp weather. She had never emptied it. Opening it had felt too much like reading a stranger’s letters.

It stuck. She put her shoulder to it. Behind two stone jars of caraway seed, on the bottom shelf, lay a long shape in flour sacking tied with butcher’s string.

Fenna’s last loaf was the size of two ordinary loaves, and it had not gone to stone. The sacking was gray with age and the string was stiff, but when Sarah pressed the crust through the cloth it gave. Her scalp prickled. Bread did not keep like that unless it had been kissed. A kiss given in love set a loaf’s feeling for good; every card said so, in one margin or another. She sat down on the cold flagstones with the loaf across her knees and understood that Fenna had bent her head over it in this passage, on the night before the last run, and pressed her mouth to the crust, the way you would kiss a child’s forehead at a door.

She broke off a corner. It parted like new bread, and the crumb inside was soft and white.

It tasted of a quay in the rain. It tasted of a voice saying that is how it must be, and of a woman standing very straight with her hands folded in front of her, because to lift them would have been to ask. Underneath, thin and sour with age, ran a wish: that she had put her hand on his sleeve and said no. It was a goodbye Fenna had wished for the rest of her life that she had refused.

Sarah ate nothing more. She rewrapped the loaf and retied the string with fingers that did not feel like her own, and carried it up the stair and laid it on the loft table beside the card.

The morning bread went out into Larkmere with a dark seam through the crust. She had tried to keep the taste of the passage out of her hands, and she had failed. Customers lingered a moment longer at the counter than they meant to and looked toward the water, and went away quiet. Odalys came at noon and ate one slice by the window and set the rest of it down on the sill.

“Whatever that is,” said Odalys, “you are carrying it wrong.” She had been circling the word birthday since sunrise, like a cat around a dish, and for once she let it go. “Put it down in front of him.”

The last run of the season stood on the harbor clerk’s board in square chalk. Sarah passed the board twice a day, and in the last few weeks it had begun to loom. She still did not know what she would say when its day came. She knew only that she wanted him to hear this first.

She did not go down to the dusk crossing. Since the night on the gray wool he had taken to walking home by Lantern Street, which was not the way home, and she had stopped pretending not to know the hour. At closing she hung the lantern on its hook by the window and waited behind the door with Fenna’s card in her pocket and the sacked loaf in her arms.

His step came up the lane, slowed under the window, and stopped. She opened the door before he could decide to go on.

He had his cap crushed in one fist. He did not ask what was wrong, because he could see that she was troubled, and because he did not ask things first.

“Come in,” said Sarah.

“It is late.”

“I know what hour it is. Come in.”

He looked once back down the lane toward the harbor. Then he stepped over the sill.

The bakery was dark. She had banked the ovens at closing and left the lamp unlit, and she led him through the shop by feel. The only light in the place was the red eye of the coals through the grate, enough to show the edge of the table, the pale shapes of the flour racks, and his face when he turned it toward her. The room smelled of yeast and ash and warm brick. She took him down the passage behind the ovens, where there was no light at all, and stopped at the far end beside the open cupboard, and turned.

The passage was exactly as wide as it had been. It had simply never seemed so narrow. His shoulders nearly met the wall on either side. She had her back against the shelf and the loaf in her arms between them, and she could hear him breathing.

“I read the card,” she said. “The last of it.”

He waited. He was good at that. “Tell me.”

She told him. She said the words in order, plainly, as Fenna had set them down, and the passage was so dark and close that she could hear each one land. When she came to I let him call it kind, his breath went out of him once, and did not come back for a long moment.

“The man before my grandfather,” he said at last.

“Yes.”

“Gideon told me he went on a last run and did not come back. He did not tell me why.” He was looking at the sacking in her arms, not at her. “It was not a storm.”

“It was a sentence. He said it was kind to go, so that she would not have to ask him to stay.”

“He thought he was sparing her.” He said it to the sacking. His voice was dry and very even, the voice of a man reading a figure off a slate. “I know the shape of that. I could have said it to you word for word.”

“I know you could.”

She had brought the loaf for this. She drew back the sacking from its torn corner, and broke off a piece of the crumb, and held it out between her finger and thumb. “Taste it,” she said. “It is hers. She kissed it. I am not telling you to do anything. I only want you to know what it cost, to be the one left standing there.”

He took it from her fingers. His skin was warm and rough and brushed hers for no longer than a heartbeat. He put the crumb in his mouth and closed his eyes.

She watched it reach him. She watched his throat move, and move again, and his hand with the crushed cap in it tighten until the peak bent double. He swallowed a long time. The red light from the passage mouth lay along one cheekbone and the line of his jaw, and she saw the muscle in it jump and hold. A man could be struck very still by a single mouthful of bread, she thought, and wondered how many times in her life she had been on the other side of a counter and not known it.

She had once been very good at standing with her hands folded. She had made a whole quiet art of it, in another city, in another life, and she had put it away when she crossed the water and had not thought to miss it. She was not going to fold them now. She was not going to ask, either. She kept her fingers wound in the edge of her apron and the loaf pressed to her ribs and waited to see what he would do with a thing he had been given.

“I am not going anywhere,” said Jack. His eyes were still shut. He sounded surprised at himself. “I am standing here.”

“Yes,” said Sarah, not quite steadily. “I can see that.”

Then he bent his head and rested his forehead against hers.

She had not known it would be like that. She had braced for a grip, an arm, the whole weight of a coat. She had not braced for this plain meeting of two foreheads, his skin dry and hot and faintly rough, and the bone of his brow settling against hers with a care that was almost a question. He did not touch her anywhere else. His arms hung at his sides. She felt the effort of that in the very air between them, a stiffness in his shoulders like a wire drawn tight, and she lowered the loaf an inch and set it on the shelf at her back without taking her eyes from the dark where his face was.

They breathed.

It was the same air. That was the thing she could not get over. His breath came across her lips, warm and slow, with the faint salt of the bread on it, and she breathed it in, and gave it back, and he breathed that in. It went on and on between them like a loaf proving under a cloth, rising a little with each slow breath and not yet ready. His eyes stayed closed. She could feel his lashes, or thought she could, a faint stir against her cheekbone, and the pulse in his temple going against hers, quick and then slow as he forced it down.

The inch was there. She knew its width exactly, as she knew the width of a finger of rise in a tin. His mouth was the length of a held breath from hers. If she lifted her chin she would close it. If she tilted her face the least degree, her lips would find his, and she would learn what a kiss from him tasted like, and the loaves in her window would tell the whole town by morning. She thought of that, and found that she was not afraid of it. She was afraid of a different thing. She had spent a long time learning how little a woman could ask for, and she did not yet trust her own mouth to ask for this.

Her lips parted. She did not know she had done it until the air touched them.

He kept his eyes closed. She understood why. If he opened them, he would see her mouth, and the long, careful discipline of his hands at his sides would be spent. She understood too that it was his inch to cross, and that she would not hurry him across it, and that this was a form of tenderness she had never had from anyone and had not known to want.

So she waited. She let her fingers stay wound in her apron, and her breath come short, and her heart beat against the cold edge of the shelf at her back, and she let him keep his inch.

In the dark behind the wall a coal settled with a tick.

It might have been an hour. It might have been the length of one loaf’s baking. When he drew back, it was slowly, an inch at a time, as a man draws back from a sleeping child, and the air came in between their faces cool and empty. His eyes opened. She could see them now, black in the red dark, fixed on hers, and she saw what the closing of them had cost.

“Goodnight, Sarah,” he said. His voice was rough.

“Goodnight.”

He went down the passage and out through the back door. The latch clicked behind him. She heard his boots on the cobbles, steady, then slower, then stopped a moment at the corner of the lane, and then going on.

She stayed where she was, with her fingers resting on Fenna’s loaf on the shelf behind her and her forehead still warm where his had been. Behind her, in the banked oven, another coal settled with a small, soft sound, like a door being pulled to.
