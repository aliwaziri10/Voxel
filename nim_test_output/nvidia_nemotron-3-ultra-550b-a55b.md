The workroom held the stove heat like a stitch held tension. Hester sat at the long table with the Blue across her knees, the cables rising in twisted columns from a hem that had not been blocked. The Grey lay folded on the chair beside her, collar to cuff, the chalk dust still caught in the weave where his wrist had pressed. She had not put it away. She had not decided where it belonged.

Gran slept in the loft above the drying racks, her breathing a slow rasp through the floorboards. The racks were empty. The wool had gone into the Blue instead, every skein she had dyed and spun and held back for the winter that was not stopping. The cabling at the yoke was hers. The body was hers. The sleeves were half done, live stitches waiting on waste yarn, and she had no plan for the neck. She had ripped out the warm row three times. The fourth time she had left it. The warmth was set in the wool now, rule one and rule three, and she could not unknit what she had meant.

The door opened. Cold air curled around the frame and died at the stove.

Ansel stood in the doorway in his shirtsleeves. His braces were loose at the shoulders. His shirt collar gaped where the top button had given up. He smiled before he knew why, the expression arriving ahead of the thought, and she saw the moment he saw the Blue. The smile did not vanish. It softened at the edges.

"You have been busy," he said.

She did not answer. She lifted the Blue from her lap and held it out. The cables hung heavy. The live stitches at the cuffs caught the light.

"Try it," she said.

He took it. The wool was warm as a stone with a morning of sun in it. He pulled it over his head and the cables settled across his shoulders and the hem fell to his hips. The sleeves ended at his elbows, raw edges curling. He stood in the half-knit Blue and his hands found the waist and stayed there, fingers spread against the fabric.

She rose from the table. The tape measure lay coiled beside her scissors. She crossed the space between them without deciding to.

His collar had folded wrong under the yoke. She reached up. Her fingers found the edge of the wool and the edge of his shirt and the skin beneath, and for one breath the warmth went through her fingertips into the place behind her ribs where the gauge had gone slack without her permission. Her hands had stopped being polite without her.

She fixed the collar. Her thumb brushed the pulse at his throat. He did not move. His breathing slowed. The Blue held him and he let it in, rule two, for one breath, and then he braced.

"Thank you," he said. His voice was low. "It fits."

She dropped her hands. The warmth stayed on her fingers. She stepped back and picked up the tape measure from the table and held it like a weapon.

"Out," she said. "Gran will wake. The stove needs wood. You have a school."

He looked at her. His eyes were the color of the river when the light leaves it. He did not laugh. He did not make a joke. He picked up the Grey from the chair and held it against his chest over the Blue, the two woolens layered, the warm and the cold.

"Right," he said. "Wood. School. Going."

He went. The door shut behind him. The Blue was still warm in her hands.
